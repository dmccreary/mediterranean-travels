    About This Guide¶

    Welcome, Fellow Traveler

    I'm Glaux, a little owl from Athens, and I'll be walking the route with you from the Bosphorus to the Acropolis. The monuments will wait for us; they have waited for thousands of years. The people won't, and that includes the ones who lived here long ago. Look closer.

    Mediterranean Travels is a day-by-day guide for a thirteen-day journey from Istanbul to Athens in November 2026: three days on our own in Istanbul, a seven-night cruise past Troy, Ephesus, Rhodes, and Crete, and three days in Athens with a trip to Delphi. It covers the history of civilization, culture, and food along the way. Underneath all of that, it has one larger purpose: to use travel as practice in empathy.

    Why This Guide: Travel as Empathy Practice¶

    It is easy to visit Hagia Sophia, Ephesus, or the Acropolis, take a photograph, and move on. Travel offers something rarer than a photograph: a daily chance to stand where other people stand and ask what the world looks like from there. Empathy is the ability to understand and share another person's feelings and point of view. Like any skill, it improves with practice, and a trip like this one puts us in front of people who are different from us in a new way every day.

    Empathy is easy when the other person is like us. When someone shares our language, our schooling, our assumptions about how the world works, and our sense of what is normal, imagining their point of view takes almost no effort, because it is mostly our own point of view. Psychologists have found that empathy fails most often across group lines, toward people we see as different from ourselves.1 The real exercise starts when the other person does not share what we know.

    Three Pairs of Shoes¶

    This guide asks us to step into three kinds of shoes, each harder than the last.

    Whose shoes What we share What we don't How we close the gap
    A fellow traveler on the ship Language, schooling, a modern worldview, the same trip Very little Almost no effort needed
    A spice merchant in Istanbul today The modern world: phones, prices, weather, family Language, faith, livelihood, the city's long memory Ask, listen, and watch
    A citizen of Athens in 430 BCE Being human: fear, hope, love, grief, pride in a home Almost every explanation of how the world works Read the sources and imagine with care

    The merchant. At the Spice Bazaar in Istanbul, we bought saffron and Turkish delight from merchants who sell to thousands of visitors a week. From our side of the counter, the merchant's offer of a taste and a glass of tea can look like a sales tactic. From the merchant's side, it can be all of these at once: hospitality that is a point of honor, a living for a family, and a shop that may have been in the family for generations, in a building that has stood since the 1660s. November is the quiet season, so every customer counts, and so does the tea. The gap between us is real, but it can be crossed. The merchant lives in the same century we do, worries about the same rent and weather, and is standing right in front of us. We can ask.

    The ancient Athenian. Now picture a potter in Athens in the summer of 430 BCE, the second year of the war with Sparta. The city is packed with refugees from the countryside, and a plague is killing people faster than they can be buried. The potter has never heard of germs. As far as anyone knows, disease can be the anger of a god. So he does what any sensible person in his world would do: he prays, makes offerings at the temples, and listens for what the oracle at Delphi has said. The historian Thucydides, who caught the plague and survived it, records that the prayers and oracles did no good, and that people finally gave them up in despair.2

    Stepping into these shoes is much harder than stepping into the merchant's. We can't ask the potter anything. Worse, the tools we would use to make sense of his choices, such as germ theory, the scientific method, and the habit of testing a claim against evidence, are exactly what he did not have. Our first instinct is to feel superior: how could people believe Zeus threw lightning, or that Athena guarded their city? That instinct is the trap historians call presentism, judging the past by the knowledge and values of the present. The historian Sam Wineburg argues that understanding people in the past is an "unnatural act," because our minds keep turning them into versions of ourselves.3

    Not Less Intelligent, Differently Equipped

    Notice that the Athenians were not less intelligent than we are; they lived with a different set of explanations. If we had grown up where the gods were the best account anyone had of storms, harvests, and plague, most of us would have prayed to Athena too.

    Why Ancient Athens Is the Hardest Case¶

    Athens makes the exercise especially sharp because it holds both worlds at once. It is the city where Socrates taught people to question everything, where democracy was invented, and where philosophers argued about whether the gods behaved as the stories said. It is also the city that, in 399 BCE, put Socrates on trial for not recognizing the gods the city recognized, and voted to put him to death. In 413 BCE, an Athenian army in Sicily was ready to retreat when an eclipse of the moon darkened the sky. The general Nicias, following the advice of his seers, refused to move for twenty-seven days, and the army was destroyed.4

    From the present, both stories look like failures of reason. Historical empathy asks for something harder. Educators describe it as three moves made together: understanding the context of the time, taking the perspective of the people in it, and making an honest emotional connection to what they faced.5 Applied to Nicias, the three moves sound like this. An eclipse was a terrifying sign from the gods in a world without astronomy that anyone on that beach could use. His soldiers, whose courage he depended on, believed it too. And a general who led them into a fight against the gods' warning, and lost, would be blamed for both. None of this makes his choice right. It makes it understandable, and that is the goal.

    The guide practices this kind of understanding without pretending that belief in the gods was simple ignorance. For most Greeks, the gods were not a theory to be tested. They were part of being a citizen, the way a flag, a holiday, and a national anthem are part of belonging today. Paul understood this when he stood on the Areopagus and began his speech by praising the Athenians as "very religious" and pointing to their altar "to an unknown god." He met them where they were before asking them to go anywhere new.

    Taking a Perspective Versus Getting One¶

    One more finding shapes how this guide works. In a long series of experiments, the psychologists Tal Eyal, Mary Steffel, and Nicholas Epley found that simply trying to imagine another person's point of view did not make people more accurate about what that person thought or felt. What worked was getting the perspective, by asking the person directly and listening to the answer.6

    For the people we meet on this trip, that means asking: a waiter about the olive harvest, a guide about growing up in Selçuk, a merchant about how long the shop has been in the family. For the people of the past, it means reading what they wrote themselves, such as Herodotus, Thucydides, Sophocles, and the inscriptions they cut into stone, rather than relying only on what later writers said about them.

    Ask Someone Who Lives Here

    When a moment in a market or a museum puzzles you, swap "Why would anyone do that?" for "What would I need to believe for that to make sense?" Then ask someone who lives here, or find the oldest source that tells the story in the people's own words.

    Where We Practice on the Route¶

    Each stop offers a different pair of shoes. Here are some of the people this guide asks us to understand from the inside:

    • A Grand Bazaar shopkeeper, closed on Sunday and open late on a quiet November weekday, in Chapter 6: Bazaars, Scripts, and Presses.
    • A worshipper at Friday prayer, for whom the mosque we tour is a place of worship first and a monument second, in Chapter 5: Ottoman Istanbul.
    • Families uprooted by the 1923 population exchange, Greek Orthodox and Muslim alike, in Chapter 7 and the Who Was Exchanged in 1923? MicroSim.
    • Soldiers on both sides at Gallipoli, in Chapter 8: Troy and Gallipoli and the story The Soldier Who Carried His Enemy.
    • Demetrius the silversmith, who led a riot against Paul because his livelihood and his goddess were both at stake, in Chapter 11 and the story Great Is Artemis!.
    • A citizen of Athens at the Assembly and in the jury that tried Socrates, in Chapter 16 and the Who Could Vote in Athens? MicroSim.
    • A farmer climbing to Delphi to ask Apollo's priestess what to do, in Chapter 17: The Oracle and the A Day at the Oracle MicroSim.

    Chapter 2: Traveling Well sets out the habits behind all of this: the thoughtful traveler's six principles, and the difference between cognitive empathy (understanding what someone thinks) and emotional empathy (feeling something of what they feel).

    Why This Matters After the Trip¶

    The same skill matters at home, especially in teaching. A teacher who knows a subject well finds it hard to remember what it was like not to know it, just as we find it hard to imagine not knowing about germs. Good teachers, and good AI tutors, start from what the learner already believes and build from there, the way Paul did on the Areopagus. That idea runs through the guide's Learning for All thread and connects it to our companion book, AI-Native Schools, about bringing a good education to every child on Earth.

    How to Use This Guide¶

    This guide is built to be read one chapter a day, at breakfast or the night before, from November 11 to November 23, 2026. It includes:

    • 21 chapters, one for each day of the trip plus orientation chapters before we leave, two deep dives (Paul at Ephesus, and The Oracle and the Algorithm), and a closing chapter for the trip home
    • 68 interactive MicroSims: maps, timelines, sorting games, and simulations that run in the browser, from the Viking Cruise Map to Crack Linear B Like Ventris
    • 22 illustrated stories in graphic-novel style, starting with Glaux and the Gods Who Moved In, which introduces the Greek gods before we meet them on the route
    • A Glossary of 666 terms, places, people, and foods, with pronounce buttons for the hard-to-say names
    • An FAQ with 94 questions and answers
    • A Learning Graph of 598 connected concepts, showing which ideas each chapter builds on
    • Search (top right) for any person, place, or dish

    Read Chapter 1 and Chapter 2 before departure. They set out the big timeline of the region, a few words of Turkish and Greek, and the habits of a thoughtful traveler. And keep a journal: a few lines each evening about whose shoes we stood in that day is worth more than a hundred photographs.

    Cruise port times come from cruise-agency listings and can change. Confirm them with Viking, and check opening hours locally, since November is off-season.

    Color Theme: Terracotta and Olive¶

    The site's colors come from the places on the trip: roof tiles, amphorae, and olive groves. Terracotta, the color of fired clay, runs through the header and the links. Olive green, the color of the groves along the Aegean coast and of Glaux the owl's satchel, highlights a link when you point at it. Together they make a warm, earthy palette that belongs to both Turkey and Greece.
