    Quiz: Paul at Ephesus, Part 1: The Apostle's Life, Letters, and Ephesian Years¶

    Test your understanding of Paul's world, his letters, the Book of Acts, and his years in Ephesus with these review questions.

    1. Which list gives the seven letters that nearly all scholars agree Paul wrote himself?¶

    1. Romans, Ephesians, Colossians, Galatians, Philippians, Hebrews, and Titus
    2. Romans, 1 Corinthians, 2 Corinthians, Galatians, Philippians, 1 Thessalonians, and Philemon
    3. Romans, 1 Corinthians, Ephesians, 1 Timothy, 2 Timothy, Titus, and Philemon
    4. Galatians, Ephesians, Colossians, 1 Thessalonians, 2 Thessalonians, Hebrews, and Romans
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is B. The seven undisputed letters are Romans, 1 and 2 Corinthians, Galatians, Philippians, 1 Thessalonians, and Philemon. They share a vocabulary, a style full of rhetorical questions, the same core ideas, and a situation that fits a traveling apostle of the 50s CE. The other options mix in disputed letters, such as Ephesians, Colossians, 2 Thessalonians, and the Pastoral letters, or Hebrews, which does not name its author and which scholars of all traditions agree is not Paul's.

    Concept Tested: Seven Undisputed Letters

    See: The Seven Undisputed Letters

    2. When the Book of Acts and Paul's own letters disagree, why do historians usually give the letters priority?¶

    1. Acts was written in Latin and translated badly into Greek
    2. Acts contains no accurate local details, so none of it can be trusted
    3. Paul's letters were checked and approved by the apostles in Jerusalem
    4. The letters are Paul's own words from the 50s CE, while Acts came about a generation later and has its own purposes
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is D. Acts, by the author of Luke's Gospel, dates to about 80–90 CE, a generation after Paul's letters. It aims to show the message moving from Jerusalem to Rome and the movement as respectable before Roman officials, and it sometimes smooths over conflicts Paul describes sharply. Option B is wrong: where Acts can be checked, its local details are often right, such as the proconsul Gallio, whose term an inscription found at Delphi dates to about 51–52 CE. So historians read it with care and prefer Paul's words.

    Concept Tested: Book of Acts

    See: The Book of Acts

    3. A guide in Ephesus tells you that Paul taught daily in the hall of Tyrannus for two years. Using the chapter's four kinds of statement about Paul, which kind is this?¶

    1. Something only Acts says, since Paul's letters do not mention the hall
    2. Something Paul's own letters say, in his own voice
    3. A later tradition added by guides and pilgrims centuries afterward
    4. A scholars' inference drawn from both Acts and the letters together
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is A. The chapter sorts every claim into four kinds: what Paul's letters say, what only Acts says, what scholars infer from both, and what later tradition adds. The hall of Tyrannus comes from Acts, which says Paul moved his teaching there from the synagogue and taught daily for two years. No building has been identified as the hall, and the "fifth to tenth hour" detail appears only in one early manuscript, the Codex Bezae. Paul's letters speak of Ephesus but never of this hall.

    Concept Tested: Hall of Tyrannus

    See: The Hall of Tyrannus

    4. In every other undisputed letter, Paul follows his greeting with a thanksgiving. In Galatians he writes instead, "I am astonished that you are so quickly deserting." What can you infer from this?¶

    1. Galatians was written by a follower who did not know Paul's usual letter form
    2. The thanksgiving section was lost when the letter was copied
    3. Paul broke the expected letter form on purpose, signaling anger and urgency that first listeners would feel at once
    4. Paul wrote Galatians before he had learned the Greek conventions for letters
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is C. A first-century listener knew how letters were supposed to go, so a missing thanksgiving would be felt at once, the way we would notice a letter that began without "Dear." Paul also calls them "foolish Galatians" and takes the pen to write in large letters at the end. Form, tone, and handwriting all say the letter is urgent: other teachers were telling his non-Jewish converts to be circumcised and keep the law. Galatians is undisputed, which rules out option A.

    Concept Tested: Letter to the Galatians

    See: The Letter to the Galatians

    5. Acts claims that during Paul's Ephesian years "all the residents of Asia" heard the message. How could this be roughly true even though Paul stayed in Ephesus?¶

    1. Paul walked to every city in the province during his two to three years there
    2. The Roman governor ordered all the province's cities to send delegates to hear Paul
    3. Pilgrims to the Temple of Artemis were required to attend Paul's lectures
    4. From the capital and port, co-workers went out along the roads, and visitors came in on business, so churches grew across the province
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is D. Ephesus was the provincial capital, its biggest port, and a road hub, and the Temple of Artemis drew pilgrims. Paul's letters show how the message spread without him: Epaphras, a native of Colossae, founded communities in the Lycus valley about 170 km inland; Philemon of Colossae probably met Paul in Ephesus; and Paul sends greetings from "the churches of Asia." A single base with co-workers and visitors could reach a whole province in two or three years. No source says Paul traveled the whole province.

    Concept Tested: Paul in Ephesus

    See: Paul in Ephesus

    6. Acts says three times that Paul was a Roman citizen, but his own letters never mention it and say he was "beaten with rods" three times. How should you describe his citizenship to a fellow traveler?¶

    1. "Paul was definitely a Roman citizen, because Acts is our only narrative of his life."
    2. "Acts says Paul was a citizen; his letters don't, and the question can't be settled from the evidence."
    3. "Paul was definitely not a citizen, because citizens were never beaten by officials."
    4. "Paul bought his citizenship from a tribune in Jerusalem for a large sum."
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is B. Being beaten with rods was a Roman punishment a citizen was supposed to be spared, so some historians doubt the citizenship. Others reply that provincial officials did not always respect the law, or that Paul chose not to claim his rights. Because the evidence cannot settle it, the guide itself says "Acts says Paul was a citizen." Option D confuses Paul with the tribune Claudius Lysias, who in Acts says he bought his citizenship, while Paul says he was born a citizen.

    Concept Tested: Roman Citizenship

    See: Roman Citizenship

    7. Paul's own letters describe his call in a few words, while Acts tells the road-to-Damascus scene three times with shifting details. What can a historian say with confidence?¶

    1. Paul fell from his horse when a bright light struck him, exactly as painters show
    2. Nothing at all, because the accounts disagree
    3. Paul believed the risen Jesus had appeared to him, and it changed his life; the scene's details come from Acts' retellings
    4. Paul was blinded for three years and went to Jerusalem only after his sight returned
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is C. Paul's letters say only that God "was pleased to reveal his Son to me" and that Jesus "appeared also to me," with no light, fall, or blindness. Acts' three tellings share a core, a light, a voice, and a commission, but shift details to suit each audience, as ancient historians did. So a historian can say Paul believed he had seen the risen Jesus and that it changed his life. The horse in option A comes from neither source; it entered the story through painters such as Caravaggio.

    See: The Road to Damascus

    8. Who were the "God-fearers" that Acts mentions?¶

    1. Gentiles drawn to Judaism's one God and its moral teaching who did not become Jews
    2. A strict group of Pharisees who refused to eat with Gentiles
    3. Priests of Artemis who protected the temple's treasury at Ephesus
    4. Paul's opponents in Galatia who demanded that converts be circumcised
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is A. Gentiles are people who are not Jewish, and in Ephesus they were the great majority. Some were drawn to Judaism's single God and its moral teaching without becoming Jews, and Acts calls them God-fearers. Inscriptions near our route show the term was real: a donor list at Aphrodisias names donors called theosebeis, "God-worshippers," separately from Jewish donors, and a theater seat at Miletus is usually read as reserved for Jews "who are also God-fearers."

    See: Gentiles

    9. Many scholars think the Pastoral letters (1 Timothy, 2 Timothy, and Titus) were written after Paul's death. Which evidence gives the strongest case for that view?¶

    1. The letters are addressed to individuals rather than to whole communities
    2. They use many words found nowhere else in Paul and describe settled church offices that fit the next generation
    3. Churches today read them less often than the seven undisputed letters
    4. They mention Timothy and Titus, who could not have known Paul personally
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is B. The case is strongest for the Pastorals because several independent lines of evidence agree: a large share of vocabulary found nowhere else in Paul, a church with settled offices of overseers, elders, and deacons with written qualifications, and "the faith" as a fixed body of teaching to guard. Option A is weak, since Philemon is also personal and undisputed. Option D is false: Timothy and Titus were Paul's co-workers who carried his letters. Use in churches (option C) says nothing about authorship.

    See: The Disputed Pauline Letters

    10. Koine Greek let Paul's letters travel without translators. How does the chapter compare that with what AI translation offers learners today?¶

    1. AI translation works exactly like Koine, by persuading everyone to learn one common language
    2. AI translation removes every risk, because no language can be crowded out once machines translate
    3. Koine spread ideas more widely than AI translation ever could, since machines cannot translate meaning
    4. Learners can keep their own language and still reach ideas in any other, though large languages may crowd out small ones
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is D. Alexander's conquests made Koine the common tongue, so Paul could write in one language to Galatians, Corinthians, and Romans. Instead of everyone learning one common language, AI translation lets each learner keep their own and still reach ideas written in any other, a real lowering of the cost of learning. It carries a familiar risk: a few large languages may crowd out small ones, as Koine slowly crowded out Anatolia's old languages. That rules out options A and B.
