    Quiz: Day 6: Troy and Gallipoli¶

    Test your understanding of Troy's layers and legends, Homer's oral tradition, and the memory of Gallipoli with these review questions.

    1. You stand at Schliemann's great north-south trench at Troy, reading its walls from the top down. How should you date the trench itself?¶

    1. By the oldest layer it reaches, Troy I, around 3000 BCE
    2. By the layer at its midpoint, since a trench averages the layers it crosses
    3. As younger than every layer it cuts through, which fits its known date in the early 1870s
    4. By the Roman marble fragments at the top, around the time of Roman Ilium
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is C. Two rules read Troy's layers. The law of superposition says that in undisturbed ground a layer lies above anything older. The second rule says people dig, so a trench, pit, or foundation must be dated by the youngest layer it cuts, not the oldest material it exposes. The great trench cuts through every city, so it is younger than all of them, and we know its date: Schliemann dug it in the early 1870s. Option A makes exactly the mistake the second rule prevents.

    2. Troy VI and Troy VIIa are both candidates for Homer's city. What evidence suggests that Troy VIIa, rather than Troy VI, ended in violence?¶

    1. Troy VIIa has the thickest walls and the largest houses of any layer on the mound
    2. Troy VIIa burned around 1180 BCE, with arrowheads and human remains in its ruins
    3. Troy VIIa contains a Hittite treaty naming the king who besieged it
    4. Troy VIIa is the layer where Schliemann found Priam's Treasure
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is B. Troy VI, the grandest Troy with its sloping limestone wall, ended around 1300 BCE in damage that looks like an earthquake: collapsed walls but no great fire. The survivors rebuilt with small houses crammed against the walls and storage jars buried in the floors, like a city expecting a siege. That city, Troy VIIa, was destroyed by fire around 1180 BCE, and excavators found arrowheads and human remains. Option A describes Troy VI, and Priam's Treasure came from Troy II.

    3. What is Wilusa?¶

    1. The Hittite capital in central Anatolia, abandoned around 1180 BCE
    2. The Greek name for the Mycenaean kingdom that attacked Troy
    3. A Phoenician trading port that supplied Troy with tin
    4. A kingdom in northwest Anatolia named in Hittite texts, which most scholars identify with Troy
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is D. Wilusa is a kingdom named in Hittite texts that most scholars identify with Troy. Homer's Ilios was once Wilios, since early Greek had a w sound that later dropped out. Around 1280 BCE a Hittite king made a treaty with Alaksandu of Wilusa, a name strikingly like Alexandros, Paris's other name. The Mycenaean Greeks appear in the texts as Ahhiyawa (option B), and the Hittite capital was Hattusa (option A).

    4. Troy VIIa, Hattusa, Ugarit, and the Mycenaean palaces all fell between about 1200 and 1150 BCE. Which analysis of the Bronze Age collapse best fits the chapter?¶

    1. The palace systems were tightly connected, so shocks such as drought, raids, and earthquakes in one place spread into a crisis everywhere
    2. A single invasion by the Sea Peoples destroyed every kingdom in one campaign
    3. Iron weapons gave a new people an advantage that no Bronze Age army could match
    4. Each kingdom collapsed for unrelated local reasons, and the shared dates are a coincidence
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is A. The chapter traces a chain: drought cuts Anatolian harvests, the Hittite king depends on grain from Egypt and Ugarit, Ugarit's forces are away helping him when raiders arrive, palaces cannot pay soldiers, the tin trade stops, and earthquakes strike cities short of labor. No single cause explains it (option B), but the dates show the systems were tied together, so the palaces were strong because they were connected and fragile for the same reason. Egypt survived, so it was not universal destruction.

    5. What did Milman Parry and Albert Lord discover about how oral poets like Homer could perform epics of thousands of lines?¶

    1. The singers memorized a fixed text word for word, which their teachers checked against written copies
    2. The singers stored ready-made formulas and typical scenes and recomposed the song each time, fitting phrases to the meter
    3. The singers read from hidden scrolls, since nobody could remember so many lines
    4. The singers improvised entirely new stories at each performance, with no repeated phrases
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is B. Parry noticed that Homer's repeated phrases, like "swift-footed Achilles" or "rosy-fingered Dawn," are chosen by meter more than meaning: each formula fills a different-sized space in a line of dactylic hexameter. In 1933–1935 Parry and Lord recorded singers in Yugoslavia who did not memorize fixed texts but recomposed each song from a stock of formulas and scenes; one singer, who could not read, performed more than 12,000 lines. Option D ignores the formulas, which are the heart of the method.

    6. Where does the fullest version of the Trojan Horse story come from?¶

    1. The Iliad, which ends with the horse being dragged into Troy
    2. A Hittite letter describing a wooden siege engine at Wilusa
    3. Virgil's Aeneid, a Roman poem written around 19 BCE
    4. The inscriptions found in the burned layer of Troy VIIa
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is C. The Trojan Horse does not appear in the Iliad, which ends before Troy falls. The Odyssey mentions it three times, briefly, as a stratagem of Odysseus. The fullest version is Virgil's Aeneid, written around 19 BCE, more than eleven centuries after Troy VIIa burned, where Laocoön warns, "I fear the Greeks, even bearing gifts." Historians have suggested it might remember a siege engine or an earthquake, but there is no evidence for either.

    7. At Anzac Cove you read the "Johnnies and Mehmets" words attributed to Atatürk in 1934. Their earliest known source is a 1953 interview. Using the chapter's rule about the gap between an event and its evidence, how should you classify them as history?¶

    1. A late or unsupported story, because the earliest evidence comes 19 years after the event
    2. Firm history, because the words are carved on official memorials in three countries
    3. A real core with a shaped telling, because the evidence was made within ten years
    4. A proven forgery, because no copy from 1934 has been found
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is A. The rule says evidence made at the time or within about ten years, with no reason to invent, is firm history; evidence more than ten years later makes a story late or unsupported, which may be true but cannot be shown to be. No 1934 record contains the words, and the earliest source is Şükrü Kaya's recollection in 1953, a 19-year gap. Being carved on memorials (option B) reflects cultural memory, not evidence. Missing evidence is not proof of forgery (option D).

    8. Why do Turks, Australians, and Britons remember the same Gallipoli campaign so differently?¶

    1. Each country was taught different facts, and historians still disagree about who won
    2. Only the Turkish memory is accurate, because the battle was fought on Turkish soil
    3. Britain and Australia keep their records secret, so their citizens know little of the campaign
    4. Cultural memory keeps the parts of a shared past that help each community and retells them as its own meaning
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is D. Cultural memory is how a community remembers together, through monuments, anniversaries, and school lessons, and it cares less about what happened than what the past means now. For Turks, Çanakkale geçilmez recalls the homeland's defense and Atatürk's rise; for Australians and New Zealanders, a national coming of age; for Britain, often a failure of command. The facts, such as the Allied withdrawal, are not in dispute (option A). Same hills, four memories, each true to its keepers.

    9. You have a free afternoon in Çanakkale and want your spending to help local families. Which plan fits the chapter's advice?¶

    1. Buy an extra excursion aboard the ship, since the cruise line pays local guides well
    2. Hire a licensed local guide, eat at a family restaurant on the waterfront, and buy from a local ceramic workshop
    3. Stay aboard and shop in the ship's boutique, which stocks Turkish goods
    4. Visit only free sites, so that you put no strain on the town's services
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is B. When a passenger buys an excursion aboard, much of the money goes first to the cruise line and its contracted operator, and the local share is mainly the coach, guide, tickets, and a souvenir stop. Hiring a licensed local guide, eating in a family restaurant, and buying from a local workshop keeps nearly all of it in Çanakkale. The excursion still buys convenience and the certainty of getting back to the ship, so the chapter suggests choosing local spending on purpose when there is a free hour.

    10. The chapter compares conversational AI to a Homeric singer. Which AI tutor design best answers the risks that comparison reveals?¶

    1. A tutor that writes complete answers quickly, so students finish practice problems sooner
    2. A tutor that refuses to answer questions in a learner's own language, so they must read sources
    3. A tutor that gives hints instead of answers, so learners still do the practice, and that points them to fixed sources to check
    4. A tutor that gives the identical answer every time, so it never needs to be checked
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is C. Like a singer, AI recomposes each answer from absorbed patterns, so a fluent answer is not a checked one, and fixed written sources still matter. And a singer became a singer through years of practice; a tool that composes for us can remove that practice. In the Turkish high-school study, students with open chatbot access did worse on later exams, while a hint-giving version removed most of the harm. Option A is exactly the open access that hurt learning.
