    Quiz: Day 1: Istanbul, a City on Two Continents¶

    Test your understanding of Istanbul's water, walls, empires, and everyday customs with these review questions.

    1. Which description best fits the Bosphorus Strait?¶

    1. A wide bay on the Aegean coast that served as Constantinople's main harbor
    2. A canal dug by the Ottomans to link the Golden Horn with the Sea of Marmara
    3. The strait near Troy that connects the Sea of Marmara with the Aegean Sea
    4. A channel about 31 km long linking the Black Sea and the Sea of Marmara, dividing Istanbul between Europe and Asia
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is D. The Bosphorus runs about 31 km from the Black Sea to the Sea of Marmara, between about 700 m and 3.7 km wide, and divides Istanbul between Europe and Asia. Together with the Dardanelles, it is the only sea route between the Black Sea and the world's oceans. Option C describes the Dardanelles, the other strait on our route. The Golden Horn is the city's harbor inlet, and the Bosphorus is a natural strait, not a dug canal.

    2. According to the story Herodotus tells, why were the founders of Chalcedon called "blind"?¶

    1. They had ignored an oracle from Delphi that told them where to settle
    2. They passed up the better site across the water, with its harbor, sea defenses, fish, and control of the strait
    3. They built their city without walls, so it was captured almost at once
    4. They chose a site on the Asian shore, where the farmland was too poor to feed a city
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is B. A Persian general, hearing that Chalcedon was founded seventeen years before Byzantium, said its founders must have been blind to pass up the far better site across the Bosphorus. Byzantium's strengths were the sea's: a harbor, water on three sides, migrating fish, and a toll on passing ships. Chalcedon actually had the better farmland, so option D reverses the facts. The Delphi oracle version (option A) comes from later Roman writers and is almost certainly an embellishment.

    See: Byzantium

    3. What were the Theodosian Decrees?¶

    1. Laws of Theodosius I in 391–392 that banned pagan sacrifice and worship and closed the temples
    2. Orders of Theodosius II in 413 that set the rules for building and guarding the land walls
    3. The founding charter of Constantinople issued by Constantine in 330
    4. A treaty that divided the Roman Empire between the emperor's two sons in 395
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is A. In 391 and 392, Theodosius I issued laws that banned pagan sacrifice, closed the temples of the old gods, and forbade pagan worship even in private homes. Their effects reached sites across our route: the oracle at Delphi fell silent by the end of the 300s. The walls (option B) belong to his grandson Theodosius II, and the 395 split of the empire between his sons was a separate event, not a decree about worship.

    4. Why does the chapter treat the gold solidus as one of the reasons the Byzantine Empire lasted 1,123 years?¶

    1. Its gold paid for the Theodosian Walls in a single year
    2. It was the only coin allowed in the Mediterranean, so rivals had to buy it from Constantinople
    3. Its weight and purity held for about 700 years, so its trustworthiness helped pay soldiers, buy peace, and attract trade
    4. It was minted from the treasure Constantine captured from Persia, giving the empire endless reserves
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is C. Constantine introduced the solidus around 309, with about 4.5 grams of nearly pure gold, and emperors kept its weight and purity essentially unchanged for about seven hundred years. A merchant in Venice, Cairo, or Kyiv would accept it without weighing it, much like a medieval US dollar. That trust was a source of power: it let the empire pay soldiers, buy peace, and attract trade. The chapter makes no claim that the solidus was the only legal coin or that it funded the walls.

    5. The Theodosian Walls held Constantinople's land side for about a thousand years. Which analysis best explains both their success and their two failures?¶

    1. They were taller than any siege tower, and they fell only when the defenders ran out of food
    2. Five layers trapped attackers under fire from two levels and bought time; the 1204 crusaders came by the harbor side, and only cannon broke them in 1453
    3. They were built of stone too hard to dig under, and they failed only after earthquakes weakened them
    4. They were guarded by a huge permanent army, and they fell when Mehmed bribed the commanders
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is B. An attacker met a moat, an outer terrace, the outer wall, an inner terrace, and a higher inner wall, so anyone who climbed the outer wall stood exposed to defenders shooting from two levels. The walls bought time, and invaders usually ran short of food or were struck by disease. No army broke them by force for a thousand years: the 1204 crusaders entered from the Golden Horn, where sea walls were lower, and Mehmed's cannon breached them in 1453. The 447 earthquake was repaired in under two months.

    6. What lesson for AI and education does the chapter draw from Cardinal Bessarion's gift of Greek manuscripts to Venice?¶

    1. Learning is safest when it is kept in one great library behind strong walls
    2. Scholars should move to whichever city is winning the latest war
    3. Manuscripts are more reliable than printed books and should be preferred
    4. Learning that is copied, shared, and spread across many places survives when a single school is lost
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is D. Theodosius II's state-paid school of 425 was protected by walls for a thousand years, but what carried Greek learning through the fall of the city was copies. Bessarion gave about 750 manuscripts to Venice on condition they be open to scholars, and they became the core of the Biblioteca Marciana. The chapter's parallel is resilience: openly licensed educational resources are the modern version of that gift. Option A is the opposite lesson, since a single school behind walls can be lost in one day.

    7. Why did Mehmed II build the fortress of Rumeli Hisarı in 1452, a year before the siege?¶

    1. To close the Bosphorus so no ship could bring grain or help to Constantinople from the Black Sea
    2. To house the giant cannon that would later breach the Theodosian Walls
    3. To guard the Golden Horn against the Venetian fleet anchored inside it
    4. To serve as his palace while he governed the new city after the conquest
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is A. Mehmed built Rumeli Hisarı at the narrowest point of the Bosphorus, facing the older Anadolu Hisarı across the water. With cannon on both shores, the Ottomans could stop any ship coming from the Black Sea, the source of much of the city's grain and of any help from the Genoese colonies. Contemporaries called it Boğazkesen, "strait-cutter" or "throat-cutter." He had closed the strait before firing a shot at the walls. It stands north of the city, not on the Golden Horn.

    8. Where does the name "Istanbul" come from?¶

    1. From the Turkish words for "city of Islam," given by Mehmed II in 1453
    2. From the Latin name of a Roman fort that stood on the Asian shore
    3. From medieval Greek eis tin polin, "to the City," which Turkish speakers heard as one word
    4. From the name of Byzas, the legendary founder of the Greek colony
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is C. Greeks in the countryside said eis tin polin, "into the city" or "to the City," when going to Constantinople, because there was only one City. Turkish speakers heard the phrase as a single word. The Ottomans used it in everyday speech, while official documents usually said Kostantiniyye. Byzas (option D) is the legendary source of "Byzantium," the city's older Greek name. Foreigners switched for good after 1930, when the postal service asked for letters addressed to İstanbul.

    9. Reading a Turkish breakfast table as history, which item belongs to the newest layer, and why?¶

    1. The olives, because olive trees reached Anatolia only with the Ottomans
    2. The yogurt, because Turkic herders brought it west only in the 1900s
    3. The tomatoes, because they arrived from the Americas just before the Republic
    4. The tea, because Turkey became a tea-drinking nation only after plantations at Rize in the 1930s and 1940s
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is D. Food as history reads a dish in layers. Olives and olive oil are the oldest layer, cultivated long before Byzantium was founded. Yogurt and kaymak come from Turkic herders who moved into Anatolia after 1071. Tomatoes and peppers came from the Americas after 1492 and were common by the 1800s. Tea is newest of all: the Ottomans drank coffee, and Turkey became a nation of tea drinkers in the 20th century, after plantations were planted at Rize.

    10. You are paying for lunch in Istanbul by card, and the machine asks whether to charge you in US dollars or Turkish lira. What should you do?¶

    1. Choose Turkish lira, because the dollar conversion offered usually has a worse exchange rate than your own bank
    2. Choose US dollars, because the lira's inflation makes lira prices unreliable
    3. Choose US dollars, because Turkish restaurants prefer foreign currency
    4. Cancel and pay with dollar bills, since cash in dollars is accepted everywhere
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is A. Card machines and ATMs often offer to charge in dollars "for convenience," a service called dynamic currency conversion, and its exchange rate is usually worse than our own bank's. Whenever a screen asks, choose Turkish lira. The lira's inflation (option B) is a reason to check the exchange rate and avoid trusting printed prices, not a reason to pay in dollars. The chapter's rule is to pay in lira, withdrawn from a bank ATM, and to carry small notes for tips.
