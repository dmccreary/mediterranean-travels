    Quiz: Day 2: Byzantine Constantinople and Hagia Sophia¶

    Test your understanding of Byzantine Constantinople, Hagia Sophia, and how to read a city of layers with these review questions.

    1. Procopius praised Justinian in Buildings and called him a demon in The Secret History, written in the same years. What lesson about primary sources does this teach?¶

    1. Ask who wrote a source, for whom, and why, because each book served a different purpose
    2. A primary source is always more trustworthy than a modern history book
    3. Procopius was a liar, so neither book can be used as evidence
    4. The later of two conflicting sources should always be believed
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is A. A primary source comes from the time it describes, but that does not make it true. Buildings was written for the emperor and The Secret History against him, so the first question is "who wrote this, for whom, and why?" The chapter says plainly that the lesson is not that Procopius was a liar (option C); we need both books, read with their purposes in mind. Option B confuses being close to events with being reliable.

    2. Your guide in Hagia Sophia says Justinian entered the finished church and declared, "Solomon, I have outdone thee." How should you test the story?¶

    1. Check whether the line appears on an inscription inside the building today
    2. Accept it, since guides at major sites repeat only verified quotations
    3. Find the earliest source and the gap since the event; here it is a text written about three hundred years later
    4. Compare it with what Mehmed II said when he entered the church in 1453
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is C. The myth-versus-history test is to find the earliest source and ask how long after the event it was written. Procopius in the 550s and Paul the Silentiary in 563 never mention the line. It first appears in the anonymous Diēgēsis, around the 8th or 9th century, which also features an angel and a dream. So the line belongs to the myth of Hagia Sophia. It still tells us something real: later residents believed their church had surpassed Solomon's Temple.

    3. Walking through the Basilica Cistern, you notice that the columns and capitals come in many different styles and heights. Which kind of city layer are you seeing?¶

    1. Converted: a building that kept its walls but changed its use
    2. Reused: spolia, stone pieces carried from older buildings into a new one
    3. Built over: a new structure raised on the rubble of an older one
    4. Removed: gaps left where looters took away the original columns
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is B. The chapter names four ways a layer is added: built over, reused, converted, and removed. Mismatched columns and capitals are the classic clue for reuse. Such reused pieces are called spolia, Latin for "spoils," and builders used them to save quarrying labor and for prestige. The chapter calls the Cistern "a museum of spolia." Conversion would show additions for a new use, such as minarets on a church, and removal would leave empty niches or bare stone.

    4. What is the difference between restoration and reconstruction?¶

    1. Restoration is done by governments, while reconstruction is done by private owners
    2. Restoration rebuilds missing parts from a best guess, while reconstruction only cleans surfaces
    3. Restoration applies to mosaics and paintings, while reconstruction applies only to domes
    4. Restoration repairs and conserves what survives, while reconstruction rebuilds what is lost
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is D. Restoration repairs and conserves the surviving material so it lasts, without inventing what is lost. Reconstruction rebuilds missing parts, from evidence or a best guess. The line matters because a reconstruction can look as old as the original and quietly turn a guess into a fact. Option B reverses the two terms. Hagia Sophia has had both: the dome rebuilt higher after 558 was a reconstruction, while the Fossati brothers' work of 1847–1849 was a restoration.

    5. How are the Nika Riots of 532 connected to the Hagia Sophia we visit today?¶

    1. The rioters attacked the newly finished Hagia Sophia because Justinian had taxed them to build it
    2. Justinian built Hagia Sophia as a fortress where he could hide from future riots
    3. The fires burned the earlier church of Hagia Sophia, and Justinian began the new one five weeks later
    4. The riots began inside Hagia Sophia when the Blues and Greens fought over seats
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is C. In January 532, the Blues and the Greens, normally rival racing fans, united in the Hippodrome against Justinian's tax officials. Fires destroyed much of the city center, including the second church of Hagia Sophia. After Theodora persuaded Justinian not to flee, his generals trapped the crowd in the Hippodrome. On February 23, 532, construction of the new Hagia Sophia began on the ashes of the old one. The riots began in the Hippodrome, not the church, and the new building came after them.

    6. Hagia Sophia's first dome partly collapsed in 558, yet the rebuilt dome has stood for about fifteen centuries. Which analysis best explains its survival?¶

    1. It was rebuilt 6 to 7 m higher with a steeper curve that pushed outward less, and later builders kept adding buttresses
    2. It was rebuilt in lightweight concrete, which earthquakes cannot crack
    3. Its 40 windows were bricked up, which made the dome solid enough to resist earthquakes
    4. Its two half-domes were removed, so the central dome carries less weight
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is A. After earthquakes in 553 and 557, the eastern part of the shallow first dome fell in 558. Isidore the Younger rebuilt it about 6 to 7 m higher, so its steeper curve pushed outward less. Later partial collapses were repaired, and Byzantine and Ottoman builders, including Sinan in the 1570s, added buttresses to hold the walls in against the dome's thrust. The 40 windows and the two half-domes are still part of the building, which rules out options C and D.

    7. In the Basilica Cistern, a guide says the two Medusa heads were set sideways and upside down to cancel the Gorgon's deadly gaze. Applying the chapter's method, what is the better explanation?¶

    1. They were turned over by an earthquake after the Cistern was built
    2. The builders needed blocks of the right height for two short columns, and those positions fit
    3. Ottoman engineers flipped them in the 1500s to show the end of pagan beliefs
    4. They were carved that way on purpose for a Roman temple of Medusa
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is B. No source from 532 explains the placement, so the "cancel her gaze" story fails the myth-versus-history test. The practical explanation fits the evidence: the builders needed blocks of the right height to bring two short columns up to the roof, and sideways and upside down were the positions that fit. That matches the rest of the Cistern, where spolia were used wherever they would fit. Nobody knows which earlier building the heads came from, so option D has no support.

    8. What was Byzantine iconoclasm?¶

    1. The split between the churches of Rome and Constantinople in 1054
    2. The practice of writing icons on wooden panels in strict traditional poses
    3. The Ottoman covering of Christian mosaics after 1453
    4. A policy, from about 726 to 843, of banning and destroying religious images as idolatry
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is D. Iconoclasm, Greek for "image-breaking," was the Byzantine policy of banning religious images, in two periods from about 726 to 787 and from 815 to 843, on the belief that venerating them was idolatry. Icons were restored for good in 843 by the regent Theodora, a victory Orthodox churches still celebrate as the Triumph of Orthodoxy. Option A describes the Great Schism, and option B describes how icons are made, not the movement against them.

    9. The Fourth Crusade was called to attack Muslim Egypt but ended by sacking Christian Constantinople in 1204. Which chain of causes best explains the change?¶

    1. In debt to Venice, the crusaders took Zara, backed Alexios for the throne, and attacked when his promised money never came
    2. The pope ordered the crusaders to punish Constantinople for its part in the Great Schism of 1054
    3. Storms drove the Venetian fleet into the Bosphorus, and the starving crusaders attacked the city for supplies
    4. The Byzantine emperor invited the crusaders inside the walls, then tried to hold them as hostages
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is A. The crusade went wrong because of money. Fewer crusaders arrived than the 33,500 Venice had built ships for, so they could not pay. Doge Enrico Dandolo let the debt wait if they helped take Zara. Then Alexios Angelos promised 200,000 marks to restore his father. Alexios IV could not pay, was overthrown, and the new emperor refused, so the crusaders and Venetians stormed the sea walls on April 12, 1204. Pope Innocent III had excommunicated them over Zara, so option B is wrong.

    10. What modern parallel does the chapter draw from the Byzantine switch from uncial to minuscule script?¶

    1. Handwriting is slower than typing, so students should not learn to write by hand
    2. New scripts always make old books easier to find and read
    3. What is not copied into a new medium is left behind, just as AI tutors learn mainly from text digitized in a few languages
    4. Only professional scribes should decide which books a school reads
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is C. When scribes switched to minuscule in the 9th century, every book had to be copied again. Copying was slow and parchment costly, so works on school reading lists survived and hundreds of others were lost; only 7 of Aeschylus's roughly 90 plays survive. Today's bottleneck is data: AI tutors learn from digitized text, mostly in a handful of languages. The lesson is that deciding what gets copied decides who gets to learn, so the choice should be made widely and on purpose, the opposite of option D.
