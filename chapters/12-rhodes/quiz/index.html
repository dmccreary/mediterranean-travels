    Quiz: Day 8: Rhodes, From the Colossus to the Knights¶

    Test your understanding of Hellenistic Rhodes, the Knights' city, the island's later rulers, and the Greek table with these review questions.

    1. What does the Greek word philoxenia mean, and why did it carry special weight in ancient Greece?¶

    1. "Love of wisdom," because philosophers were honored guests in every city
    2. "Fear of foreigners," because strangers were thought to bring disease
    3. "Friendship among citizens," because only citizens could share a meal
    4. "Love of the stranger," a religious duty protected by Zeus Xenios
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is D. Philoxenia joins philos, "loving," and xenos, a word meaning both "stranger" and "guest." In ancient Greece hospitality was a religious duty: Zeus himself, as Zeus Xenios, protected travelers and punished those who mistreated them. Homer's Odyssey is built on the custom, and the suitors who abuse Odysseus's hospitality die for it. The custom lives on in the fruit or sweet a taverna brings that nobody ordered. "Love of wisdom" (option A) is philosophy.

    2. At the entrance to Mandraki harbor, a fellow traveler points to the two bronze deer and says the Colossus once stood with one foot on each column. What is the best reply?¶

    1. Yes, and ships sailed between its legs until the earthquake of 226 BCE
    2. Probably not: a bronze statue that tall could not stand with its legs so far apart, and it more likely stood on land, though no one knows where
    3. Yes, because the deer columns were built on the statue's original foundations
    4. No, because the Colossus was never built; it existed only in legend
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is B. The famous engravings show the Colossus astride the harbor mouth, but a bronze statue about 33 m tall could not have stood with its legs that far apart, and the entrance was far too wide. Scholars think it more likely stood on land, near the harbor or in a sanctuary of Helios higher in the city; no one knows. It was real, though (option D): Chares of Lindos built it, it stood about 54 years, and Pliny the Elder saw the fallen pieces.

    See: The Colossus of Rhodes

    3. A museum case shows a silver coin with a woman's head in a crested helmet on one side and, on the other, an owl, an olive sprig, and the letters ΑΘΕ. What are you looking at?¶

    1. A Rhodian coin showing the sun god Helios
    2. A Roman coin showing the goddess of war, Minerva
    3. An Athenian silver tetradrachm showing Athena, the coin traders called "owls"
    4. A Spartan coin showing Artemis the huntress
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is C. A god is identified by attributes. A helmet alone could mean any war deity, but the owl and the olive together settle it: Athena, whose sacred animal was the little owl and whose gift to Athens was the first olive tree. The letters ΑΘΕ read A-TH-E, short for "of the Athenians." So this is an Athenian silver tetradrachm, the coin traders across the Mediterranean simply called "owls." Rhodes's own god Helios was a sun god, not a helmeted goddess.

    Concept Tested: Athena

    See: Athena

    4. What set the Rhodes School of Rhetoric apart from the other famous styles of its day?¶

    1. It taught a middle way between the plain Attic style and the ornate, emotional Asian style
    2. It taught only the plain, spare style of Athens and banned emotion from speeches
    3. It taught in Latin, so Roman students did not need to learn Greek
    4. It taught rhetoric through writing alone, with no speeches in front of an audience
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is A. The Attic style, named for Athens, was plain and spare; the Asian style, popular in cities such as Ephesus, was ornate, rhythmic, and emotional. Rhodes stood between them on the map and in taste: rich enough to move an audience, controlled enough to be trusted. Its most famous teacher, Apollonius Molon, reined in the young Cicero's overflowing delivery. Molon listened to students speak and made them do it again, which rules out option D, and Cicero declaimed to him in Greek.

    5. What does a label stating "acidity ≤ 0.3%" tell you about a bottle of extra virgin olive oil?¶

    1. The oil has been refined with heat and chemicals to remove its acid
    2. The oil is too acidic to be sold as extra virgin, which requires at least 1%
    3. Its free acidity is well under the 0.8% limit for extra virgin, a sign of sound, quickly milled olives
    4. The oil was made from unripe olives, which are always lower in fat
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is C. Free acidity measures how much the oil has broken down; it rises when olives are bruised, overripe, or left too long before milling. EU rules require 0.8% or less for extra virgin, so 0.3% is well inside the limit. Option B reverses the rule: a bottle at 1.5% could not be extra virgin. The best grades need no heat or chemicals. The chapter's other tips: look for a harvest date such as "2026/27" and choose dark glass or a tin.

    6. Inside the Palace of the Grand Master you admire grand halls and Hellenistic floor mosaics. How should you date most of what you are seeing?¶

    1. As the knights' original 14th-century palace, preserved untouched since 1522
    2. As an Ottoman palace built for the island's governors after the siege
    3. As a Byzantine citadel that the knights only repainted
    4. As an Italian reconstruction of 1937–1940 on medieval foundations, with mosaics brought from Kos
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is D. The knights built the palace in the 14th century on a Byzantine citadel, but in 1856 lightning ignited gunpowder stored in the nearby Church of St. John, and the explosion wrecked much of the palace. The Italian governor rebuilt it from 1937 to 1940 as a residence for the king of Italy and Mussolini, filling it with mosaics brought from Kos. It is a good museum, but it is mostly a 1930s reconstruction on medieval foundations, a case for Chapter 4's restoration-versus-reconstruction test.

    7. After the siege of 1480, why did the knights rebuild the walls of Rhodes lower and much thicker, with projecting bulwarks?¶

    1. Tall, thin medieval walls shatter under cannon fire, so walls had to absorb shot and let defenders fire along their face
    2. An earthquake had toppled the tall walls, and there was no stone left to rebuild them to their full height
    3. The Ottomans had agreed by treaty to attack only low walls
    4. Lower walls were cheaper, since the order had lost most of its income
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is A. The new problem after 1480 was cannon. Tall, thin medieval walls shatter under cannon fire, so the knights made the walls lower and much thicker, built massive bulwarks that projected outward so defenders could fire along the wall face, and widened the moat. The dry moat was a killing ground, trapping attackers between the outer and main walls. Each tongue held its own stretch. The chapter gives no earthquake, treaty, or money reason for the redesign.

    8. The knights surrendered Rhodes to Suleiman in December 1522. Why could both sides tell the outcome as a victory?¶

    1. The knights kept the island, while Suleiman received a yearly payment of tribute
    2. The knights left with their arms and honor and the inhabitants kept their faith, while Suleiman ended a costly siege and won the island intact
    3. Christian Europe sent a fleet that forced a draw, so neither side lost
    4. Suleiman released all his prisoners, and the knights agreed never to fight again
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is B. A city taken by storm could expect to be sacked and its people killed or enslaved. Instead the knights surrendered on terms: they left with their arms, relics, and honor, inhabitants could go with them, and those who stayed kept their faith and churches. Suleiman ended a siege that had cost him enormous losses and gained the island intact. No help came from Christian Europe (option C), and the knights sailed out on January 1, 1523, later holding Malta against another Ottoman siege in 1565.

    9. Crete's Muslims all left in the 1923 population exchange, yet Rhodes still has a small Turkish-speaking Muslim community. What explains the difference?¶

    1. The Muslims of Rhodes converted to Orthodox Christianity before 1923
    2. The treaty exempted all islands with more than one mosque
    3. Rhodes's Muslims spoke Greek, and the exchange sorted people by language
    4. The exchange applied only to Greece and Turkey, and in 1923 Rhodes belonged to Italy
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is D. The 1923 exchange swapped the Orthodox of Turkey for the Muslims of Greece, but in 1923 Rhodes belonged to neither country: Italy had held it since 1912. So the island's Muslims were not exchanged, and they are Greek citizens today, some still worshipping in their own mosques. The Dodecanese joined Greece only by the 1947 treaty. Option C is wrong twice: the exchange sorted people by religion, not language, and this community's home language is Turkish.

    10. In 1944 a registry list of Jewish residents, prepared for a "routine check," helped the deportation find every family on Rhodes. What lesson does the chapter draw for AI tutors?¶

    1. Schools should never keep any records about their students
    2. AI tutors are dangerous and should not be used in schools
    3. Learner data is not neutral: collect only what teaching needs, control who sees it, and plan for a change in who is in charge
    4. Student data is safe as long as it is collected for an ordinary purpose
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is C. Records made for ordinary purposes, a census, a school roll, a registry, became the tool that found every family in July 1944. The Jews of Rhodes were murdered by people who chose to murder them, but the list made it fast and complete. An AI tutor collects detailed information about each student, so the chapter's safeguards are to collect only what teaching needs, control access, and plan for new people in charge. Option D is exactly the assumption the 1944 list disproves.
