    Quiz: Day 13: Athens, Museums, Tutors, and the City Today¶

    Test your understanding of the Antikythera mechanism, Athens's monuments and modern history, and what the research says about tutoring and new learning technology with these review questions.

    1. What was the Antikythera mechanism?¶

    1. A Roman water clock used to time speeches in the law courts
    2. A bronze statue of a youth recovered from a shipwreck off Crete
    3. A Byzantine astrolabe used by sailors to find their position at sea
    4. A hand-cranked device of bronze gears from the 2nd century BCE that modeled the cycles of the Sun and Moon
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is D. Sponge divers found a Roman-era shipwreck off Antikythera in 1900; it had sunk about 70–60 BCE. Among the finds was a lump of corroded bronze and wood, and in 1902 Valerios Stais saw a gear wheel in it. The surviving 82 fragments hold at least 30 gears from a shoebox-sized box with dials and a crank, covered in tiny Greek inscriptions. Turning the crank modeled the Sun, the Moon, and probably the planets. Option B describes the Antikythera Youth, a statue from the same wreck.

    2. The chapter says the Antikythera mechanism "sits between the tripod and the algorithm." Which analysis explains that position?¶

    1. Like the oracle, it gave different answers to rich and poor; like a modern model, it learned from new examples
    2. Unlike the oracle, its answers were the same for all, fixed in advance, and open to inspection; unlike a learning model, its rules never changed
    3. It was a religious object consulted at Delphi, and also the first computer to learn from data
    4. It combined the Pythia's trance with a written record of every answer she gave
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is B. Compared with the oracle of Chapter 17, the mechanism gave the same answer to anyone who turned the crank, its answer was fixed before the event so it could be checked, and its model could be inspected gear by gear. Compared with the models of Chapter 19, it lacked one thing: its rules were set once by its maker from centuries of records and never changed, so a wrong ratio stayed wrong. A machine-learning model adjusts its own rules from examples, which rules out options A and C.

    3. A Greek parapegma, a stone peg calendar, lists the yearly risings of the stars and the weather expected with them. Which of its predictions should you trust more, and why?¶

    1. The star risings, because they repeat exactly every year, while the weather does not
    2. The weather forecasts, because they were based on centuries of farmers' experience
    3. Both equally, because both were carved in stone by the same astronomers
    4. Neither, because ancient calendars could not predict anything
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is A. Babylonian astronomers found that eclipses and the positions of the Moon and planets repeat in regular cycles, so once a cycle is known, prediction becomes counting. Star risings repeat exactly every year, so a fixed model could predict them for centuries; the weather does not repeat, so the forecasts tied to the stars were far less reliable. Being carved by the same hand (option C) does not make them equally reliable. The chapter's test: before trusting any prediction machine, ask whether the thing it predicts actually repeats.

    4. The Temple of Olympian Zeus was begun about 520 BCE and finished by Hadrian about 650 years later. How did Aristotle use the unfinished temple in his Politics?¶

    1. As proof that democracy builds greater temples than tyranny
    2. As a model of the perfect Corinthian proportions for future architects
    3. As an example of tyrants keeping their subjects busy and poor with vast building projects
    4. As evidence that Zeus had abandoned Athens for Olympia
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is C. The temple was begun by the tyrant family of the Peisistratids and finished by Hadrian in 131–132 CE, with 104 Corinthian columns about 17 m tall, of which 15 still stand. In his Politics, Aristotle argued that tyrants keep their subjects busy and poor with vast building projects, and he named the Peisistratids' temple beside the pyramids of Egypt. Its long building history reads like a summary of Athens's masters, including Antiochus IV and Sulla.

    5. What was the Athens Polytechnic uprising, remembered every November 17?¶

    1. A riot against Ottoman rule that began the Greek War of Independence
    2. A student occupation in November 1973 against the military junta, crushed when a tank broke through the gate
    3. A protest against austerity during the debt crisis of 2010
    4. A strike by university teachers demanding higher wages in 1981
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is B. From November 14 to 17, 1973, students occupied the National Technical University on Patission Street to protest the colonels' junta, which had ruled since April 21, 1967. They broadcast from a makeshift radio station, and thousands joined them. Early on November 17 a tank crashed through the main gate; research documents at least 24 civilians killed. The junta fell in July 1974. The bent gate is preserved as a memorial, and a march sets out each year on November 17.

    6. During the debt crisis, Greece's economy shrank by about a quarter. If it stood at 100 before the crisis, how much must it grow to return to 100?¶

    1. About 25 percent, the same as the loss
    2. About 15 percent, since growth compounds each year
    3. About 50 percent, since debt doubles the loss
    4. About one third, because growth is measured from the new, lower base of 75
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is D. A fall of about a quarter leaves 75. To climb back to 100, the economy must grow by 25 ÷ 75, or one third, not one quarter (option A), because growth is measured from the new, lower base. At a steady 2 percent a year the climb takes about 15 years, and at 1 percent about 29 years. Option B confuses the number of years with the percentage needed. That is why a crisis that "ended" in 2018 still shapes wages and drove many young Greeks abroad.

    7. Greek coffee is made exactly like the Turkish coffee of Istanbul. Why do Greeks call it by a different name?¶

    1. The European Union required a Greek name when Greece adopted the euro
    2. Greek coffee uses a different bean grown on Crete
    3. After the Turkish military intervention in Cyprus in 1974, "Turkish coffee" came to feel wrong, and the name changed
    4. Greeks invented the drink first, and Turks later borrowed it
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is C. Greek coffee is ground fine, boiled in a long-handled briki, and poured unfiltered, the same drink as Turkish coffee. Until the 1970s Greeks usually called it "Turkish coffee" too. After the Turkish military intervention in Cyprus in 1974, the name came to feel wrong to many Greeks, and within a few years "Greek coffee" was the usual name. The coffee did not change; the word did, because a word can carry a wound. Coffee reached Istanbul from Yemen, so option D has no support.

    8. A 2020 review of randomized school tutoring trials found an average effect of about 0.37 standard deviations. Where does that move the average tutored student, compared with a class that was not tutored?¶

    1. From the 50th to about the 64th percentile
    2. From the 50th to about the 98th percentile
    3. From a score of 50 percent to a score of 87 percent on the test
    4. Nowhere meaningful, since effects below one standard deviation are too small to matter
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is A. An effect size states the difference between groups in standard deviations, and the useful translation is a percentile. About a third of a standard deviation moves the average student from the 50th to about the 64th percentile, a real gain. Option B is Bloom's famous 2 sigma result, which came from small, short studies and has not held up at full size. A percentile is not a test score (option C), and the chapter calls even smaller effects large by education research standards (option D).

    9. How does mastery learning differ from conventional teaching?¶

    1. It holds time fixed and lets mastery vary, so grades spread out across the class
    2. It lets the fastest students skip every test and move on at once
    3. It replaces teachers entirely with self-paced software
    4. It holds mastery fixed and lets time vary, giving targeted practice and a second check to students below the threshold
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is D. Conventional teaching holds time fixed: everyone gets three weeks on fractions, and the grades spread out, which is option A. Mastery learning, proposed by Bloom in 1968, holds mastery fixed and lets time vary. A student who scores 70 percent on the fractions check does not move on with a C; she gets targeted practice on what she missed, takes a second check, and moves on when she reaches a threshold such as 80 or 90 percent. It appears in both of Bloom's stronger conditions.

    10. Socrates' warning that writing weakens memory survives only because Plato wrote it down, and similar fears returned with printing and calculators. What does this pattern suggest about worries over AI?¶

    1. The worries are always wrong, so concerns about AI over-reliance can be ignored
    2. The fear recurs with every new learning tool; it is worth taking seriously, but the answer lies in how the tool is used, not in refusing it
    3. Each new tool has made people steadily less intelligent, so AI should be banned from schools
    4. Only writing was ever feared, so the comparison with AI is mistaken
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is B. In the Phaedrus, King Thamus warns that writing is "an aid not to memory, but to reminiscence," yet we know the warning only through Plato's careful writing. The abbot Trithemius urged monks to keep copying by hand, and had his praise of scribes printed; teachers later argued over calculators. The fear recurs, but the chapter also says AI over-reliance is real, so option A goes too far. Guardrails decide whether a tutor helps or harms, which rules out a blanket ban.
