    Quiz: Day 4: Bazaars, Scripts, and Presses¶

    Test your understanding of the bazaars and spice trade, the multi-faith Ottoman Empire, and the long road from scribe to printing press with these review questions.

    1. You find a brass lamp you like in the Grand Bazaar. Which approach to bargaining follows the chapter's advice?¶

    1. Offer a very low price you would never actually pay, to leave room for the seller to come down
    2. Decide privately the most you would happily pay before asking the price, then walk toward that number in a friendly way
    3. Refuse any tea the seller offers, since accepting it obliges you to buy
    4. Bargain hard over every item in the shop, including the spices sold by weight
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is B. The chapter's shortcut is to set your price before you ask theirs: decide the most you would happily pay and keep it to yourself, so the conversation is a friendly walk toward your number. The rules also say never name a price you would not pay (option A) and do not bargain on food, spices sold by weight, or price-tagged goods (option D). Accepting tea creates no obligation, and walking away politely is always allowed.

    2. Why was pepper that was cheap on India's Malabar Coast so expensive in Venice around 1450, and what changed in 1498?¶

    1. Every merchant and ruler along the route took a share, until Vasco da Gama's sea route around Africa skipped the middlemen
    2. Pepper spoiled quickly on the journey, until the Portuguese learned to dry it in 1498
    3. Venice taxed spices heavily to fund its fleet, until the Ottomans conquered Venice in 1498
    4. Pepper grew only in tiny amounts, until new farms in Egypt began producing it in 1498
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is A. A sack of pepper passed from Calicut to Aden, up the Red Sea, across to the Nile, through Cairo to Alexandria, where the Mamluk sultan taxed it and a Venetian bought it. At least five sets of merchants and three sets of rulers took a share. In 1498 Vasco da Gama reached Calicut by sailing around Africa, opening a route that skipped every middleman between India and Lisbon. Spices were valued partly because they did not spoil, which rules out option B.

    3. At the Spice Bazaar, a stall offers a large, cheap bag labeled "Turkish saffron." What is the most likely truth, and what should real saffron look like?¶

    1. It is genuine saffron, because Turkey grows so much that it is sold in bulk
    2. It is saffron from Kozani in Greece, relabeled for tourists
    3. It is real saffron powder, which is cheaper than threads but has the same flavor
    4. It is probably safflower; real saffron comes by the gram in small sealed packets of deep red threads
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is D. Big, cheap bags labeled "Turkish saffron" usually hold safflower petals, a different plant that colors food but has none of saffron's flavor. Saffron is the hand-picked stigmas of a crocus, only three per flower, so a kilogram takes around 150,000 flowers. Real saffron is sold by the gram in small sealed packets, its threads deep red with lighter tips and shaped like tiny trumpets. In warm water it releases its color slowly. If the price seems too good, ask to see the threads.

    4. In what order did a Roman bather move through the main rooms of the public baths?¶

    1. Hot room, steam room, cold plunge, and then a massage on the navel stone
    2. Exercise court, library, hot room, and then a warm pool
    3. Changing room, cold room (frigidarium), warm room (tepidarium), and hot room (caldarium)
    4. Warm room, hot room, changing room, and then a cold room in the garden
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is C. In Roman bath culture, bathers moved from a changing room to a cold room, the frigidarium, then a warm room, the tepidarium, then a hot room, the caldarium. The rooms were heated by hot air from furnaces circulating under raised floors and inside walls, a system called the hypocaust. The navel stone and the scrub in option A belong to the later Turkish bath, which adapted the Roman custom with steam.

    5. Under the Ottoman millet system, how were non-Muslim communities governed?¶

    1. Each religious community, led by its own head, ran its own worship, schools, charities, and family courts, while paying a special tax
    2. Non-Muslims were required to convert within a generation or leave the empire
    3. All communities were governed by the same Islamic courts and had identical rights and duties
    4. Each community elected representatives to a parliament that made laws for the whole empire
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is A. The millet system organized non-Muslims into religious communities, the Greek Orthodox, Armenians, and Jews, each under its own religious head. Each ran its own worship, schools, charities, and courts for marriage, divorce, and inheritance. In return non-Muslims paid the cizye poll tax and lived under restrictions, such as needing permission to build churches. So it allowed real self-rule with real limits on equality, which rules out option C. Forced conversion (option B) was the Spanish policy of 1492, not the Ottoman one.

    6. Who were the Sephardic Jewish refugees who settled in the Ottoman Empire after 1492?¶

    1. Jews from Germany who fled persecution and spoke Yiddish
    2. Jews expelled from Spain, welcomed by Sultan Bayezid II, who kept their Judeo-Spanish language, Ladino
    3. Jews from Jerusalem who came to Istanbul after Mehmed II's conquest and spoke Hebrew
    4. Jews from Venice who moved to Rhodes with the Knights Hospitaller and spoke Italian
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is B. "Sephardic" comes from Sefarad, the Hebrew name for Spain. On March 31, 1492, Ferdinand and Isabella ordered Spain's Jews to convert or leave. Many refugees found a welcome from Sultan Bayezid II and settled in Istanbul, Edirne, İzmir, and above all Salonica. They brought skills in medicine, finance, textiles, and printing, and kept Ladino, a form of 15th-century Spanish. Later, after Suleiman took Rhodes in 1522, Sephardic families settled in its walled town.

    7. On your first morning in Rhodes, your phone shows a different time from the ship's daily program, and the all-aboard time is this afternoon. Which time should you use?¶

    1. Your phone's time, because phones always update to the local network automatically
    2. Turkish time, because the ship is registered to its departure port of Istanbul
    3. The average of the two times, to leave a safe margin
    4. Ship's time as announced in the daily program, since the ship will not wait for late passengers
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is D. The all-aboard time is the one number to remember every port day, because the ship will not wait for late passengers on independent outings. When the ship sails from Kuşadası to Rhodes, its clocks go back one hour for Greece. Phones usually update when they find a Greek network, but at sea they may not, and they sometimes pick up a Turkish tower near the coast, which is why option A is risky. The rule is always to use ship's time.

    8. The chapter says a scribe's cost is almost all marginal cost, while a press has a large fixed cost. What does that comparison show?¶

    1. Scribes were always cheaper than presses, which is why handwriting lasted so long
    2. The press was cheaper for single copies but too expensive for large editions
    3. For one copy the scribe is cheaper, but for many copies the press wins, the same shape as an AI tutor today
    4. Fixed and marginal costs were equal, so the choice between scribe and press was a matter of taste
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is C. Each new copy by a scribe takes as long as the last, so the cost per copy stays high. A press needs a large up-front cost, casting type, building the press, and setting every page, but each extra copy costs very little. So for one copy the scribe is cheaper, while for a hundred the press wins overwhelmingly. The chapter notes that digital learning has the same shape: a good AI tutor is expensive to build and nearly free to share. Option B reverses the logic.

    9. About 275 years passed between Gutenberg's press and the first Ottoman book printed in Arabic script in 1729. Which analysis best fits the chapter's evidence?¶

    1. A 1485 decree by Bayezid II banning printing on pain of death fully explains the delay
    2. Several causes worked together: reverence for handwritten sacred text, a script needing hundreds of letter forms, scribes' livelihoods, and weak demand
    3. The Ottomans had never heard of printing until Müteferrika brought the idea from Europe
    4. Arabic script was physically impossible to print until new technology appeared in the 1720s
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is B. Most historians now think several causes worked together. Handwriting the Quran was an act of devotion; joined letters that change shape need hundreds of sorts; copyists and calligraphers lived by handwriting; and manuscripts met the needs of a small reading public. The famous 1485 ban (option A) survives in no copy, and many historians doubt it existed. The Ottomans knew of printing: Istanbul had a Hebrew press from 1493, which rules out option C, and European printers had already printed Arabic, crudely.

    10. Müteferrika's printed books sat unsold. What lesson does the chapter draw from this for AI tutors?¶

    1. Cheap copies do not create readers; an AI tutor reaches only children with a device, a connection, a language it speaks well, and a reason to trust it
    2. Printing was a failed technology, so AI tutors will probably fail in the same way
    3. Books should be given away free, because price was the only barrier to reading
    4. An AI tutor should be built only after every child can already read well
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is A. Printing made copies cheap but did not make readers: by one estimate, only about one person in ten could read Ottoman script on the eve of the 1928 reform. The circle widened for real only when schooling and a simpler script came together. Likewise, an AI tutor has a high cost to build and almost no cost per learner, but it will reach only children who have a device, a connection, a language it speaks well, and a reason to trust it. Price was never the only gate (option C).
