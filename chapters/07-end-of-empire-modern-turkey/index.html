    A Sunday Between Empire and Republic¶

    Day 5, Sunday, November 15, is our last day in Istanbul. The Viking Star is tied up at Galataport in Karaköy, and it sails at 6:00 PM, just after sunset. The Grand Bazaar is closed, as it is every Sunday, so the city shows us a different face: families out for a long breakfast, ferries full of people crossing to the Asian shore for no reason but the ride, and church bells in Beyoğlu answering the call to prayer. A short walk up the shore from the ship stands Dolmabahçe Palace, where the last sultans lived, and where Mustafa Kemal Atatürk, the founder of the Turkish Republic, died at 9:05 AM on November 10, 1938, eighty-eight years and five days before our visit. We arrived in Istanbul on November 11, the morning after the anniversary, when flags and portraits were still out all over the city.

    This chapter tells how the empire whose palaces and mosques we have walked through for four days came to an end. It is a story of about two centuries: how a state that once reached from Hungary to Arabia lost its lands one by one, how Greece became an independent country, how the First World War brought the warships of Britain and France to the Dardanelles we sail through tonight, and how an officer born in Salonica built a new republic on the ruins and then changed the very letters in which its people read. It is also a story with more than one side. The same years that Turks remember as a war of liberation, Greeks remember as a catastrophe, and both memories are true to the people who hold them.

    From the Sultan's Palace to the Schoolhouse Blackboard

    Today we walk past a chandelier said to weigh as much as a truck, a gate in Fener that has been shut for two centuries, and a clock stopped at 9:05. By sailaway, the portraits on every shop wall and the letters on every street sign will have a story behind them, and we will know why a whole country went back to school in 1928. Look closer.

Part Five looks at Istanbul on an ordinary Sunday. The Learning for All note then tells the story of the 1928 alphabet reform and the Nation's Schools.

    Part One: The Long Retreat¶

    Ottoman Decline¶

    Ottoman decline is the name historians long gave to the gradual loss of territory, military strength, and economic independence by the Ottoman Empire, from its failed siege of Vienna in 1683 to its end after the First World War. In 1853 the Russian tsar, Nicholas I, is reported to have called it a "sick man" in a conversation with a British diplomat, and the phrase "the sick man of Europe" stuck. The losses were real. But many historians now dislike the word "decline," because a state that kept going for 240 years after Vienna, reformed its army, law, and schools, and fought the Great Powers to a standstill at Gallipoli was not simply dying. "Transformation under pressure" describes it better, and it is the story Dolmabahçe Palace tells.

    The pressures came from several directions at once.

    • Military. After 1683, Habsburg Austria and then Russia grew stronger than the Ottomans. The Treaty of Karlowitz in 1699 gave up most of Hungary, the first large permanent loss. The Treaty of Küçük Kaynarca in 1774, after a war with Russia, made the Crimean Khanate independent, and Russia annexed Crimea in 1783.
    • Economic. The sea route around Africa, which Chapter 6 described, moved the spice trade away from Ottoman ports, and European factory goods undersold Ottoman crafts in the 19th century. The capitulations, trade privileges first granted to foreign merchants when the empire was strong, became channels of foreign power when it was weak: foreigners in the empire paid lower customs duties and answered only to their own consuls' courts.
    • Nationalism. The idea that each nation should have its own state spread from the French Revolution through the Balkans. For an empire built on religious communities living side by side, the millet system of Chapter 6, it was dynamite. Serbs, Greeks, Romanians, Bulgarians, Albanians, Armenians, and Arabs each came to want a state of their own.

    The Ottomans answered with reform. Sultan Mahmud II destroyed the Janissaries, the old infantry corps that had resisted every change, in 1826. In 1839 the Gülhane Edict, read aloud in the park we visited on Day 3, began the Tanzimat, "reorganization": new courts, new schools, a new army, and in 1856 a promise of equality for subjects of every faith. In 1876 the empire got its first constitution and parliament, which Sultan Abdülhamid II suspended in 1878 and ruled without for thirty years. In 1908 a group of reforming officers and officials, the Young Turks, forced him to restore it. Their party, the Committee of Union and Progress, would lead the empire into the First World War.

    Worked example: reading Dolmabahçe as a balance sheet. Let's walk through the palace and read it as a record of the empire's money. Sultan Abdülmecid built Dolmabahçe Palace between 1843 and 1856 on a bay of the Bosphorus that had been filled in to make a garden, which is what dolma bahçe, "filled garden," means. Its architects were Garabet Balyan and his son Nigoğayos, members of an Armenian family that designed many of the city's 19th-century buildings, a reminder of the multi-faith empire. The style is European: a facade about 600 m long on the water, 285 rooms, and a Ceremonial Hall whose crystal chandelier is said to weigh about four and a half tonnes and to have been a gift from Queen Victoria. A figure of 14 tonnes of gold leaf on the ceilings is often quoted. Now read the dates beside the building. In 1854, during the Crimean War, the empire took its first foreign loan. By 1875 it could no longer pay the interest and defaulted. In 1881 its European creditors set up the Ottoman Public Debt Administration, which took over the collection of the taxes on salt, tobacco, alcohol, stamps, silk, and fishing and sent the proceeds to bondholders abroad. A palace built to show that the empire was a modern European power, and a debt office that showed how far it depended on Europe, came within a generation of each other.

    Decline or Transformation?

    Notice how the two columns of that table run together. An empire that was only dying would not have built new schools, a constitution, and a modern army while it lost provinces. Think of it as a household remodeling the kitchen while the roof leaks: both stories are true, and the end came from war, not old age.

    Ottoman Rule in Greece¶

    Ottoman rule in Greece is the period during which the lands of today's Greece were part of the Ottoman Empire. It began in Macedonia and Thrace in the late 14th century and spread piece by piece: Thessaloniki fell for good in 1430, Athens in 1458, the Peloponnese in 1460, Rhodes in 1522, and Crete, after a siege of Candia (Heraklion) that lasted from 1648 to 1669, among the last. Most of the Ionian Islands, off the west coast, stayed Venetian and never became Ottoman. The end came just as unevenly, from 1830 for the first Greek kingdom to 1912 for Thessaloniki, so a Greek city might have been Ottoman for two centuries or for nearly five.

    For most Greeks, Ottoman rule meant life as members of the Orthodox millet, shaped by a handful of institutions.

    • The Church. The Ecumenical Patriarch in Istanbul, whom Chapter 4 introduced, was both the head of the Orthodox Church and the official answerable to the sultan for its people.
    • Taxes and the levy. Christians paid the poll tax, the cizye, and from the 15th to the 17th century the devşirme took boys from many Greek villages for the sultan's service.
    • Local notables. Villages and towns were often run day to day by their own Christian notables, who collected the taxes and bargained with the Ottoman governor.
    • Klephts and armatoloi. In the mountains, bands of klephts, outlaws who lived by raiding, became folk heroes in songs, while Christian militias called armatoloi were paid to keep the roads safe, and men sometimes moved from one group to the other.

    By the 18th century some Greeks had risen high inside the Ottoman system. The Phanariots, wealthy Greek families of the Fener (Greek Phanar) district on the Golden Horn, served as the sultan's chief interpreters and, from 1711, as governors of the Danubian provinces in what is now Romania. Greek merchants carried much of the trade of the Balkans and the Black Sea, and their money paid for Greek schools and Greek printing presses in Venice, Vienna, and Bucharest. The great red-brick building we can see on the hill above Fener, built in 1881–1883, is the modern home of the Patriarchal school founded in the 15th century, which still teaches today.

    Worked example: the Parthenon's Ottoman life. Let's follow one building we will see on Day 10 through its Ottoman centuries. When the Ottomans took Athens in 1458, the Parthenon on the Acropolis had already been a Christian church for many centuries. The new rulers made it a mosque and added a minaret at one corner. Travelers in the 17th century described it as nearly complete, roof and all. In 1687 a Venetian army besieged the Acropolis, and the Ottoman garrison stored gunpowder inside the Parthenon. On September 26 a Venetian shell hit it, the powder exploded, and the middle of the building was blown out; about three hundred people are said to have died. The Venetians left the next year, and the Ottomans built a small mosque inside the ruin. The roofless Parthenon we photograph is a casualty of an Ottoman–Venetian war, which Chapter 15 tells in full.

    One popular story about these centuries deserves Chapter 4's myth-versus-history test. In Greece, generations of schoolchildren learned of the krypto scholio, the "secret school," in which priests taught children to read at night, by candlelight in churches, because the Ottomans forbade Greek schooling. It inspired a famous painting of 1885 and a much-loved poem. Many historians now consider it a 19th-century legend: no Ottoman ban on Greek schools has been found, and the Church ran schools openly in many towns. What is true is that schools were few, poor, and mostly for boys, so the story may remember a real hunger for learning in a mistaken form.

    The Greek War of Independence¶

    The Greek War of Independence was the uprising of Greeks against Ottoman rule that began in 1821 and ended with an independent Greek state recognized in 1830, its borders fixed in 1832. It was prepared by a secret society, the Filiki Eteria fee-lee-KEE eh-teh-REE-ah ("Friendly Society"), founded by three Greek merchants in Odessa in 1814. In March 1821 one of its leaders crossed from Russia into the Danubian provinces with a small army and a revolt that quickly failed, but the revolt that broke out in the Peloponnese in the same weeks spread across the south of Greece and the islands.

    It was a war of great cruelty on both sides. When Greek fighters took the Ottoman town of Tripolitsa in September 1821, they killed thousands of its Muslim and Jewish inhabitants. In April 1822 an Ottoman force on the island of Chios killed or enslaved tens of thousands of Greeks, a massacre that Eugène Delacroix painted in 1824 and that turned European opinion. Volunteers from across Europe, the Philhellenes ("friends of the Greeks"), came to fight, the poet Lord Byron among them; he died of fever at Missolonghi in 1824. By 1825 the sultan had called in the army of Egypt, which nearly crushed the revolt. What saved it was the Great Powers. On October 20, 1827, a combined British, French, and Russian fleet destroyed the Ottoman and Egyptian fleet in the bay of Navarino, the last great battle fought entirely between sailing ships.

    Worked example: the closed gate in Fener. Let's find the war in Istanbul, where it left a mark we can still see today. When the revolt broke out, the Ecumenical Patriarch, Gregory V, condemned it, as the sultan required, but the sultan held him responsible for his flock all the same. On Easter Sunday 1821 (April 22 by our calendar), Gregory was hanged from the central gate of the Patriarchate in Fener, in his robes. Greek sailors recovered his body from the Bosphorus and took it to Odessa; in 1871 it was brought to Athens, where he is buried in the cathedral, and his statue stands outside the old University of Athens. The central gate of the Patriarchate was closed after his death and has stayed closed for more than two hundred years; visitors still enter by a side door. One gate in an Istanbul street ties together the millet system, a war in the Peloponnese, and a statue we can see in Athens.

    Greece celebrates its independence on March 25, and every Greek child learns that on that day in 1821 Bishop Germanos of Patras raised the banner of revolt at the monastery of Agia Lavra. Here Chapter 4's test is useful again. Fighting had already begun a few days earlier, the earliest written account of the Agia Lavra scene was published years later by a foreign author, and March 25 is also the feast of the Annunciation, a date with obvious meaning for a national rebirth. Many historians treat the scene as a symbolic tradition rather than a recorded event. The revolt itself is history; its date is partly a story a nation chose to tell about its beginning.

    The Modern Greek State¶

    The modern Greek state is the independent Greece that began as the small Kingdom of Greece in 1832 and grew, over 115 years, to the borders it has today. The Great Powers chose its first king, Otto, a seventeen-year-old Bavarian prince, who landed at Nafplio in 1833. In 1834 the capital moved to Athens, then a battered town of a few thousand people, chosen for its ancient name. Otto's Bavarian architects laid out a neoclassical city of straight avenues around a royal palace, which is now the Greek Parliament, facing Syntagma ("Constitution") Square, named for the constitution that a revolt forced the king to grant in 1843.

    The first kingdom held fewer than a million people, while several times as many Greeks lived outside it, in Istanbul, Smyrna, Thessaloniki, Crete, and across Anatolia. From that gap grew the Megali Idea meh-GAH-lee ee-DEH-ah, the "Great Idea" of uniting all Greeks in one state, with Constantinople as its capital. It guided Greek politics for almost eighty years. It won territory in every generation, and it ended in Anatolia in 1922, as Part Three tells.

    Worked example: when did our Greek stops become Greek? Let's read our own itinerary as a history of the Greek state. Athens and Delphi lay inside the first kingdom of 1832, so they have been Greek the longest. Heraklion became Greek in 1913, the year Crete's union with Greece was finally recognized, so the Cretans we meet on Day 9 had great-grandparents who grew up under the sultan or in the autonomous Cretan State. Rhodes became Greek only in 1947, after 35 years of Italian rule and war, so on Day 8 we may meet people whose grandparents went to Italian schools. From the first kingdom to Rhodes is 115 years. In one cruise we pass through three generations of Greek state-building, and each island's sense of itself still carries the date it joined.

    Part Two: The Straits and the Great War¶

    The Dardanelles Strait¶

    The Dardanelles Strait (in Turkish, Çanakkale Boğazı; in ancient Greek, the Hellespont) is the narrow channel, about 61 km long, that connects the Sea of Marmara with the Aegean Sea. It is between about 1.2 and 6 km wide, narrowest at the Narrows beside the town of Çanakkale, where we dock tomorrow. Its north shore is the Gallipoli Peninsula, in Europe; its south shore is in Asia. A strong surface current runs out of it toward the Aegean, which made it hard for sailing ships to enter. Together with the Bosphorus, it is the only sea route between the Black Sea and the rest of the world's oceans, so whoever holds both shores holds the key to that route, the chokepoint idea of Chapter 1.

    The strait has been crossed in legend and in history, and each crossing below is one we can think about tonight.

    • Leander, in myth. A young man swam it every night to visit Hero, a priestess on the far shore, until a storm drowned him. In 1810 Lord Byron swam it to prove it could be done.
    • Xerxes, in 480 BCE. Herodotus tells us that the Persian king crossed on bridges of boats, and that when a storm broke the first bridges, he had the water whipped.
    • The Allied fleet, in 1915. It tried to force the strait from the Aegean and was stopped at the Narrows, as the next sections tell.
    • The 1915 Çanakkale Bridge, since 2022. Opened on March 18, 2022, its central span of 2,023 m is the longest of any suspension bridge in the world; the number honors the Republic's centenary year.

    Since the Montreux Convention of 1936, Turkey controls the passage of warships through both straits, while merchant ships pass freely in peacetime.

    Worked example: tonight's sailing. Let's work out what the ship does between Istanbul and Çanakkale. From Galataport to the mouth of the Dardanelles at Gelibolu is about 210 km across the Sea of Marmara, and from there to Çanakkale another 40 km or so, about 250 km in all. The ship leaves at 6:00 PM and docks at 9:00 AM, 15 hours later, so its average speed is about 250 ÷ 15 ≈ 17 km/h, roughly 9 knots, well below a cruise ship's normal speed. It can take its time, which is why we sail through the night and arrive in the morning. Somewhere in the dark we pass under the 1915 Çanakkale Bridge, near the strait's Marmara end; if we are up early, we may see the last stretch to Çanakkale at first light. In 1915 an Allied fleet tried to make the same trip in reverse, up the strait from the Aegean, past forts on both shores and lines of mines. Its goal was Istanbul, nearly 300 km from the strait's mouth, and it never got past the Narrows.

    World War I¶

    World War I (1914–1918) was the war between the Allied Powers, chiefly Britain, France, Russia, and later Italy and the United States, and the Central Powers, Germany, Austria-Hungary, the Ottoman Empire, and Bulgaria. For the Ottoman Empire it was the last of a decade of wars that had begun with Italy's invasion of Libya in 1911 and continued with the Balkan Wars of 1912–1913, and it ended the empire. The war was led on the Ottoman side by three men of the Committee of Union and Progress, Enver, Talat, and Cemal Pashas, who had taken control of the government in 1913.

    Worked example: two ships seized, two ships arrived. Let's see how the empire was drawn into the war, because the story passes through our strait. In the summer of 1914 two modern battleships were nearly finished in British shipyards for the Ottoman navy, paid for in part by public donations collected across the empire. On the eve of war, the British government seized both for its own navy, and Ottoman public opinion was furious. On August 2, 1914, the Ottoman government signed a secret alliance with Germany. A week later two German warships, the battlecruiser Goeben and the cruiser Breslau, escaped a British pursuit across the Mediterranean and were allowed into the Dardanelles. The Ottomans announced that they had bought them, and their German crews put on fezzes. On October 29, under Ottoman flags but still under their German admiral, they bombarded Russian ports on the Black Sea. Russia declared war on November 2, and Britain and France a few days later. Two ships taken away and two ships let in through the Dardanelles had helped carry an exhausted empire into a world war.

    The war brought terrible suffering to the empire's civilians. The worst fell on the Armenians. On April 24, 1915, the day before the Allied landings at Gallipoli, Ottoman authorities arrested hundreds of Armenian community leaders and intellectuals in Istanbul. In the months that followed, the government deported the Armenian population of Anatolia toward the Syrian desert, and huge numbers died in massacres, on death marches, and in camps; estimates of the dead range from about 600,000 to more than a million. Assyrian and Greek communities also suffered mass killings and deportations. Most historians, and many governments, including that of the United States since 2021, call the destruction of the Armenians a genocide. The Turkish state rejects that term and describes the deaths as part of a wartime tragedy that struck all the peoples of Anatolia. It is one of the most painful subjects in the region, and the thoughtful traveler of Chapter 2 listens before speaking about it.

    On October 30, 1918, the Ottoman government signed an armistice with the Allies aboard a British battleship at Mudros, on the Greek island of Lemnos, just outside the Dardanelles. Two weeks later an Allied fleet sailed up the strait it had failed to force in 1915 and anchored off Istanbul, and Allied troops occupied the city.

    The Gallipoli Campaign¶

    The Gallipoli Campaign (in Turkish, the battles of Çanakkale) was the Allied attempt in 1915 to force the Dardanelles, capture Istanbul, knock the Ottoman Empire out of the war, and open a sea route to Russia. It began with naval attacks. On March 18, 1915, a great Allied fleet tried to push through the Narrows and lost three battleships to a line of mines laid secretly in the days before; Turks commemorate that day as the Çanakkale Victory. On April 25 the Allies landed troops on the Gallipoli Peninsula: British forces at Cape Helles, its southern tip, while the French made a diversionary landing at Kum Kale on the Asian shore before moving to Helles, and the Australian and New Zealand Army Corps, the Anzacs, at the cove that now bears their name. The armies fought for eight months over a few kilometers of ridges and gullies, in summer heat and disease and then winter cold, until the Allies withdrew in December 1915 and January 1916. More than 100,000 men died.

    For Turks the campaign is a defining national memory, the defense of the homeland at the gate of the capital. For Australians and New Zealanders it is a founding memory too, commemorated every year on Anzac Day, April 25. And for the history in this chapter, it did one more thing: it made the name of a young Ottoman officer. Chapter 8 tells the campaign in full tomorrow, on the battlefield itself, including the Çanakkale Martyrs' Memorial and the words of reconciliation attributed to Atatürk.

    Mustafa Kemal Atatürk¶

    Mustafa Kemal Atatürk (1881–1938) was the Ottoman army officer who led the Turkish national movement after World War I and became the founder and first president of the Republic of Turkey. He was born Mustafa in Salonica (Thessaloniki), the son of a customs clerk; tradition says a mathematics teacher gave him the name Kemal, "perfection." The surname Atatürk, "Father Turk," was given to him by the Turkish parliament in 1934.

    On April 25, 1915, he commanded the 19th Division at Gallipoli. When the Anzacs came ashore below the heights, he led his men onto the ridge in time to stop their advance, at enormous cost to his own troops. In August he held the line again. Gallipoli made him a colonel, a hero to his soldiers, and a name the public knew. In May 1919, as the Allies occupied Istanbul and Greek troops landed at Smyrna, he sailed to Samsun on the Black Sea, nominally to restore order, and instead began to organize resistance.

    Part Three: From Empire to Republic, 1918–1923¶

    Four Wars, Two Names Each

    If the years from 1918 to 1923 blur together, that is normal: several wars, three treaties, and two governments overlap, and each side gives them different names. Hold on to one thread at a time, Smyrna for the Greeks and Ankara for the Turks, and use the treaty sim below to see how it all ended. Most people need two readings to sort this part out.

    The Greco-Turkish War¶

    The Greco-Turkish War (1919–1922) was the war between Greece and the Turkish nationalist movement over western Anatolia. It began on May 15, 1919, when Greek troops landed at Smyrna (İzmir), with the approval of the Allies meeting at the Paris Peace Conference. Smyrna had a large Greek Orthodox population, and the Greek prime minister, Eleftherios Venizelos, saw a chance to fulfill the Megali Idea. In 1920 the Treaty of Sèvres SEV-ruh gave Greece Eastern Thrace and the administration of the Smyrna zone. Greek armies pushed deep into Anatolia, until the Turkish nationalists stopped them in a three-week battle on the Sakarya River, in August and September 1921, about 80 km from Ankara. In August 1922 the Turkish army broke the Greek front, and on September 9 it entered Smyrna. Days later a fire destroyed the Greek and Armenian quarters of the city; tens of thousands of Christians fled to the waterfront, and many died. Who set the fire is still disputed, a clear case of Chapter 4's contested history, though many historians attribute it to Turkish forces.

    The two sides do not even share a name for the war. In Turkey it is the western front of the War of Independence (Kurtuluş Savaşı, "War of Liberation"), and August 30, the day of the decisive victory in 1922, is a national holiday. In Greece it is the Asia Minor Catastrophe (Mikrasiatiki Katastrofi), the end of three thousand years of Greek life on the Anatolian coast, including the Ionian cities we will see around Ephesus on Day 7. Both armies and irregular bands burned villages and killed civilians as they advanced and retreated. Both names are true to the people who use them.

    The war's bitterest result was signed in Lausanne, Switzerland, on January 30, 1923: the Convention Concerning the Exchange of Greek and Turkish Populations. It required the compulsory exchange of the Greek Orthodox Christians living in Turkey for the Muslims living in Greece. About 1.2 million Orthodox Christians, most of whom had already fled in 1922, and about 400,000 Muslims were made to leave homes their families had lived in for centuries, and none could return. Two groups were exempted: the Greek Orthodox of Istanbul who had settled there before October 30, 1918, and the Muslims of Western Thrace, in northeastern Greece. Under the main treaty, the Greek Orthodox of the islands of Imbros and Tenedos, near the mouth of the Dardanelles, were exempted too.

    Faith, Not Language

    Notice the rule the treaty used to decide who was "Greek" and who was "Turkish": religion, not language. Turkish-speaking Orthodox Christians from Cappadocia were sent to Greece, and Greek-speaking Muslims from Crete were sent to Turkey. The millet system of Chapter 6 had sorted people by faith for five centuries, and in its last act it sorted them across a border.

    Worked example: the exchange on our route. Let's find the exchange at three of our stops. On Day 9 in Heraklion, the Church of Agios Titos, which had served as a mosque under Ottoman rule, became a church again in 1925, after the city's Muslims had left for Turkey; many of them settled on the Turkish Aegean coast, where Cretan dishes and Greek words survive in their grandchildren's kitchens. On Day 8, Rhodes was untouched, because in 1923 it belonged to Italy, not Greece, and the exchange applied only to Greece and Turkey. And here in Istanbul, the Greek Orthodox community was exempted, so in 1923 it still numbered more than a hundred thousand. Most of its members left in later decades, after the riots of September 1955 and the expulsions of Greek citizens in 1964, and today a few thousand remain. Chapter 6's multi-faith city emptied in two stages: the exchange took most of the Anatolian Greeks in 1922–1923, and later pressures took most of Istanbul's.

    The Turkish War of Independence¶

    The Turkish War of Independence (1919–1923) was the struggle of the Turkish nationalist movement, led by Mustafa Kemal, against the occupation and partition of Anatolia after World War I. It was larger than the war with Greece. On the eastern front, the nationalists fought the new Armenian republic in 1920. On the southern front, they fought the French in Cilicia, where the cities of Antep, Maraş, and Urfa later added honorific titles to their names, becoming Gaziantep ("Antep the Veteran"), Kahramanmaraş ("Heroic Maraş"), and Şanlıurfa ("Glorious Urfa"). On the western front they fought Greece. Meanwhile the sultan's government in occupied Istanbul still claimed authority, and for three years Turkey had two governments.

    The movement's aims were set out in the National Pact (Misak-ı Millî) of January 1920, which claimed for the nation the lands with an Ottoman Muslim majority that were still held by Ottoman forces when the armistice was signed in October 1918, demanded the security of Istanbul and the Sea of Marmara, and left the future of the Arab provinces, already occupied in 1918, to their inhabitants. In April 1920 a Grand National Assembly opened in Ankara. The sultan's government signed the Treaty of Sèvres on August 10, 1920, which would have left the Turks a small state in central and northern Anatolia, and the Assembly rejected it. After the victories of 1921 and 1922, the Allies negotiated again with the Ankara government, and the Treaty of Lausanne, signed on July 24, 1923, recognized an independent Turkey with roughly its present borders. Allied troops left Istanbul in October 1923.

    Part Four: Atatürk's Republic¶

    The Republic of Turkey¶

    The Republic of Turkey is the state proclaimed by the Grand National Assembly on October 29, 1923, with Mustafa Kemal as its first president and Ankara, made the capital about two weeks earlier, as its seat of government. The sultanate had already been abolished on November 1, 1922; the last sultan, Mehmed VI, left Istanbul on a British warship on November 17. In 1924 the Republic abolished the caliphate as well. The first census, in 1927, counted about 13.6 million people; today Turkey has about 85 million.

    For its first two decades the Republic was a one-party state, governed by Atatürk's Republican People's Party. Multi-party politics began after 1945, and in 1950 an election brought the opposition Democrat Party to power peacefully. The decades since have seen rapid growth, membership of NATO from 1952, and military interventions in 1960, 1971, and 1980, as well as a failed coup attempt in 2016. A referendum in 2017 changed the system from a parliamentary to a presidential one. The Republic celebrated its centenary in 2023.

    Worked example: the year as a history lesson. Let's read the Turkish calendar of national holidays, which tells the Republic's founding story in order.

    • April 23, National Sovereignty and Children's Day: the Grand National Assembly opened in Ankara on April 23, 1920. Atatürk dedicated the day to children, and children take over seats in parliament.
    • May 19, Commemoration of Atatürk, Youth and Sports Day: he landed at Samsun on May 19, 1919.
    • July 15, Democracy and National Unity Day: the failed coup attempt of 2016.
    • August 30, Victory Day: the decisive battle of 1922.
    • October 29, Republic Day: the proclamation of 1923.

    Add the two great religious festivals, the Ramazan Bayramı at the end of the fast and the Kurban Bayramı at the time of the pilgrimage, and the calendar holds both halves of modern Turkey, the Republic and the faith. November 10 is not a holiday, but, as we will see, it may be the most solemn day of all.

    Atatürk's Reforms¶

    Atatürk's reforms (in Turkish, Atatürk devrimleri) are the changes in law, religion, dress, language, and daily life that the Republic made between 1922 and 1938 to build a secular, Western-style nation-state. They were carried out from the top, quickly, by a government that tolerated little opposition. The main steps came in a rush.

    • 1924: the caliphate abolished on March 3; on the same day all schools, including the medreses of Chapter 5, were placed under the Ministry of Education, and the ministry of religious law and endowments was replaced by a Presidency of Religious Affairs. The religious courts were closed the next month.
    • 1925: the fez banned and the brimmed hat required for men; the dervish lodges closed.
    • 1926: the international calendar and clock adopted from January 1; a civil code based on Switzerland's ended polygamy and made civil marriage the law.
    • 1928: Islam removed as the state religion from the constitution; the new alphabet adopted.
    • 1930 and 1934: women given the vote in local and then national elections.
    • 1932: the call to prayer required in Turkish, as Chapter 5 described, until 1950.
    • 1934: surnames required by law.
    • 1935: Sunday, rather than Friday, made the weekly day of rest.

    Almost nothing in the clerk's day was left untouched, from his head to his watch to his name. That is why historians call the reforms a revolution, and why they are still argued about. To supporters they made Turkey modern, open, and equal for women. To critics they were imposed on a pious people without their consent, and they cut the country off from its past. Both views are held in Turkey today, often within one family.

    Secularism in Turkey¶

    Secularism in Turkey, called laiklik lah-eek-LEEK, from the French laïcité, is the constitutional principle, written into the constitution in 1937, that the state and its law are not based on religion. It works differently from the separation of church and state in the United States. Rather than staying out of religion, the Turkish state manages it. The Presidency of Religious Affairs (Diyanet), created in 1924, runs the country's mosques, employs their imams, and sends out a sermon for Friday prayers each week. For decades, laiklik also meant limits on religious expression in public life, such as a ban on the headscarf in universities and government offices, lifted in stages after 2010. How much the state should promote, restrain, or stay neutral about religion is one of the central questions of Turkish politics.

    A State That Runs the Mosques

    Notice the paradox: the Republic that made religion a private matter also put the mosques on the state payroll. Think of laiklik as the state holding the steering wheel of religion rather than leaving the car. Once we see that, the Diyanet's prayer-time app from Chapter 5 and the 1932 Turkish call to prayer make sense as the same idea.

    Faith in Modern Turkey¶

    Faith in modern Turkey describes how religion is practiced in today's secular republic. Nearly all Turks are Muslim by background, most of them Sunni, but practice ranges from daily prayer to none at all, and the streets of one Istanbul neighborhood can hold both. A large Alevi minority, estimated at anywhere from about a tenth to a fifth of the population, follows a distinct tradition and worships in a cemevi, a gathering house, rather than a mosque. Christians and Jews together number well under one percent. On a Sunday morning in Istanbul, the Divine Liturgy is sung at the Patriarchal Church of St. George in Fener, Mass is said at the Catholic Church of St. Anthony of Padua on İstiklal Avenue, and the noon call to prayer sounds over both, a small reminder of the multi-faith city of Chapter 6.

    Hagia Sophia as Mosque¶

    Hagia Sophia as mosque refers to the two periods in which the great church has served as a mosque: from 1453, when Mehmed II made it the empire's chief mosque, to 1934, and again since 2020. Chapter 4's table lays out its four lives, and the signs of the first mosque period are the ones we saw on Day 2: the minarets added over the following century or so, the mihrab turned toward Mecca, the minbar, and the great roundels. Its conversion to a museum in 1934, by a cabinet decision signed during the busiest years of the reforms, belonged to the same program as the alphabet and the hat. Today it is again a working mosque, with prayers five times a day, while foreign visitors pay to follow the gallery route.

    The Hagia Sophia Status Debate¶

    The Hagia Sophia status debate is the continuing argument over whether the building should be a mosque, a museum, or something else, and who should decide. Chapter 4 worked through it as a case of contested history: the dates are agreed, and their meaning is not. Seen from Day 5, the debate is also an argument about Atatürk's legacy. For many religious Turks, the 1934 museum was a wound imposed by a secular state, and its reversal in 2020 restored the conquest's mosque. For many secular Turks, the museum was the young Republic's gesture of openness to the world, and its reversal a step away from Atatürk's Turkey. For Orthodox Christians, it remains their mother church. We do not need to choose a side to understand why each side cares so much.

    Atatürk Memorial Day¶

    Atatürk Memorial Day is November 10, the anniversary of Atatürk's death in Dolmabahçe Palace at 9:05 AM in 1938, at the age of 57. At 9:05 AM every year, sirens sound across Turkey, and the country stops for a minute of silence: people stand still in the streets, drivers stop their cars on the highways and get out, and classes and offices fall silent. Crowds visit his mausoleum, Anıtkabir ah-nuht-kah-BEER, in Ankara, and the room where he died at Dolmabahçe. A clock in that room still shows 9:05. Because we arrived on November 11, we saw the city the day after, still hung with flags and his portrait.

    Respect Is the Law Here

    Atatürk's portrait hangs in shops, offices, and taxis, and Turkish law protects his memory: insulting it is a crime. A joke or a careless remark that would pass at home can give real offense here. Treat his image as we would a national flag, and if a conversation turns to him, listen and ask rather than judge.

    Part Five: Istanbul on a Sunday¶

    The Turkish Economy Today¶

    The Turkish economy today is one of the twenty largest in the world, an economy of about 85 million people that makes cars, appliances, textiles, and steel, grows much of the world's hazelnuts and dried apricots, and welcomes around 50 million foreign visitors a year. It has been in a customs union with the European Union since 1996, and much of what it makes is sold in Europe. Istanbul alone produces about 30 percent of the country's output. The weak point of recent years has been inflation: prices rose by more than 80 percent in the year to late 2022, and the lira has lost most of its value against the dollar since 2018, which, as Chapter 3 noted, is why prices on menus change so often.

    Worked example: living with high inflation. Let's see what high inflation means for the people we meet, using illustrative round numbers. Suppose a lunch costs 500 lira today and prices rise 50 percent a year. Next year the same lunch costs 750 lira, and the year after, 1,125: more than double in two years. At home, at 3 percent a year, a $20 lunch becomes $20.60 and then about $21.22. A salary that is raised once a year falls further behind every month, a lease agreed in lira loses value to the landlord, and savings kept in lira melt. That is why many Turkish families save in gold or in foreign currency, why rents and wages are renegotiated often, and why the price of a glass of tea is a topic of everyday conversation. For us as visitors, the lira's fall makes Turkey inexpensive; for the waiter, it can make life hard. A tip in lira, left in cash as Chapter 3 advised, matters more than its size suggests.

    Daily Life in Istanbul¶

    Daily life in Istanbul is the routine of a city of about 15.7 million people, nearly a fifth of Turkey's population, stretched along both shores of the Bosphorus. Millions of people commute every day between Europe and Asia, by ferry, by three bridges, by a road tunnel, and by the Marmaray railway tunnel under the strait, opened in 2013. Sunday is the day for a long serpme kahvaltı, a "spread" breakfast of many small dishes, for walks along the Bosphorus at Ortaköy and Bebek, and for football, where the city's three great clubs, Galatasaray, Fenerbahçe, and Beşiktaş, divide families and neighborhoods. The city's street cats are fed and sheltered by whole streets. Under the routine lies a worry everyone shares: Istanbul sits near a major fault, and since the earthquake of 1999 east of the city, the question of whether a building is earthquake-safe is part of choosing where to live.

    One Card for the Whole City

    Here's a shortcut for our last day: buy an İstanbulkart from a machine at any tram or ferry stop, load it with lira, and use it for trams, ferries, the metro, and the funicular. One card can pay for two people; just tap it once for each of us. A Sunday ferry to Üsküdar and back costs less than a coffee and gives the best view of the city we are leaving.

    Learning for All: A New Alphabet and a Nation at School¶

    On the evening of August 9, 1928, at a party gathering in Gülhane Park, at Sarayburnu below the walls of Topkapı, Mustafa Kemal stood up and announced that the Turkish nation would write with new letters. Within five months the new alphabet was the law, the newspapers were printed in it, and adults across the country were sitting in classrooms to learn it. Chapter 6 ended with Müteferrika's books unsold because too few people could read them, and with the promise that the circle would finally widen. This is that story, together with what it cost, and how uncertain the numbers behind it are.

    The Alphabet Reform of 1928¶

    The alphabet reform of 1928 replaced the Arabic script, which had been used to write Turkish for most of a thousand years, with a Latin-based alphabet of 29 letters designed to fit Turkish sounds. A commission of scholars and writers prepared it over the summer of 1928. After the announcement in Gülhane, Mustafa Kemal toured the provinces with a portable blackboard, and photographs show him chalking letters in front of crowds. The Grand National Assembly passed the law on November 1, 1928. Newspapers switched to the new letters from December 1, government offices in the following months, and printing books in the Arabic script ended.

    The new alphabet keeps the letters English readers know, drops q, w, and x, and adds six letters for Turkish sounds. It has one letter for each sound, and every letter is always pronounced the same way, which is what makes it easy to learn. The table shows the letters that differ from English.

    Letter Sounds like Example Meaning
    c j in jam cami (jah-MEE) mosque
    ç ch in church çay (chai) tea
    ş sh in ship şehir (sheh-HEER) city
    ğ lengthens the vowel before it; not pronounced on its own dağ (daah) mountain
    ı (no dot) a short, unrounded "uh" kapı (kah-PUH) gate
    i, İ (dotted) ee in see İstanbul Istanbul
    ö like German ö, the vowel of her with rounded lips gör see!
    ü like German ü, ee with rounded lips gül rose

    Worked example: five words, two scripts, revisited. Let's go back to Chapter 6's five words, which shared two spellings in the Ottoman script. In the new alphabet they are kol (arm), kul (servant), kör (blind), gör (see!), and gür (abundant): five different spellings for five different words. The eight vowels, a, e, ı, i, o, ö, u, ü, each have their own letter, and k and g are separate letters, so nothing is left for the reader to guess. Now try two signs we pass today: Giriş (gee-REESH) on the way into Dolmabahçe means "entrance," and Çıkış (chuh-KUHSH) on the way out means "exit." A beginner who knows the eight letters in the table can sound out any Turkish word on the first day, which no beginner in the Ottoman script could do. That is what the reformers meant to give the nation: a script whose first step takes weeks, not years.

    The reform had real costs, and they were felt for generations. Within one generation, most Turks could no longer read their grandparents' letters, the inscriptions on Ottoman fountains and gravestones, or the millions of books and documents written before 1928. The poem carved on the Fountain of Ahmed III, just outside the Imperial Gate of Topkapı that we walked through on Day 3, is unreadable to most Istanbulites today. A campaign from 1932 onward to replace Arabic and Persian words with Turkish ones widened the gap, so that even an Ottoman text printed in the new letters can be hard to understand. And the reform was imposed from the top by a one-party government, without a vote of the people, and the Arabic script was banned from public use. In recent years Ottoman Turkish has returned as a school subject in some Turkish schools, and how much of it every Turk should learn is itself part of today's politics.

    The Nation's Schools¶

    The Nation's Schools (Millet Mektepleri mil-LET mek-tep-leh-REE) were the free adult literacy classes opened across Turkey on January 1, 1929, to teach the new alphabet to the whole adult population. They met in the evenings in school buildings, coffeehouses, and village rooms, and soldiers learned in the army. Adults who could not read, roughly those between 16 and 45, were expected to attend a few months of classes, and they received a certificate when they passed. Mustafa Kemal was named the schools' Head Teacher (Başöğretmen) on November 24, 1928, the day Turkey still celebrates as Teachers' Day. By the government's own count, more than a million adults earned certificates in the schools' first years. In the 1930s their work passed to the People's Houses, community centers opened in 1932, and from 1940 the Village Institutes trained teachers from village families to go home and teach.

    Run Chapter 2's four-step pattern on the reform, and it fits closely.

    1. Locked up: reading Turkish in the Arabic script took years of schooling, and most people never had them.
    2. Opened: a script that could be learned in weeks, with free classes for every adult.
    3. Resisted: the scholars, calligraphers, and religious teachers whose learning lived in the old script, and many ordinary pious people who saw the Arabic letters as the letters of the Quran, though under a one-party government resistance had little room.
    4. Never all the way: the classes reached towns faster than villages and men faster than women, many adults who passed lost the skill without books to practice on, and the generation that learned the new letters lost the old ones.

    Literacy Rate Uncertainty¶

    Literacy rate uncertainty is the margin of doubt in any count of how many people could read, which comes from how literacy was defined, who was counted, and who did the counting. Chapter 2 gave Turkey's literacy before the reform as roughly one person in ten. That figure comes from the census of 1927, the year before the reform, which is usually quoted as about 10.6 percent, far lower among women and in villages. The census of 1935 found about one person in five, and today well over 95 percent of Turkish adults can read. The direction of the change is not in doubt. The exact starting point is less certain than the neat number suggests.

    Worked example: one village, three literacy rates. Let's take an illustrative village of 1,000 people in 1927 and count its readers three ways. Of the 1,000, 300 are children under seven. Of the 700 others, 90 can read and write Ottoman Turkish, 40 more learned to recite the Quran from its Arabic letters but cannot read a letter in Turkish, and 10 read only Greek. Counted over the whole population, with only the first group as literate, the rate is 90 ÷ 1,000 = 9.0 percent. Counted over people aged seven and over, it is 90 ÷ 700 ≈ 12.9 percent. Counting everyone aged seven and over who could read any script, it is 140 ÷ 700 = 20.0 percent. The same village gives 9, 13, or 20 percent depending on three choices that a census report rarely explains. The real census asked people to report their own ability, with no test, which adds a fourth uncertainty.

    The table lists the questions to ask of any historical literacy figure, with the answers for Turkey's 1927 count.

    Question Why it matters Turkey, 1927
    What counted as literate? Reading only, or reading and writing; reciting or understanding Self-reported reading and writing, with no test
    Which script or language? Readers of other scripts may be left out Mostly the Arabic script; most Greek and Armenian readers were already gone
    Out of whom? All ages, or adults only, changes the rate a great deal Quoted figures do not always say whether young children are included
    Who counted, and why? A new government has reasons to stress how much it improved The Republic's new statistics office, a year before the reform
    What is it compared with? Earlier estimates may rest on weaker evidence still Ottoman-era estimates rest largely on school records and travelers' impressions

    None of this means the reform did not work. It means that "one in ten" is a census answer to a particular question, asked by a government with a stake in the result, and that historians who argue Ottoman reading was somewhat more common in the cities are not being contrary. Chapter 4's primary-source test applies to numbers as much as to stories.

    The alphabet reform holds three lessons for AI in education. The first is the lesson of Chapter 2: lowering the cost of the first step widens the circle. A script that could be learned in weeks did more for Turkish literacy than three centuries of presses, and an AI tutor that speaks to each learner in plain language and at their own pace could be that kind of simpler interface to knowledge. The second is a warning about how change is made. The reform moved a whole nation in a few years because a one-party state commanded it, and it left a generation cut off from its own written past. Every large change carries losses as well as gains, which AI-Native Schools discusses through William Bridges' idea of endings in Leading Change: Vision, Culture, and Early-Stage Concerns, and national mandates, as its chapter Sustaining Change: Systems Thinking and National Adoption shows with a recent AI textbook rollout, can provoke the resistance they try to skip.

    The third lesson is about measurement: a claim that a new tool raised learning deserves the same questions as the 1927 census, from what was counted to who did the counting. There is also a hopeful twist. Researchers are now training text-recognition systems on Ottoman script, so the technology of our own time may reopen the archives that the reform of 1928 closed.

    Key Takeaways¶

    • Ottoman decline was a two-century retreat from Vienna (1683) to 1922, driven by military defeat, debt, and nationalism, and met by real reform; Dolmabahçe Palace (1843–1856) and the debt default of 1875 tell the story together.
    • Ottoman rule in Greece lasted from the late 14th century to 1912, organized through the Orthodox millet; the "secret school" is probably a legend.
    • The Greek War of Independence (1821–1830) began in the Peloponnese, cost the Patriarch Gregory V his life at the gate in Fener that is still closed, and was won at Navarino in 1827.
    • The modern Greek state began as a small kingdom in 1832 and grew for 115 years: Athens and Delphi in 1832, Heraklion in 1913, Rhodes in 1947.
    • The Dardanelles Strait, about 61 km long and narrowest at Çanakkale, is crossed tonight on our way to Day 6.
    • World War I drew the empire in through the Dardanelles in 1914 and ended it; the Armenians suffered deportation and mass death in 1915.
    • The Gallipoli Campaign of 1915 made the name of Mustafa Kemal Atatürk (1881–1938), born in Salonica, who led the national movement from 1919.
    • The Greco-Turkish War (1919–1922) ended with the burning of Smyrna and the population exchange of 1923, decided by religion, not language.
    • The Turkish War of Independence (1919–1923) reversed the Treaty of Sèvres wherever the National Pact made a claim, and the Treaty of Lausanne (1923) recognized modern Turkey.
    • The Republic of Turkey was proclaimed on October 29, 1923. Atatürk's reforms changed law, dress, the calendar, names, and the alphabet in fifteen years.
    • Secularism in Turkey (laiklik) means the state manages religion through the Diyanet; faith in modern Turkey ranges from devout Sunni and Alevi practice to none.
    • Hagia Sophia as a mosque (1453–1934 and since 2020) and the Hagia Sophia status debate are also an argument about Atatürk's legacy.
    • Atatürk Memorial Day, November 10, stops the country at 9:05 AM.
    • The Turkish economy today is large and industrial but has lived with high inflation; daily life in Istanbul runs on ferries, breakfasts, and football for 15.7 million people.
    • The alphabet reform of 1928 gave Turkish 29 letters, one per sound, and the Nation's Schools taught adults to read them, at the cost of cutting a generation off from the old script.
    • Literacy rate uncertainty reminds us that "one in ten" depends on what was counted, out of whom, and by whom.
