    Quiz: Day 5: The End of Empire and the Birth of Modern Turkey¶

    Test your understanding of the Ottoman Empire's last century, the wars that made modern Greece and Turkey, and the 1928 alphabet reform with these review questions.

    1. Why do many historians now prefer "transformation under pressure" to "Ottoman decline"?¶

    1. The empire lost very little territory between the siege of Vienna in 1683 and 1922
    2. The empire was never defeated in war by Austria or Russia
    3. The phrase "sick man of Europe" was invented by Turkish historians in the 20th century
    4. A state that lasted 240 years after Vienna, reformed its army, law, and schools, and held at Gallipoli was not simply dying
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is D. The losses were real: Hungary in 1699, Crimea in 1783, and province after province to nationalism and debt. But an empire that kept going for 240 years after Vienna, reformed its army, law, and schools, and fought the Great Powers to a standstill at Gallipoli was not simply dying, so "transformation under pressure" fits better. Options A and B contradict the military defeats the chapter lists. The "sick man" phrase is attributed to the Russian tsar Nicholas I in 1853.

    2. On Day 8 in Rhodes you meet an older resident whose grandparents went to Italian schools, while on Day 9 a Cretan mentions great-grandparents who grew up under the sultan. What explains the difference?¶

    1. Greek stops joined the modern Greek state at different times: Crete in 1913 and Rhodes only in 1947, after Italian rule
    2. Rhodes was never part of the Ottoman Empire, while Crete was Ottoman until 1947
    3. Both islands joined Greece in 1832, but Italy ran Rhodes's schools by treaty
    4. Crete joined Greece in 1947, while Rhodes joined in 1913 after a short Italian visit
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is A. The modern Greek state grew over 115 years. Athens and Delphi lay inside the first kingdom of 1832. Heraklion became Greek in 1913, so Cretans may have great-grandparents who grew up under the sultan or in the autonomous Cretan State. Rhodes became Greek only in 1947, after 35 years of Italian rule, so its older residents may have grandparents with Italian schooling. Rhodes was Ottoman from 1522, which rules out option B, and option D reverses the dates.

    3. How did Mustafa Kemal first become a name the Ottoman public knew?¶

    1. As governor of Salonica, where he was born, during the Balkan Wars
    2. As the officer who negotiated the Treaty of Sèvres in 1920
    3. As commander of the 19th Division at Gallipoli in 1915, where he stopped the Anzac advance on the heights
    4. As the leader of the Greek army's defeat at the Battle of Navarino in 1827
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is C. On April 25, 1915, Mustafa Kemal commanded the 19th Division at Gallipoli. When the Anzacs came ashore below the heights, he led his men onto the ridge in time to stop their advance, at great cost, and he held the line again in August. Gallipoli made him a colonel and a public hero. The sultan's government signed Sèvres, which his Ankara Assembly rejected, and Navarino came decades before his birth in 1881.

    4. Under the 1923 population exchange, a Turkish-speaking Orthodox Christian family lived in Cappadocia. What happened to them, and why?¶

    1. They stayed in Turkey, because they spoke Turkish
    2. They were sent to Greece, because the exchange sorted people by religion, not language
    3. They could choose either country, because the exchange was voluntary
    4. They moved to Istanbul, because all Orthodox Christians were exempted there
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is B. The Lausanne convention of January 1923 required the compulsory exchange of Greek Orthodox Christians in Turkey for Muslims in Greece, and none could return. Its rule was religion, not language: Turkish-speaking Orthodox Christians from Cappadocia went to Greece, and Greek-speaking Muslims from Crete went to Turkey. The exchange was compulsory, not voluntary. The Istanbul exemption covered only Greek Orthodox who had settled there before October 30, 1918, not anyone who moved there.

    5. The Treaty of Sèvres took away the Arab provinces and gave Eastern Thrace to Greece. Using the National Pact, what should you predict the Treaty of Lausanne did with these two terms?¶

    1. It reversed the Thrace term, which lay inside the Pact's claim, and kept the Arab term, which the Pact had left out
    2. It kept both terms, because Lausanne simply confirmed Sèvres
    3. It reversed both terms, returning the Arab provinces and Eastern Thrace to Turkey
    4. It kept the Thrace term and reversed the Arab term, because Greece had won the war
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is A. The National Pact of 1920 claimed lands with an Ottoman Muslim majority still held by Ottoman forces at the 1918 armistice, and left the Arab provinces to their inhabitants. Eastern Thrace was inside the Pact, and the nationalists won the war, so Lausanne reversed that term and drew the border on the Maritsa River. The Arab provinces were outside the Pact, so Lausanne kept that term. One question, "was it inside the Pact?", predicts most of the treaty. Turkey, not Greece, won.

    6. How does secularism in Turkey (laiklik) differ from the separation of church and state in the United States?¶

    1. Turkey bans all public religious practice, including the call to prayer
    2. Turkey's constitution makes Sunni Islam the official religion of the state
    3. Turkey leaves religion entirely to private communities, with no government role
    4. The Turkish state manages religion, running the mosques and employing their imams through the Diyanet
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is D. Laiklik, written into the constitution in 1937, means the state and its law are not based on religion. But rather than staying out of religion, as in the United States, the Turkish state manages it: the Presidency of Religious Affairs (Diyanet), created in 1924, runs the mosques, employs their imams, and sends out a weekly Friday sermon. The chapter compares it to the state holding the steering wheel of religion. Option C describes the American model; options A and B are not Turkish law.

    7. What happens across Turkey every November 10 at 9:05 AM?¶

    1. Fireworks mark the anniversary of the Republic's proclamation
    2. Sirens sound and the country stops for a minute of silence on the anniversary of Atatürk's death
    3. Mosques give a special call to prayer for the soldiers of Gallipoli
    4. Schools hold Teachers' Day ceremonies in honor of the Head Teacher
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is B. Atatürk died in Dolmabahçe Palace at 9:05 AM on November 10, 1938. Every year at that moment sirens sound, and the whole country stops for a minute of silence: people stand still, drivers get out of their cars, and classes fall silent. The Republic was proclaimed on October 29, 1923 (option A). Teachers' Day (option D) falls on November 24, the day Mustafa Kemal was named Head Teacher of the Nation's Schools.

    8. Weighing the gains and costs of the alphabet reform of 1928, which judgment fits the chapter best?¶

    1. It was a gain with no real cost, since the old script was used by almost no one
    2. It failed, because adults could not learn a new script and literacy stayed at one in ten
    3. It made reading learnable in weeks, but it was imposed from the top and cut a generation off from its written past
    4. It was chosen by a national vote, so the losses were accepted freely by the people
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is C. The new 29-letter alphabet had one letter for each sound, so a beginner could sound out any Turkish word within weeks, and literacy rose from about one in ten in 1927 to one in five by 1935. But a one-party government imposed it without a popular vote and banned the Arabic script from public use. Within a generation, most Turks could not read their grandparents' letters or the inscriptions on Ottoman fountains. Both the gain and the cost are real.

    9. In the chapter's illustrative village of 1,000 people in 1927, the same readers produce literacy rates of 9, 13, or 20 percent. What does this show?¶

    1. The census takers made arithmetic mistakes that later historians corrected
    2. Villages always have lower literacy than cities, whatever method is used
    3. Only the lowest figure is honest, because it counts the whole population
    4. A literacy rate depends on what counts as reading, who is in the denominator, and who did the counting
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is D. Counting only readers of Ottoman Turkish over the whole population gives 9.0 percent; counting them over people aged seven and over gives about 12.9 percent; counting anyone seven or older who could read any script gives 20.0 percent. The real 1927 census also relied on self-reports with no test. So "one in ten" answers a particular question, asked by a government with a stake in the result. No method is the only honest one (option C); each answers a different question.

    10. What were the Nation's Schools (Millet Mektepleri)?¶

    1. Free evening classes, opened in 1929, that taught adults across Turkey to read the new alphabet
    2. Religious schools attached to mosques that taught children the Quran in Arabic
    3. Private academies in Istanbul that trained teachers for the Ottoman court
    4. Schools run by each millet for its own Greek, Armenian, or Jewish children
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is A. The Nation's Schools opened on January 1, 1929, as free adult literacy classes meeting in the evenings in schools, coffeehouses, and village rooms, while soldiers learned in the army. Adults roughly 16 to 45 attended a few months of classes and received certificates. Mustafa Kemal was named Head Teacher, and by the government's count more than a million adults earned certificates in the first years. Despite the similar name, they had nothing to do with the millet communities' own schools in option D.
