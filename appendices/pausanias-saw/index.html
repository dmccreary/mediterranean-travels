    Pausanias Saw¶

    Pausanias was a Greek travel writer of the 2nd century CE, probably from Lydia in western Asia Minor. Between about 150 and 175 CE he wrote the Description of Greece, ten books that walk the mainland site by site: what stood at each temple, who dedicated each statue, and which story the locals told about it. He wrote for travelers, and he wrote about five hundred years after the Parthenon was built and about seventeen hundred years before archaeologists began to dig. The excavators at Olympia and Delphi used his book as a field guide to name the ruins they found.

    This appendix sets his words beside our own trip. For each place we visited in Greece, it quotes what Pausanias saw and then says what is there now. The gap between the two is a record of earthquakes, plunder, conversion, and the lime kilns that burned marble into mortar. The chapters already cite Pausanias as a source; here he speaks for himself.

    Two Men Named Pausanias

    Watch out for the Spartan regent Pausanias, who commanded the Greeks at Plataea in 479 BCE and had his own name carved on the Serpent Column. He lived about six hundred years before the travel writer. In this appendix "Pausanias" always means the travel writer, who tells us what happened to the Spartan's tripod.

    How We Quote Him¶

    Pausanias wrote in Greek. We quote two English translations that are both in the public domain:

    • W. H. S. Jones (Loeb Classical Library, 1918), for Book 1 on Athens and Attica, and Jones's 1926 volume for two passages from Books 3 and 4.
    • J. G. Frazer (1898), for Book 10 on Delphi and a passage from Book 9. Frazer's spelling is British ("honour", "theatre"), and we keep it.

    References such as 1.22.4 mean book 1, chapter 22, section 4. These are the standard numbers, so they work in any translation. Each section heading links to the Greek and English text in the Perseus Digital Library.

    Pausanias names things by their ancient names, and some of them differ from the names we use. He calls the Propylaea simply "the gateway," the Temple of Athena Nike the "temple of Wingless Victory," the Stoa Poikile "the portico which they call painted," and the Areopagus "the Hill of Ares."

    Cape Sounion¶

    Chapter 15. The first lines of the whole book describe the view from a ship coming round the tip of Attica, as ours did on Day 10.

    When you have rounded the promontory you see a harbor and a temple to Athena of Sunium on the peak of the promontory.

    — Pausanias 1.1.1, trans. Jones

    What we saw. The temple on the peak is the marble temple of Poseidon, built about 444 to 440 BCE. Athena's smaller sanctuary sits on a lower hill just to the northeast. Either Pausanias, looking up from the deck, gave the temple on the summit to the wrong god, or his text names the wrong one. It is a useful warning that even the best ancient guide can be wrong about what is in front of him.

    From the same cape, sailors could see something we cannot: the spear of Athena on the Acropolis, about 70 km away by road.

    The point of the spear of this Athena and the crest of her helmet are visible to those sailing to Athens, as soon as Sunium is passed.

    — Pausanias 1.28.2, trans. Jones

    He is describing Phidias's bronze Athena Promachos, which he says was paid for from the spoils of Marathon. Nothing of it survives but the foundations of its base, behind the Propylaea.

    Piraeus¶

    Chapter 15. We docked where Pausanias begins his walk into Athens.

    But when Themistocles became archon, since he thought that the Peiraeus was more conveniently situated for mariners, and had three harbors as against one at Phalerum, he made it the Athenian port. Even up to my time there were docks there, and near the largest harbor is the grave of Themistocles.

    — Pausanias 1.1.2, trans. Jones

    What we saw. Piraeus is still the port of Athens and one of the busiest passenger ports in Europe. The three harbors Themistocles chose are still in use, now filled with cruise ships, ferries, and yachts.

    The Acropolis¶

    The Gateway and the Temple of Wingless Victory¶

    Chapter 15.

    There is but one entry to the Acropolis. It affords no other, being precipitous throughout and having a strong wall. The gateway has a roof of white marble, and down to the present day it is unrivalled for the beauty and size of its stones. ... On the right of the gateway is a temple of Wingless Victory. From this point the sea is visible, and here it was that, according to legend, Aegeus threw himself down to his death.

    — Pausanias 1.22.4, trans. Jones

    He explains the name in his book on Sparta, where he saw a statue of the war god Enyalius in chains:

    The idea the Lacedaemonians express by this image is the same as the Athenians express by their Wingless Victory; the former think that Enyalius will never run away from them, being bound in the fetters, while the Athenians think that Victory, having no wings, will always remain where she is.

    — Pausanias 3.15.7, trans. Jones

    What we saw. There is still only one way up, at the west end. The marble ceiling of the gateway that Pausanias admired is gone. The Ottomans took the temple of Athena Nike apart in 1686 to build a gun battery, and it has since been reassembled from its own blocks. The statue that could not fly away is lost.

    The wing to the left of the gateway, which Pausanias calls "a building with pictures" (1.22.6), was a picture gallery. He lists paintings of the Trojan War there. None survive; the room is bare.

    The Parthenon¶

    Chapter 15.

    As you enter the temple that they name the Parthenon, all the sculptures you see on what is called the pediment refer to the birth of Athena, those on the rear pediment represent the contest for the land between Athena and Poseidon. The statue itself is made of ivory and gold.

    — Pausanias 1.24.5, trans. Jones

    The statue of Athena is upright, with a tunic reaching to the feet, and on her breast the head of Medusa is worked in ivory. She holds a statue of Victory about four cubits high, and in the other hand a spear; at her feet lies a shield and near the spear is a serpent.

    — Pausanias 1.24.7, trans. Jones

    What we saw. Both pediments are almost empty. What survives of their figures is divided between the Acropolis Museum and the British Museum, as Chapter 15 explains. The gold-and-ivory Athena Parthenos was lost in late antiquity; we know her shape from Pausanias, from small Roman copies, and from coins. Notice what Pausanias does not describe: the frieze and the metopes. He gives a building that is now the most studied in Greece three sentences before moving on to its statue.

    The Erechtheion¶

    Chapter 15.

    On the walls are paintings representing members of the clan Butadae; there is also inside – the building is double – sea-water in a cistern. ... But this cistern is remarkable for the noise of waves it sends forth when a south wind blows. On the rock is the outline of a trident. Legend says that these appeared as evidence in support of Poseidon's claim to the land.

    — Pausanias 1.26.5, trans. Jones

    About the olive they have nothing to say except that it was testimony the goddess produced when she contended for their land. Legend also says that when the Persians fired Athens the olive was burnt down, but on the very day it was burnt it grew again to the height of two cubits.

    — Pausanias 1.27.2, trans. Jones

    He also describes the ancient wooden statue of Athena "which fell from heaven," and a golden lamp by Callimachus that burned day and night on one filling of oil a year (1.26.6–7).

    What we saw. The marks in the rock under the north porch are still shown as Poseidon's trident. An olive tree grows beside the building again, a modern planting in the old place. The statue and the lamp are gone. The most famous feature of the building today, the Porch of the Caryatids, is one Pausanias never mentions.

    The Theater of Dionysus¶

    Chapter 15.

    In the theater the Athenians have portrait statues of poets, both tragic and comic, but they are mostly of undistinguished persons. With the exception of Menander no poet of comedy represented here won a reputation, but tragedy has two illustrious representatives, Euripides and Sophocles.

    — Pausanias 1.21.1, trans. Jones

    What we saw. The stone seats and the marble front-row thrones remain on the south slope. The portrait statues are gone. Pausanias's judgment that most of the poets were "undistinguished persons" is one of the rare places where he sounds like a critic.

    The Agora and the Areopagus¶

    The Royal Stoa and the Council House¶

    Chapter 16.

    First on the right is what is called the Royal Portico, where sits the king when holding the yearly office called the kingship.

    — Pausanias 1.3.1, trans. Jones

    Hard by is the council chamber of those called the Five Hundred, who are the Athenian councillors for a year.

    — Pausanias 1.3.5, trans. Jones

    Near to the Council Chamber of the Five Hundred is what is called Tholos (Round House); here the presidents sacrifice, and there are a few small statues made of silver.

    — Pausanias 1.5.1, trans. Jones

    What we saw. All three are low foundations along the west side of the Agora. Pausanias's order of buildings helped the American excavators identify them. In his day the Council of 500 still met, though Athens had long been a Roman provincial city and the council no longer governed anything that mattered.

    The Temple of Hephaestus¶

    Chapter 16.

    Above the Cerameicus and the portico called the King's Portico is a temple of Hephaestus. I was not surprised that by it stands a statue of Athena, because I knew the story about Erichthonius. But when I saw that the statue of Athena had blue eyes I found out that the legend about them is Libyan.

    — Pausanias 1.14.6, trans. Jones

    What we saw. This is the best-preserved temple of the classical Greek world, still roofed because it served as a church for centuries. For a long time it was wrongly called the Theseion, the sanctuary of Theseus, which Pausanias places elsewhere, near a gymnasium (1.17.2). The old name survives in the neighborhood and the metro station, Thiseio. His note on its location, "above the King's Portico," fits the temple on the hill exactly.

    The Painted Stoa¶

    Chapter 16.

    At the end of the painting are those who fought at Marathon; the Boeotians of Plataea and the Attic contingent are coming to blows with the foreigners. In this place neither side has the better, but the center of the fighting shows the foreigners in flight and pushing one another into the morass, while at the end of the painting are the Phoenician ships, and the Greeks killing the foreigners who are scrambling into them.

    — Pausanias 1.15.3, trans. Jones

    What we saw. Only the foundations of the stoa survive, on the north side of the Agora beyond the metro tracks. The paintings are lost entirely, and Pausanias's description is the fullest account of them we have. This is the porch where Zeno taught, and where the Stoics got their name.

    The Hill of Ares and the Unknown Gods¶

    Chapter 16.

    There is also the Hill of Ares, so named because Ares was the first to be tried here ... The unhewn stones on which stand the defendants and the prosecutors, they call the stone of Outrage and the stone of Ruthlessness.

    — Pausanias 1.28.5, trans. Jones

    At Phalerum, the old harbor, he lists:

    altars of the gods named Unknown, and of heroes, and of the children of Theseus and Phalerus

    — Pausanias 1.1.4, trans. Jones

    What we saw. The Areopagus is bare, slippery limestone, with a bronze plaque of Paul's speech at its foot. Pausanias wrote about a century after Paul spoke here and never mentions him. His "gods named Unknown," in the plural, are one of the two ancient witnesses Chapter 16 uses to test the altar Paul describes in Acts.

    The Temple of Olympian Zeus and the Lyceum¶

    Chapter 20.

    Before the entrance to the sanctuary of Olympian Zeus – Hadrian the Roman emperor dedicated the temple and the statue, one worth seeing, which in size exceeds all other statues save the colossi at Rhodes and Rome, and is made of ivory and gold with an artistic skill which is remarkable when the size is taken into account – before the entrance, I say, stand statues of Hadrian, two of Thasian stone, two of Egyptian.

    — Pausanias 1.18.6, trans. Jones

    The Lyceum has its name from Lycus, the son of Pandion, but it was considered sacred to Apollo from the beginning down to my time, and here was the god first named Lyceus.

    — Pausanias 1.19.3, trans. Jones

    What we saw. Pausanias saw the Olympieion new: Hadrian had finished it only about twenty-five years earlier. Of its 104 columns, 15 stand and a sixteenth lies where a storm blew it down in 1852. The colossal statue and the precinct "full of statues" of Hadrian are gone. At the Lyceum, Pausanias mentions the god and the name but not Aristotle, whose school made the place famous. What survives is a grid of low foundation walls off Rigillis Street, found in 1996.

    Delphi¶

    The Temple of Athena Forethought¶

    Chapter 18. On the road in from the east, Pausanias reached Athena's sanctuary, the one we call Athena Pronaia, first.

    On entering the city you come to a row of temples. The first of them was in ruins, and the next was empty both of images and statues. The third contained portrait statues of a few Roman emperors; and the fourth is called the temple of Forethought Athena.

    — Pausanias 10.8.6, trans. Frazer

    What we saw. The terrace of the Marmaria, with the foundations of two temples of Athena and two treasuries, and in the middle the round Tholos. Pausanias describes a row of temples, one already in ruins in his day, but never mentions a round building. That silence is one reason nobody knows what the Tholos was for.

    The Castalian Spring¶

    Chapter 18.

    On the way up from the gymnasium to the sanctuary you have on the right of the road the water of Castaly, and it is sweet to drink.

    — Pausanias 10.8.9, trans. Frazer

    What we saw. The spring still runs between the two cliffs, and the two fountain houses survive, though the upper one is often fenced off because of falling rock. Pausanias reports three different stories about where the spring's name came from and does not choose among them.

    Up the Sacred Way¶

    Chapter 18.

    The city of Delphi stands wholly on a slope, and not only the city, but also the sacred close of Apollo. The close is very spacious, and is situated at the highest part of the city. There are passages through it at short intervals. I will mention what seemed to me the most noteworthy of the votive offerings.

    — Pausanias 10.9.1, trans. Frazer

    On entering the precinct you see a bronze bull made by Theopropus, an Aeginetan, and dedicated by the Corcyraeans.

    — Pausanias 10.9.3, trans. Frazer

    Just past the bull, the Spartans answered Athens in bronze:

    Opposite them are offerings of the Lacedaemonians from booty taken from the Athenians: they consist of images of the Dioscuri, Zeus, Apollo, and Artemis; also Poseidon crowning Lysander, son of Aristocritus, and a statue of Agias, who acted as soothsayer to Lysander, and a statue of Hermon who steered Lysander's flag-ship.

    — Pausanias 10.9.7, trans. Frazer

    What we saw. Empty statue bases along a polished stone path. Of the hundreds of bronzes Pausanias walked between, almost none survive. He also tells us where many of them went:

    It was destined, too, not to escape the all-comprehensive disdain of Nero, who robbed Apollo of five hundred bronze statues of gods and men together.

    — Pausanias 10.7.1, trans. Frazer

    The Athenian Treasury¶

    Chapter 18.

    The treasury of the Thebans was built with the spoils of war, and so was the treasury of the Athenians. The Theban treasury was built with the spoils of the battle of Leuctra, the Athenian treasury with the spoils taken from the army which landed at Marathon under the command of Datis.

    — Pausanias 10.11.5, trans. Frazer

    What we saw. The only treasury at Delphi still standing, rebuilt by the French excavators in 1903–1906 from its original blocks. Chapter 18's worked example tests his Marathon claim and gives the honest verdict "possibly."

    The Serpent Column¶

    Chapters 4 and 18.

    From the spoils of the battle of Plataea the Greeks dedicated a national offering in the shape of a golden tripod resting on a bronze serpent. The bronze part of the offering is preserved to this day; but the Phocian captains did not leave the gold in quite the same condition.

    — Pausanias 10.13.9, trans. Frazer

    What we saw. Only the base remains at Delphi, with a modern bronze copy of the column beside it. Constantine moved the bronze serpents to his new capital, and we saw the twisted trunk in Istanbul's Hippodrome on Day 2. Pausanias saw the bronze still standing at Delphi, about a century and a half before it left.

    The Omphalos¶

    Chapter 18.

    What the Delphians call the Navel (omphalos) is made of white marble, and is said by them to be at the centre of the whole earth, and Pindar in one of his odes agrees with them.

    — Pausanias 10.16.3, trans. Frazer

    Near the temple he saw a second, plainer stone with a ritual of its own:

    On this stone they pour oil every day, and at every festival they put unspun wool on it. There is also a notion that this stone was given to Cronus instead of the child, and that Cronus spewed it out again.

    — Pausanias 10.24.6, trans. Frazer

    What we saw. A beehive-shaped stone beside the Sacred Way, and a marble cone carved with a net of woolen bands in the Delphi museum. Both are copies, and neither can be shown to be a stone Pausanias saw. The woolen net carved on the museum's stone recalls the wool he says was laid on the stone at festivals.

    The Temple of Apollo¶

    Chapters 17 and 18. Pausanias tells the history of the temple as the Delphians told it to him, starting with legends and ending with the building in front of him:

    They say that the most ancient temple of Apollo was made of laurel, and that the boughs were brought from the laurel in Tempe. ... The Delphians say that the second temple was made by bees out of wax and feathers, and that it was sent to the Hyperboreans by Apollo.

    — Pausanias 10.5.9, trans. Frazer

    The fourth temple was built by Trophonius and Agamedes, and tradition says that it was made of stone. But it was burnt down when Erxiclides was archon at Athens, in the first year of the fifty-eighth Olympiad ... The present temple was built for the god by the Amphictyons out of the sacred treasures: the architect was Spintharus of Corinth.

    — Pausanias 10.5.13, trans. Frazer

    The sculptures in the gables represent Artemis, Latona, Apollo, the Muses, the setting of the Sun, and Dionysus with the Thyiad women. ... On the architrave are golden shields: some of them were dedicated by the Athenians from the spoils of the battle of Marathon.

    — Pausanias 10.19.4, trans. Frazer

    These men, then, came to Delphi and dedicated to Apollo the famous maxims 'Know thyself,' and 'Nothing in excess.'

    — Pausanias 10.24.1, trans. Frazer

    What we saw. The first year of the 58th Olympiad is 548 BCE, the date Chapter 18 gives for the fire. What stands now is the platform, the ramp, and six re-erected columns of the temple finished around 330 BCE. The gables and the golden shields are gone, and the carved maxims have not survived. Pausanias moves easily from a temple of feathers to a temple with a named architect without telling his reader where legend ends. Chapter 4's source test was built for passages like this one.

    The Theater and the Stadium¶

    Chapter 18.

    Abutting on the sacred close is a theatre which is worth seeing. Ascending from the close . . . And here there is an image of Dionysus, an offering of the Cnidians. There is a stadium in the highest part of the city: it was made of the common stone of Parnassus, until Herodes the Athenian rebuilt it of Pentelic marble. Such were the notable objects left at Delphi in my time.

    — Pausanias 10.32.1, trans. Frazer

    What we saw. Both survive better than almost anything Pausanias described at length. The theater's 35 rows still look down over the temple, and the stadium keeps its stone starting lines. His description of a theater "worth seeing" is one sentence long. The spaced dots in the middle of the quotation are Frazer's: they mark words missing from the surviving Greek text.

    What Pausanias Could Not See¶

    Some of the things we went to Delphi and Athens to see were not there for Pausanias, or he chose to pass them by.

    What we saw Why Pausanias does not describe it
    The Charioteer of Delphi Probably buried by the earthquake of 373 BCE, about five hundred years before his visit. It was dug up in 1896.
    The freedom records on the polygonal wall Carved where everyone could read them, but his tour of Delphi does not mention them. He was looking for famous offerings, not for the names of freed slaves.
    The Caryatids Standing in plain sight on the Erechtheion; he describes the building's altars and relics, not its porch.
    The Tholos of Delphi Standing, but never named.
    Paul on the Areopagus Christianity is outside his subject; he writes about the old gods and their sanctuaries.
    The Antikythera Mechanism (Chapter 20) Lost in a shipwreck about two centuries before he was born.

    Beyond Greece¶

    The Description of Greece covers only the mainland, so it has no tour of Istanbul, Troy, Ephesus, Rhodes, or Crete. A few of our other stops appear in passing:

    • Rhodes (Chapter 12). He measures the statue of Zeus in Athens against "the colossi at Rhodes and Rome" (1.18.6). The Colossus had lain broken on the ground since an earthquake around 226 BCE, about four centuries before he wrote, but it was still the yardstick for a giant statue.
    • Byzantium and Rhodes (Chapters 4 and 12). He ranks "the walls at Ambrossos in Phocis, at Byzantium and at Rhodes" as "the most strongly fortified places" he knows, and says the wall of Messene is stronger still (4.31.5, trans. Jones). These are the walls of Greek Byzantium, before the Theodosian Walls we saw.
    • Ephesus (Chapter 10). "But all cities worship Artemis of Ephesus, and individuals hold her in honor above all the gods," he writes, and one reason he gives is "the size of the temple, surpassing all buildings among men" (4.31.8, trans. Jones). Today one column stands in a marshy field.
    • Knossos (Chapter 13). He lists among the works of Daedalus an Athena at Knossos, where "there is also Ariadne's Dance, which Homer mentions in the Iliad, wrought in relief on white marble" (9.40.3, trans. Frazer). No such relief has been found; Evans's Knossos was buried and unknown in his day.
