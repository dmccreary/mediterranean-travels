    Spec: One Table, Two Names¶

    Status: built (2026-11-25). The image is generated, the zones are calibrated to it, and the page is in the nav at One Table, Two Names. It is built, not approved: the author approves it after trying the controls.

    Learning Objective¶

    The learner will match six Turkish and Greek dish pairs, give both names for each, and explain, without taking a side, why the two kitchens share so many dishes: one empire for centuries, and the population exchange of 1923.

    Why This Poster¶

    The trip begins at a Turkish meze table on Day 4 and ends with Greek coffee on Day 13, and the book keeps noticing the same dishes under two names. The matched list sits in one table in Chapter 15 (Shared Greek-Turkish Dishes), and the rest is spread over Chapters 3, 6, 16, and 20. A single picture of one table, with the same plate on both sides, makes the point before any text does. It is an empathy poster: the aim is that a traveler sits down on either shore and recognizes the food. The 1923 exchange is the reason the two tables are so alike, and also a loss that both communities still remember (Chapter 7).

    Layout Decision¶

    The idea in Poster Ideas put the Turkish half on the left and the Greek half on the right. This spec turns the table the other way: one long table seen from above, with a thin runner down its length, the Turkish dishes above the runner and the Greek dishes below it, so each pair sits in the same column, one over the other. Reasons:

    • A zone must be one rectangle, and a pair that crosses the runner is then one tall column. A left/right split would need a wide row for each pair and chips too small to read.
    • Six columns leave room for a chip over each pair at the top of the table.
    • Diners facing each other across a table is the picture the poster wants.

    The order of the columns follows a meal: three cold meze, the drink that goes with them, the sweet, and the coffee.

    Column Turkish Greek Zone id Main chapters
    1 Yaprak sarma, dolma Dolmades vine-leaves 6, 15
    2 Cacık Tzatziki yogurt-dip 15, 12, 3
    3 Patlıcan salatası, karnıyarık Melitzanosalata, papoutsakia eggplant 6, 15, 7
    4 Rakı Ouzo anise-spirit 6, 16, 14
    5 Baklava Baklavas baklava 5, 15, 3
    6 Türk kahvesi Ellinikós kafés coffee 3, 20

    The pairs were chosen because the chapters themselves pair them. The table in Chapter 15 also pairs gyros with döner, keftedes with köfte, and loukoumi with lokum. They are left out to keep six columns. See the open questions.

    What Each Zone Says¶

    Each zone has the same shape: both names, what the dish is, one shared root, one place the pair breaks, and where we meet it on the route. The break is a different kind in each column, so the poster avoids saying that every pair is the same dish.

    Zone Where the pair breaks
    Vine leaves Hardly at all. The names differ.
    Yogurt dip The Turkish version is often thinner (Chapter 15).
    Eggplant Papoutsakia wear béchamel and karnıyarık does not; Greek moussaka's béchamel is credited to the early 20th century.
    Anise spirit Strength (about 45 and about 40 percent), and the Cretan raki that has no anise.
    Baklava The names match but the ownership claims are argued among Turks, Greeks, and Arabs.
    Coffee The drink is the same, but "Greek coffee" is a name that replaced an older one after 1974.

    The draft facts are in data.json. Every fact is from the cited chapter sections, and none claims who invented what.

    Tone Rules¶

    1. Never argue about who invented what. The chapters say that this is asked in both countries, often with feeling, and that the honest answer is that each dish belongs to everyone who cooked it. The poster repeats that and goes no further.
    2. Same plates, both sides. The dishes are drawn as near twins, with only the differences the chapters give (a thinner dip, a béchamel on one stuffed eggplant, a glass of water beside the Greek coffee). No difference the chapters do not give.
    3. No national symbols. No flags, crescents, crosses, national colors, or costume. The tablecloth is the same on both halves.
    4. The 1923 exchange is a loss on both sides. Say that families on both shores were uprooted, as Chapter 7 does. Never use it as a punchline or frame one side as the victim.
    5. The Cyprus fact is stated as the chapter states it, as the reason the name came to feel wrong to many Greeks. The zone does not say who was right.
    6. Alcohol is a choice. The spirit zone says many Turks do not drink and declining is polite, as Chapter 6 does.

    Quiz¶

    Ten questions are drafted in data.json, at least one per zone, with three zones asked about twice. They ask about ideas (contested ownership, a name that changed, one word with two drinks) and not about what is drawn.

    To Verify Before Approval¶

    None of these is in data.json.

    1. Preparation differences beyond the few the chapters give. Fillings of vine leaves, how rakı and ouzo are distilled, the syrup and nuts of each baklava, and the thickness of both dips are not in the chapters, apart from the one-line differences listed above. The idea entry called baklava a "honey pastry". The chapters say syrup, so the data does too.
    2. A kebab or döner pair. Chapter 15 pairs gyros with döner, and Chapter 15 also says gyros reached Greece in the 20th century, probably first with refugees from Anatolia ("probably" is the chapter's word). If you add the pair, keep that hedge.
    3. Greek spellings. Chapter 15 uses "Elliniko kafe", Chapter 20 uses ellinikós kafés. The data uses the Chapter 20 form for the coffee, and Chapter 15's forms for the rest. Check the transliterations in one pass.
    4. The Turkish half of "Greek coffee". Chapter 20 says Greeks "usually called it Turkish coffee too" until the 1970s. How and when the Turkish name arose in Greek is not given. Do not add a date before 1970.
    5. Day numbers. The coffee route fact uses Day 1 for Turkish coffee because Chapter 3 describes it. Change it if your own coffee was on another day.

    Open Questions for the Author¶

    1. Six columns or seven? Seven pairs would make each column 215 px wide and the chips would need labels of about nine characters. The strongest candidates are döner and gyros, and lokum and loukoumi. If you prefer a kebab pair, I would swap it for the yogurt dip, not add a seventh column.
    2. Add people? The prompt draws no people, so no skin tones, clothes, or faces need to be chosen. If you want hands passing a plate across the runner, say so, and the prompt will add two hands in the baklava column, below the plain label band.
    3. Left and right instead of top and bottom? Possible, with six wide rows and chips over each row. The dishes would be smaller. I recommend the top-and-bottom layout above.
    4. Where does the 1923 story live? It appears in the vine-leaves and eggplant zones and is the topic of a sim. A seventh zone for "the table itself" is possible but does not fit the one-pair-per-zone rule.
    5. Companion sims. The lesson page should link Reading Food in Layers, Who Was Exchanged in 1923?, and Source, Myth, or Contested? for the baklava question.
    6. Canvas. If your image tool can produce 1792 x 1024, the columns would be about 290 px wide. If you switch, tell me and I will recompute the zones and the prompt.
