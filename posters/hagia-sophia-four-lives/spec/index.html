    Spec: Hagia Sophia, One Building, Four Lives¶

    Status: spec and draft data only. No image has been generated, and there is no index.md or main.html yet, so this poster is not in the nav.

    Learning Objective¶

    The learner will name the four lives of Hagia Sophia (cathedral, mosque, museum, mosque again), state for each one what was added, covered, or uncovered, and say which parts of the structure stayed through all four.

    Why This Poster¶

    Hagia Sophia is the clearest "city of layers" subject on the route, and Chapter 4 already gives its four lives in a single table. The facts are spread over six sections, and the chapter's own advice is to read the building as a document with many authors. One picture that holds the room fixed and changes only what was put into it or taken out of it lets a reader see each author's hand. It is also a living religious site and a contested one, so the poster needs a place where the dates (which are agreed) are kept apart from their meaning (which is not).

    Layout Decision¶

    Four interior tiles in a 2 × 2 block, plus one tall cross-section panel at the right. The idea entry in Poster Ideas calls for the same interior view drawn four times, with an optional fifth zone for what never changed. A fifth zone cannot be a rectangle on top of the four tiles, so the fifth zone gets its own panel. The panel is a vertical cross-section of the dome, pendentives, and ring of windows, which a tall shape fits better than another interior view would.

    Column 1 Column 2 Column 3 (tall)
    1. Cathedral, 537 to 1453 2. Mosque, 1453 to 1934 5. What Stayed
    3. Museum, 1935 to 2020 4. Mosque Again, 2020 to today (spans both rows)

    All four tiles share one camera position, from the nave floor looking east. The prompt asks the image tool to draw the architecture once and change only the furnishing layer, because tools do not hold a room fixed across four tiles reliably. If the tiles drift, the zones still work. A zone is one whole tile.

    What Each Zone Says¶

    The draft facts are in data.json. Each fact is taken from those chapter sections, so the poster repeats what the book already says.

    Contested History: A Labeled Note¶

    The chapter's own words for this are: the dates are agreed, the meaning is not. Following that, the zone summaries and facts state only dates and physical changes. The two zones where a decision changed the building's status, Museum (1934) and Mosque Again (2020), each carry one fact that begins "Contested history note:". It gives the readings that the chapter gives and no others. The chapter gives readings for 2020 and a single reading of 1934. It gives none for 1453, so the Mosque zone has no such note (see the open questions). Quiz questions on these decisions ask for the dates and the physical consequences, and never ask the learner to say which reading is right.

    Tone Rules¶

    1. Living faiths. Hagia Sophia is a working mosque and a church that Orthodox Christians call their mother church. People in the image are small and seen from behind, and no face is drawn in detail. No scene shows prayer in close view.
    2. No ladder and no verdict. The four lives are in date order, but the poster never ranks them or says which is the building's true purpose.
    3. Neutral verbs. The chapter's verbs are used as written: "made a mosque", "made a museum", "returned to use as a mosque". The poster does not use "converted back", "restored", or "reclaimed", because each of them carries a side.
    4. Honest about 537. The cathedral tile shows the later Byzantine state, with figural mosaics. Justinian's own mosaics had only crosses and patterns, and a fact in the zone says so.
    5. No calligraphy in the image. The roundels are drawn as abstract flourishes on discs, because the image tool will garble real lettering.

    Quiz¶

    Ten questions are drafted in data.json, two per zone. They ask about ideas and features (what a mihrab and minbar mark, what a cabinet decision changed, how a pendentive works), not about which tile is drawn where. Every zone is the answer to two questions.

    To Verify Before Approval¶

    These are not in the chapters, so none of them is in data.json.

    1. The mihrab and minbar. The chapter lists them as signs of the mosque life but gives no dates, no builders, and no position. The image shows a prayer niche in the apse and a stepped pulpit beside it, which is the usual arrangement and needs a source.
    2. The museum years, floor and roundels. The chapter does not say whether the floor was bare or carpeted between 1935 and 2020, or whether the roundels stayed on the piers. The image shows bare marble and roundels in place.
    3. The 2020 changes in detail. The chapter says "curtains or lighting over some images during prayers". It does not say which images or how. The image shows one pale curtain over the apse mosaic, which is an illustration, not a claim.
    4. The scaffold in Tile 3. The chapter mentions "restoration work" for the museum life but not scaffolding. If the author prefers, the tile can drop the scaffold.
    5. Readings of 1453. The chapter lists the 1453 conversion as contested but gives no readings of it.

    Open Questions for the Author¶

    1. Worshippers in Tiles 2 and 4. The prompt puts a few small figures, seen from behind and far away, in the mosque tiles. If you would rather show no people in a prayer space, say so, and the prompt will use an empty carpeted floor in both.
    2. Which cathedral? The tile shows the building after 867, with the Virgin and Child in the apse, so it matches what visitors see in the galleries. The alternative is Justinian's 537 state, with gold, crosses, and no figures.
    3. The 1453 note. Add a "contested history" note to the Mosque zone only if the chapter gains readings of 1453.
    4. Chip for the fourth life. "Mosque Again" fits on one line and avoids "restored". An alternative is "Mosque Today".
    5. Companion sims. The lesson page should link Read the Ruin - Which Layer?, Place the Site in Its Era, Source, Myth, or Contested?, and From the Schism to the Sack, 1054–1261.
