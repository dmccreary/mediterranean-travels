    Spec: The Colossus, Popular Picture and Likely Truth¶

    Status: built (2026-11-25). The image is generated, the zones are calibrated to it, and the page is in the nav at The Colossus: Popular Picture and Likely Truth. It is built, not approved: the author approves it after trying the controls.

    Learning Objective¶

    The learner will separate what most people imagine about the Colossus of Rhodes from what the book's evidence supports, and apply the myth versus history test from Chapter 4 (find the earliest source and ask how long after the event it was written) to three parts of the story: the pose, the place, and the end.

    Why This Poster¶

    Chapter 4 teaches a habit for the whole trip: ask when a story first appeared, and who wrote it. The Colossus is the cleanest case on the route. Nearly everyone knows the picture of a giant straddling a harbor, and the book says it almost certainly is not true. The poster puts the picture and the evidence side by side so the habit can be seen working, and it is honest about the rest: the evidence also leaves things open (the pose, the exact site), and the poster says where. It is the poster for the myth versus history concept (Chapter 4, Myth Versus History).

    Layout Decision¶

    Poster Ideas asked for two panels, "What most people imagine" and "What the evidence supports". This spec keeps the two panels (the left column and the right column of the image) and divides each into three tiles, one under the other, so the same question is asked of both versions at the same scale. A zone is one tile, as in the Oracle and Algorithm poster, so six zones.

    Row The question Left tile: popular picture Right tile: likely truth Idea's topics covered
    1 The figure A giant astride the harbor entrance, ships beneath A standing Helios, about 33 m, drawn as a pale reconstruction pose, height, how built
    2 The site The harbor mouth, one foot on each side The same harbor from above; the statue on land, in one of two possible places location
    3 The end The bronze carried off on camels The fallen pieces, and a visitor at the thumb how it fell, what became of the bronze

    Tiles are 744 x 320 px, wide strips, which suit a harbor and a caravan better than tall panels would. Row 2 is drawn from above, so the "too wide" argument is a picture, not only a sentence.

    What Each Zone Says¶

    Zone The point Chapter sections
    Popular: The Figure The straddle is the picture people carry. Chapter 12 gives two reasons against it, and Chapter 1 says "almost certainly". Includes Chapter 4's test. 12: The Colossus of Rhodes. 1: Hellenistic Period. 4: Myth Versus History.
    Likely: The Figure Helios, about 33 m, by Chares of Lindos over about twelve years, paid for by the sold siege machines. The pose is not described, so the figure is a guess. 12: Helios, The Colossus of Rhodes.
    Popular: The Site The popular story puts the feet at the Mandraki entrance. It was far too wide. Rhodes had several harbors. 12: The Colossus of Rhodes, Rhodes.
    Likely: The Site More likely on land, near the harbor or in a sanctuary of Helios. No one knows. 12: The Colossus of Rhodes, Helios, Rhodes.
    Popular: The End The camel story, from a Byzantine chronicle written centuries later. 12: The Colossus of Rhodes. 4: Myth Versus History.

    The draft facts are in data.json. Each fact is taken from those chapter sections.

    Tone Rules¶

    1. Say clearly what is not known. Every "likely" tile ends with a "where the evidence stops" fact. The right-hand figure is a reconstruction, drawn pale and soft-edged on purpose, so that the "truth" column does not look more certain than it is.
    2. Do not make the myth a joke. The popular picture has a real history as an image, and Chapter 4 says myths tell us what later people believed or wanted. The popular tiles are drawn with the same care as the others.
    3. No claim the chapters do not make. The idea entry says "no source describes the straddling pose". The chapters do not say that, so the data gives the reasons the chapters do give (the height and the width of the entrance). See below.
    4. Same scale and same camera in each row, so differences come from the subject, not from the artist.
    5. No faith claims. Helios is treated as the sun god of a Greek city, described as the book does. No modern religious symbols.

    Quiz¶

    Nine questions are drafted in data.json, covering all six tiles, with two tiles asked about twice. They ask about ideas (what rules the straddle out, why a late chronicle is weak, why a myth is not worthless) and not about what is drawn. One asks how the statue was paid for, which leads into the book's story of the siege.

    To Verify Before Approval¶

    The chapters do not give these, so none is in data.json.

    1. "No source describes the straddling pose." This is the idea entry's claim. Chapter 12 gives different reasons: the height and the width of the harbor entrance. Check whether any ancient text describes the pose or the appearance of the statue before saying so.
    2. The famous engravings. The chapter refers to "the famous engravings" but does not say who made them or when. A date would make the Chapter 4 test sharper (how long after the event?). Do not add one without a source.
    3. How it was built. The chapters give the maker (Chares of Lindos), about twelve years, a finish of about 280 BCE, and how it was paid for. They give no method, no scaffolding, no base, and no foundation. The idea entry's scaffolding and stone base are not drawn in the prompt, except a plain low platform under the right-hand figure.
    4. How it fell. The chapters give an earthquake about 226 BCE and an interval of about 54 years. Where the statue broke is not given, and the prompt does not say.
    5. Pliny and the chronicle. The chapters do not date Pliny's visit or name the Byzantine chronicle or its author. They say only "hundreds of camels" and "written centuries later". The data does not add a number.
    6. The pose and crown. The right-hand figure is standing, with rays around the head borrowed from Helios on Rhodian coins (the chapter's evidence for coins, not for the statue). Check how scholars reconstruct the figure before approval.
    7. Candidate sites. The chapters name only "near the harbor" and "a sanctuary of Helios higher in the city". No other names or locations are in the data.

    Open Questions for the Author¶

    1. Two zones or six? The poster could have just two zones, the left panel and the right panel. Six tiles answer the idea's list of topics one by one. Two would be simpler to build and weaker for the quiz.
    2. The ghost figure. The prompt draws the right-hand statue as a pale, translucent bronze with soft edges, to mark it as a guess. Image models do not always honor this. If it comes out solid, we can accept it and let the text carry the caveat.
    3. Row 2 diagram. A bird's-eye harbor with footprints and a translucent band is the riskiest tile for a text-to-image model. If it fails, the fallback is a plain aerial harbor in both tiles with the figure's footprint in a different place.
    4. Companion sims. The lesson page should link Source, Myth, or Contested?, Mind the Gap: How Old Is the Evidence?, and Who Built What We See in the Old Town?.
    5. Canvas. If your image tool can produce 1792 x 1024, the tiles would be wider (about 872 px). If you switch, tell me and I will recompute the zones and the prompt.
