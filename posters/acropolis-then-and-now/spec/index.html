    Spec: The Acropolis, Then and Now¶

    Status: built (2026-11-25). The image is generated, the zones are calibrated to it, and the page is in the nav at The Acropolis, Then and Now. It is built, not approved: the author approves it after trying the controls.

    Learning Objective¶

    The learner will compare the Acropolis of about 406 BCE with the rock a visitor climbs today, and for each of five monuments (the path and gateway, the temple of Athena Nike, the Parthenon, the Erechtheion, and the open-air statue) name what has been lost, rebuilt, replaced, or removed on purpose.

    Why This Poster¶

    Nearly everyone on the trip stands on the rock on Day 10, and orientation is practical: the gateway, the small temple on its bastion, the Parthenon, and the Erechtheion are met in that order, and the chapter ties each step to a different century. One aerial view drawn twice lets a reader hold the whole building program in mind and see the later layers (an explosion in 1687, a clearing in the 1830s, restoration since 1975) as changes to the same rock. The chapter's reading-a-ruin questions from Chapter 4 ("Added? Taken away? Restored, and what did they choose to show?") are the questions the picture asks.

    Layout Decision¶

    Two tall panels side by side, one zone per panel. The idea entry in Poster Ideas also asks for "a matching set of call-outs inside each panel": the gateway, the small temple, the Parthenon, the Erechtheion, and the path up. I did not build ten small zones, one per monument per panel, for three reasons.

    1. An image tool will not place monuments reliably enough for ten small rectangles, and the zones would be re-measured against the picture anyway.
    2. Small zones overlap a great deal in an aerial view. The Parthenon alone crosses several of the others.
    3. The comparison the poster exists for is between the two panels. Two big zones, with the five monuments in the same order inside each zone's facts, keep that comparison on the screen.

    To keep the call-outs available later, the prompt fixes the position of each of the five monuments, as a percentage of the panel, and asks for the same position in both panels (see image-prompt.md). If the author wants numbered markers, a second poster on the call-out engine, with one picture and five markers, can reuse the same image without a new prompt.

    The camera is also a change from the idea entry. The entry says "from the northwest". The prompt asks for a view from the west, a little north of west, because that is the direction a visitor climbs from, so the gateway, the small temple on the bastion to its right, and the Parthenon ahead and to the right all appear in the order the chapter describes.

    What Each Zone Says¶

    Both zones present the five monuments in the same order, so a reader can read one monument across both zones.

    Temple of Athena Nike Small Ionic temple, 427 to 424 BCE, wingless Victory Dismantled in 1686, rebuilt in the 1830s, 1930s, and early 2000s The Temple of Athena Nike

    The draft facts are in data.json. Each fact is taken from those chapter sections, so the poster repeats what the book already says.

    Tone Rules¶

    1. Mark the reconstruction. The left panel is a reconstruction, and its zone says so in a fact that begins "Where the picture breaks down". Color is limited to muted accents on the sculpted parts, because the chapter says only that the sculpture was painted and that faint traces remain.
    2. No ladder and no decline. The right panel is not a fallen version of the left. The chapter's point is that the bare classical rock we admire is itself a 19th-century choice, and the zone says so.
    3. Neutral on the Marbles. The Today zone says where the Caryatids and the sculpture are (Athens, London, and elsewhere) and takes no side. The argument belongs to Agreed Fact or Position?.
    4. No regret, no triumph. The Parthenon was a church and a mosque. The Today panel shows no minaret or tower, because they were cleared from 1835, and the zone's fact "what was erased" describes that choice without judging it.
    5. No brands or signs. The flag is plain stripes, the guide's paddle is blank, and nothing on the rock carries lettering.

    Quiz¶

    Ten questions are drafted in data.json, five per panel. They ask about ideas (a procession bringing a robe, a cleared rock, a restoration method), not about what is drawn on which side. The sample question in the idea entry ("Which monument stands on the bastion at the right of the entrance?") has a monument as its answer, so it does not fit a two-zone poster. It belongs on the call-out version if one is built.

    To Verify Before Approval¶

    These are not in the chapters, so none of them is in data.json.

    1. Colors on the sculpture. The chapter says only that the sculpture was painted and that faint traces remain. The prompt's dusty blue and warm red are an illustrator's choice and must stay labeled as a reconstruction.
    2. Scaffolding and a crane on the Parthenon today. The chapter says restoration continues but does not describe equipment on the rock. The picture's scaffold and crane are illustrations of that, and may not match the site in November 2026. The author can drop them.
    3. The aerial geometry. The relative positions of the Erechtheion, the Parthenon, and the bastion, as drawn from the west, rest on common knowledge of the site, not on a chapter figure. Check them against a plan.
    4. Details of the statue. The chapter gives the Promachos's material (bronze) and a spear tip visible from Cape Sounion. The pose, shield, and height in the image are not sourced.
    5. The 5th-century procession. The chapter describes the Panathenaia procession but not how large it was, so the picture's size is an illustration.

    Open Questions for the Author¶

    1. Call-outs on top? If you want the five monuments clickable, a call-out version can reuse the same image, as described under Layout Decision.
    2. Camera. West-northwest (the prompt) or the idea's northwest.
    3. A third panel? The chapter's Parthenon had a church and mosque life between the two views (see The Parthenon's Five Lives). A third panel for about 1674, before the explosion, would show the mosque and minaret, but it would make three panels of 480 px.
    4. Companion sims. The lesson page should link The Parthenon's Five Lives, Agreed Fact or Position?, Doric, Ionic, or Corinthian?, and Xerxes' Road to Salamis.
