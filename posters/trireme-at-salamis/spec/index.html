    Spec: A Trireme at Salamis¶

    Status: spec and draft data only. No image has been generated, and there is no index.md or main.html yet, so this poster is not in the nav.

    Learning Objective¶

    The learner will explain how the Athenian trireme linked a sea victory to a change in who held power, by naming what the ship was made of, who rowed it, and how Athens's fleet gave the poorest citizens a claim on the city.

    Why This Poster¶

    Chapter 15 gives the trireme in three numbers and the battle in five moves, and ends the worked example with a promise: about 30,000 oarsmen, "a fact whose consequences for democracy Chapter 16 follows." Chapter 16 keeps the promise in the next chapter, in the worked example "why the rowers had a vote that counted." A single cutaway puts the ship, the crew, the silver, and the narrows in one picture, so the reader sees the whole chain: the silver bought the ships, the ships needed the poor, and the poor gained power. The chapters support leading with that link, so marker 1 is the oarsmen and tells the democracy story first.

    Layout Decision¶

    Call-out engine, one cutaway in a scene. The hero is a large trireme in side view with the hull cut away in the middle, so three ranks of oarsmen, the oar openings, the deck, and the ram all show on one ship. Behind it, small and hazy, are the Athenian line on the left, the Persian ships crowded in the narrows in the middle, and Xerxes' seat on the mountain on the right. That keeps the battle in view without a second panel.

    The engine is layout: "side-panel", orientation: "landscape", showNumbers: true. Marker positions in data.json are estimates taken from the positions stated in image-prompt.md. They must be calibrated with ?edit=true once the image exists.

    Tone Rules¶

    1. Lead with the link, then say where it stops. Marker 1's tip names who democracy left out: women, metics, and enslaved people, and the enslaved miners who dug the silver. The poster does not say the rowers made Athens a fair city.
    2. Fair to the Persians. The Persian ships are drawn as capable ships caught in a bad place, and marker 8 uses the chapter's own point that Athenians watched their victory as the enemy's tragedy. Marker 7's tip asks which shore Herodotus stood on.
    3. No blood, wounds, or bodies. The scene is the start of the battle.
    4. No invented detail. The chapters give three numbers for the ship. The image shows more (deck, steering oars, benches), but data.json states none of it.
    5. No ladder. The poster does not frame Athenian democracy as the goal toward which history was heading.

    To Verify Before Approval¶

    These details are in the idea entry or the image prompt but not in the chapters, so they are not in data.json. The chapters do give the three levels of rowers, the ram at the bow at the waterline, and the crew of about 170 rowers and about 30 others, so those stay.

    1. How the three levels were arranged and named. The prompt draws staggered ranks stacked diagonally with a row of oar openings per level. The chapters say only "three levels."
    2. Steering oars, the deck's shape, and crew by role. The idea lists steering oars and "crew by role." Chapter 15 gives only "officers, deckhands, and marines." There is no steering-oar marker; add one only after a source is named.
    3. Whether anyone but citizens rowed. The chapters say "many of them from the poorest citizens." They do not say whether foreigners or enslaved men also rowed. The picture avoids marking status.
    4. Where Xerxes' seat stood and whether the chair was gilded. The chapter says "a seat at the foot of Mount Aigaleo." The prompt draws it halfway up the slope, with a canopy and attendants.
    5. The crew total of about 200. It is 170 plus 30 from the chapter, but the chapter does not add them.

    Open Questions for the Author¶

    1. Photo of the replica? The chapter names the replica Olympias, built in the 1980s. If the author has a photo or a visit planned, a second layer could compare the cutaway with it.
    2. Marker 4 is the thinnest. The chapters say little about the deck. It could be dropped, or replaced by a steering-oar marker once a source is chosen.
    3. Companion links. The lesson page should link Xerxes' Road to Salamis and Who Could Vote in Athens?.
