    Spec: Five Classrooms Across Three Thousand Years¶

    Status: built (2026-11-25). The image is generated, the zones are calibrated to it, and the page is in the nav at Five Classrooms Across Three Thousand Years. It is built, not approved: the author approves it after trying the controls. The Lyceum panel was kept as drafted (the first option in the open questions below).

    Learning Objective¶

    The learner will compare five classrooms from the route, from the Lyceum to a modern tutoring room, and for each one state who was inside, what opened the door, and who was still left outside.

    Why This Poster¶

    Learning for All is the book's third thread, and it ends nearly every daily chapter. Each chapter runs the same four-step pattern from Chapter 2: learning is locked up, a new technology or institution opens it, someone resists, and the circle widens but never all the way. The pattern is spread over five chapters. A single row of five rooms lets a reader see it at once, and the zones keep asking the same question of each room: who is still outside?

    Layout Decision¶

    Five tall panels in a single row, all drawn at the same scale, from the same camera height, with the floor at the same level. Side by side is the point: the reader compares who is in the room, what the learners hold, and how close the teacher stands. A zone is one whole panel, so this uses the same grid engine as the Three Classical Orders and Oracle and Algorithm posters. The panels are narrow (288 px wide on a 1536 px image), so the prompt asks for people drawn large and stacked toward the foreground.

    Panel Room Date Chapter
    1 The Lyceum about 335 BCE 20
    2 Rhodes, a rhetoric school first century BCE 12
    3 A Venetian print shop (Aldus Manutius) about 1500 14
    4 A medrese at the Süleymaniye 1550s 5
    5 A learning room with a tutor today 20, 21

    The order is chronological left to right, but it is not a staircase of progress. Several rooms widened access while others kept narrowing it, and the zones say so.

    What Each Zone Says¶

    Panels 2, 3, 4, and 5 follow the chapters' own four steps, so a reader can read down one step across the whole row.

    Step Rhodes Aldine Press Medrese Modern Tutor
    Locked up Advanced rhetoric lived with a few teachers in a few cities Plato in Greek needed a manuscript collection, a scribe, and a Greek teacher Advanced study needed years that poor families could not afford One-to-one tutoring went to princes, as with Alexander and Aristotle
    Opened Rich Romans could sail to the teachers, who built schools for many students Printed Greek authors with grammars; the 1501 octavo a student could own and carry An endowment paid tuition, room, meals, and a stipend AI tutoring, tried in a Harvard physics course and in Edo State, Nigeria
    Resisted The Senate expelled Greek teachers in 161 BCE Collectors scorned print; Aldus's own privileges forbade copying his type Inertia: the curriculum stayed as founded while the world moved to print Wrong answers, low use, and a Turkish study showing harm
    Never all the way Families who could pay for a voyage and a tutor; daughters almost never Men who had been to school; few women learned Greek; villagers could not read Only boys and men; Arabic; Muslim subjects Children with no connection, device, or tutor in their language

    Panel 1, the Lyceum, does not have the four steps in the book. Chapter 20 treats it as a place to visit, not as a Learning for All story, so the zone gives the room, the teacher, the method, the setback, and the limits of Athens's circle (from Chapter 16). See the open questions below.

    The draft facts are in data.json. Each fact is taken from those chapter sections, so the poster repeats what the book already says.

    Tone Rules¶

    1. No ladder. Do not draw or word the five as stages leading up to the modern tutor. The last panel ends with the same "never all the way" step as the others.
    2. Draw the exclusions honestly. The Lyceum and Rhodes show men, because that is who the chapters say attended. The medrese panel includes the neighborhood primary school with girls and boys, because the chapter says so.
    3. Keep the human in the last panel. The chapter's closing idea is learning with communities, not to them. The adult sits beside the child and looks at the child, not the screen.
    4. No brands. No logos, no robots, and no recognizable product screens.
    5. Respect for each tradition. The medrese is drawn as the serious college it was, not as a foil for the modern room.

    Quiz¶

    Ten questions are drafted in data.json, two per room. They ask about ideas (traveling to the teacher, endowed education, the paper cost of a format), not about what is drawn.

    Open Questions for the Author¶

    1. The Lyceum. The chapters do not say who attended Aristotle's school, what they paid, or whether the room was a gate or an open door. Options: (a) keep the zone as drafted, which says only what the book says; (b) add a short four-step note to Chapter 20; (c) swap the panel for Aristotle teaching Alexander at Mieza, which Chapter 20 does tell as a "locked up" story, but that would put two tutor scenes in the row.
    2. A sixth room? The Hall of Tyrannus at Ephesus (Acts 19, where Paul taught for two years) is the one classroom on the route not in the row. Adding it makes six panels of 240 px, and the people get small.
    3. Canvas. If your image tool can produce 1792 × 1024, the panels would be wider (about 336 px). If you switch, tell me and I will recompute the zones and the prompt.
    4. Companion sims. The lesson page should link Who Could Vote in Athens?, What Did the Külliye Do?, When Printing Arrived, and the Tutoring Effect Size Explorer.
