    Spec: The Oracle and the Algorithm, Side by Side¶

    Status: built (2026-11-25). The image is generated, the zones are calibrated to it, and the page is in the nav at The Oracle and the Algorithm, Side by Side. It is built, not approved: the author approves it after trying the controls.

    Learning Objective¶

    The learner will compare how the Delphic oracle and a modern predictive model answer questions, and name, for each of six matched stages, one way the two are alike and one way the parallel breaks.

    Why This Poster¶

    Chapters 17 and 19 run the oracle and the algorithm in parallel, but the parallel is spread over about thirty-five thousand words and the Limits of the Oracle Parallel table sits at the very end. One picture lets a reader see the six pairs at once, and the zones make the limits visible next to each pair instead of after it. It is also the one poster where a quiz can test the breaks in a comparison, not only the matches.

    Layout Decision¶

    The first sketch in Poster Ideas used six horizontal rows across a split scene. Image generators do not keep six rows aligned, so this spec uses six tiles in a 3 × 2 grid, each a small diptych with Delphi on the left and today on the right. A zone is one whole tile, so one hover compares both halves. This matches the grid engine used by the Three Classical Orders poster.

    Column 1 Column 2 Column 3
    1. Who asks 2. Where knowledge comes from 3. The form of the answer
    4. Who interprets 5. How we mishear 6. When the answer changes the future

    The reading order follows the life of one question: someone asks, a source knows, an answer comes back in some form, someone interprets it, we hear what we want, and the answer changes what happens next.

    The Six Pairs¶

    Tile At Delphi Today Where the parallel breaks Chapter sections
    1. Who asks A petitioner who climbed, paid the pelanos, and sacrificed an animal A user typing a prompt, and the people scored without asking The oracle answered the few who chose to come; models predict the many who did not 17: Fees and Sacrifice. 19: Consent in Mass Prediction, Limits of the Oracle Parallel
    2. Where knowledge comes from Envoys from many cities, bringing news with their questions Training data from many voices, compressed into a model The Delphi data-hub idea is plausible but unproven; a model's training data is real, if hidden 19: Oracle as Data Hub, Training Data, Data Bias
    3. The form of the answer A verse that came true whichever side won A fluent, hedged paragraph or a probability By Fontenrose's count, the oracle mostly gave commands and approvals, not predictions 17: Hedging Answers, The Croesus Prophecy. 19: Interpreting Ambiguous Answers, Prediction Uncertainty
    4. Who interprets The Athenian Assembly debating the wooden wall A human in the loop The Assembly could disagree and vote. A reviewer who cannot disagree is in the loop on paper only 17: The Wooden Walls Prophecy. 19: Human in the Loop
    5. How we mishear Croesus hearing victory A leading question that gets an agreeable answer Croesus misheard a god. A model may agree because it was trained partly on approval 17: The Croesus Prophecy. 19: Confirmation Bias
    6. When the answer changes the future Oedipus at the crossroads A prediction that feeds, or defeats, itself The oracle's prophecies were told afterward as stories. A model's feedback loop can be measured, if someone keeps score 17: The Oedipus Prophecy. 19: Self-Fulfilling Prophecy, Student Risk Prediction, Testability of Predictions

    The draft facts for each tile are in data.json. Every fact is taken from those chapter sections, so the poster repeats what the book already says and adds nothing new.

    Tone Rules¶

    1. Respect for the oracle. Chapter 19 insists that for the people who climbed to Delphi, consulting Apollo was an act of piety, and that calling it "an early algorithm" misses what it meant to them. Each Delphi half is drawn with dignity, and each tile's last fact is a place the parallel breaks.
    2. No claim that the oracle was a data center. The hub idea is labeled a reasonable suggestion, not a proven one.
    3. No vendor logos, brand colors, or recognizable product screens on the right halves.
    4. No score-keeping of "who is smarter." The point is the questions we ask of any source.

    Quiz¶

    Nine questions are drafted in data.json, covering all six tiles, with three tiles asked about twice. The questions deliberately ask about the ideas (consent, hedging, automation bias), not about what is drawn, so a reader who understands the chapters can answer without recognizing a figure.

    Open Questions for the Author¶

    1. Six tiles or seven? The chapters also have a strong seventh pair: calibration and track record (a Pythia whose answers were written down afterward, against a model scored on written predictions). Adding it makes a 3 × 3 grid with one empty cell, or a 4 + 3 layout. Six keeps the image clean.
    2. Which image tool? The prompt is written for a general text-to-image model. Tile 3 is the riskiest, because of the rule against any letters.
    3. Reuse the Glaux mascot? Not in the image, so the poster stays neutral. Glaux can appear on the lesson page.
    4. Companion sims. The lesson page should link Oracle, Gears, or Data?, Can This Answer Lose?, and When a Prediction Feeds Itself as the next step after each tile.
