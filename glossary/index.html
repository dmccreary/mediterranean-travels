    Glossary of Terms¶

    Terms are in alphabetical order. A 🔊 Pronounce button plays the name aloud; the respelling shows the stressed syllable in capitals. All the hard names are also collected in the Pronunciation Guide.

    Ablution¶

    The ritual washing of face, hands, arms and feet that Muslims perform before prayer, using fountains or taps found at every mosque.

    Example: The rows of taps along the courtyard wall of a mosque are for this washing, not for drinking.

    Acropolis¶

    The rocky hilltop in the center of Athens, a fortress and sanctuary since the Bronze Age, crowned by the 5th-century BCE temples of Athena built under Pericles.

    In Greek the word means "high city," and many towns had one.

    Example: The Parthenon, Erechtheion, Propylaea and Temple of Athena Nike all stand on it.

    Acropolis Museum¶

    The modern museum at the foot of the Acropolis, opened in 2009, which displays sculptures from the hilltop and has glass floors over an excavated ancient neighborhood.

    Example: Its top floor is aligned with the Parthenon itself, visible through the glass walls.

    See also: Parthenon Marbles Dispute

    Acting on Foresight¶

    Turning a prediction into a decision and action, which requires judgment, courage and resources, since a warning that no one acts on changes nothing.

    Example: Themistocles used the oracle's warning to argue for building ships before the Persians came.

    Adyton¶

    Say it: AD-ih-ton

    The inner room of the Temple of Apollo at Delphi, "the place not to be entered," set lower than the floor, where the Pythia sat on her tripod.

    Aegean Sea¶

    The arm of the Mediterranean between mainland Greece and Turkey, dotted with islands, across which ships, settlers and ideas have moved for at least five thousand years.

    Example: Our ship crosses it from the Dardanelles to Kuşadası, Rhodes, Crete and finally Piraeus.

    Affordable Course Materials¶

    Textbooks and other learning materials priced low enough that cost does not keep any student from having them, whether in print or in digital form.

    Example: Aldus Manutius's small, cheap editions of Virgil were an early version of the idea.

    See also: Aldine Editions, Open Educational Resources

    Agapē¶

    Say it: ah-GAH-pay

    The Greek word Paul uses in 1 Corinthians 13 for love, meaning goodwill shown in action, as distinct from erōs, desire, and philia, friendship.

    See also: Love as Greatest Virtue

    Agiorgitiko¶

    Say it: ah-yor-YEE-tee-koh

    A red wine grape native to the Nemea area of the Peloponnese, making soft, fruity red wines and one of the most widely planted native red varieties in Greece.

    Example: A glass of it pairs well with lamb souvlaki in a Plaka taverna.

    See also: Greek Wine

    Ahhiyawa¶

    Say it: ah-khee-YAH-wah

    A land named in Hittite records whose king dealt with the Hittites as an equal, most likely the Mycenaean Greeks, Homer's Achaeans.

    Example: One letter mentions an earlier quarrel between the two kings over Wilusa.

    See also: Wilusa, Mycenaean Greeks

    AI as Thinking Partner¶

    Using an AI system to question, challenge and extend one's own thinking, rather than to hand over the thinking or accept its output without checking.

    See also: Socratic Method, AI Over-Reliance

    AI Capability Forecasting¶

    Predicting what AI systems will be able to do in the future, so that schools, companies and governments can plan, a hard task because the systems change quickly and abilities are hard to measure.

    Example: One research group measures how long a task, in human working time, AI systems can complete reliably.

    See also: AI Doubling Curves

    AI Doubling Curves¶

    Charts of a measure of AI, such as task length, computing power or cost, that grows by doubling at a steady interval, and the forecasts made by extending that doubling into the future.

    Doubling looks slow for years and then sudden, and extending it is a trend with wide uncertainty, not a promise.

    AI Guardrails¶

    The design rules and limits that keep an AI system's behavior within safe and educational bounds; in a tutor, the most important is giving hints instead of answers.

    See also: AI Tutoring

    AI in Education¶

    The use of artificial intelligence in teaching and learning, such as tutoring, feedback, translation and course design, with both promise for reaching more learners and risks of over-reliance and bias.

    See also: AI Tutoring, AI Guardrails

    AI Over-Reliance¶

    Depending on AI tools so heavily that a person stops thinking, practicing or checking for themselves, losing skills and accepting errors.

    Example: A student who copies an AI's essay learns nothing about writing one.

    AI Translation¶

    The use of machine-learning systems to translate speech and writing between languages and to read text aloud or transcribe it.

    It could let learners study in their own languages instead of first learning a dominant one.

    See also: Mother-Tongue Learning

    AI Tutoring¶

    The use of AI systems to give individual students explanations, questions, hints and feedback at their own pace, aiming to bring some benefits of one-on-one tutoring to every learner.

    See also: AI Guardrails, One-on-One Tutoring

    AI-Native School¶

    A school designed around a clear purpose and way of teaching first and then equipped with the AI that serves them, with mornings of personalized academic work and afternoons of projects and collaboration.

    AI-Native Schools Companion¶

    The companion textbook AI-Native Schools, linked throughout this book, which works out in detail how a school might use AI tutoring and intelligent textbooks well.

    Alcmaeonids¶

    Say it: alk-MEE-uh-nidz

    A powerful Athenian aristocratic family who, while in exile, rebuilt the front of Apollo's temple at Delphi in fine marble in the 6th century BCE.

    Example: Herodotus reports they were also accused of bribing the Pythia.

    See also: Bribery of the Oracle

    Aldine Editions¶

    The books printed by Aldus Manutius's press in Venice, especially its small octavo volumes from 1501, which made classical texts portable and cheaper for students and readers.

    Example: A scholar could carry a pocket Virgil instead of needing a heavy manuscript at a desk.

    Aldus Manutius¶

    Say it: Manutius muh-NOO-shee-us

    A Venetian printer and publisher (about 1450–1515) whose Aldine Press issued the first printed editions of many Greek classics and introduced small, affordable books in italic type.

    See also: Aldine Editions, Affordable Course Materials

    Alexander the Great¶

    The king of Macedon (356–323 BCE), taught by Aristotle, who conquered the Persian Empire and lands as far as India, spreading Greek language and culture across the Near East.

    Alice Kober¶

    An American classicist (1906–1950) at Brooklyn College who studied Linear B for years on thousands of handmade index cards and showed that its words changed their endings like an inflected language.

    Her method laid the foundation for the decipherment.

    See also: Michael Ventris, Credit for Groundwork

    Alphabet¶

    A script in which each letter stands for a single consonant or vowel sound, so that a small set of signs can spell any word. Its simplicity made reading far easier to learn.

    Example: The Greeks borrowed the Phoenician letters and added vowels, creating the ancestor of the Latin letters used in this book.

    See also: Greek Alphabet, Phoenician Alphabet

    Alphabet Reform of 1928¶

    The law of November 1928 that replaced the Arabic-based Ottoman script with a new Latin-based alphabet of 29 letters for Turkish, designed to match the language's sounds.

    Example: Within months, newspapers, street signs and school books switched to the new letters.

    Altar to an Unknown God¶

    An altar inscription that Paul, in Acts 17, says he saw in Athens, which he used as the starting point of his speech. Ancient writers do mention Athenian altars to unnamed gods.

    See also: Meeting People Where They Are

    Amfissa Olive Groves¶

    Say it: Amfissa AHM-fee-sah

    The broad plain of olive trees below Delphi, stretching from the town of Amfissa to the Gulf of Corinth, one of the oldest continuously cultivated olive landscapes in Greece.

    Example: From the theater at Delphi, the groves look like a silver-green sea in the valley.

    See also: Olive Oil

    Amphictyonic League¶

    Say it: Amphictyonic am-fik-tee-ON-ik

    The council of Greek peoples that managed the sanctuary at Delphi, oversaw the Pythian Games and could declare sacred wars against those who violated the sanctuary.

    Anatolia¶

    Say it: an-uh-TOH-lee-uh

    The large peninsula that forms the Asian part of modern Turkey, between the Black Sea, the Aegean and the Mediterranean. It was home to Hittites, Greek cities, Romans, Byzantines and Ottomans in turn.

    Example: Troy, Ephesus and Paul's hometown of Tarsus all lie in this region.

    Anchoring Change in Culture¶

    Making a new practice part of an organization's shared habits, values and stories, so it continues because people believe in it, not because a leader enforces it.

    Ancient Agora of Athens¶

    The open public square at the foot of the Acropolis that served as the heart of civic life in ancient Athens: market, law courts, council house, temples and covered walkways.

    Example: Socrates questioned passersby here, and Paul argued in it with Stoic and Epicurean thinkers.

    Ancient Greece¶

    The Greek-speaking world of roughly 800 BCE to the Roman conquest, made up of many independent cities around the Aegean and beyond, and famous for its art, theater, philosophy and early democracy.

    Example: The Acropolis in Athens and the sanctuary at Delphi are two of the best-known places from this world on our route.

    Ancient Mediterranean Treasures¶

    The name of Viking's seven-night cruise itinerary from Istanbul to Athens, which stops at sites spanning more than three thousand years, from Troy to Byzantine Constantinople.

    Example: In one week the itinerary takes us from the Gallipoli peninsula to Ephesus, Rhodes, Heraklion and Piraeus.

    See also: Viking Star Cruise

    Ancient Prediction Machines¶

    Devices built before the modern era that worked out future events from a model fixed in their structure, rather than from a god's answer or new observation.

    Example: The Antikythera mechanism's gears predicted eclipses years ahead.

    See also: Antikythera Mechanism

    Anıtkabir¶

    Say it: ah-nuht-kah-BEER

    The monumental mausoleum of Mustafa Kemal Atatürk on a hill in Ankara, completed in 1953, visited by large crowds on national days and anniversaries.

    Example: On November 10, people across Turkey stop at 9:05 AM while officials gather there.

    Answer as Start of Inquiry¶

    Treating an answer not as the end of thinking but as a claim to be tested, so that it begins an investigation.

    Example: Told that no one was wiser than he, Socrates set out to test the claim by questioning others.

    Antikythera Mechanism¶

    Say it: Antikythera an-tee-kih-THEER-uh

    A hand-cranked bronze device of interlocking gears, made in the 2nd or 1st century BCE and found in 1901 in a shipwreck off the island of Antikythera, that modeled the movements of the sun, moon and probably planets and predicted eclipses.

    Example: Its corroded fragments are displayed in the National Archaeological Museum in Athens.

    See also: Ancient Prediction Machines

    Anzac Cove¶

    The small beach on the west coast of the Gallipoli peninsula where Australian and New Zealand troops landed on April 25, 1915, under steep cliffs held by Ottoman troops.

    Example: Visitors read Atatürk's words of reconciliation on a stone memorial above the beach.

    ANZAC Forces¶

    The Australian and New Zealand Army Corps, the combined force that landed on the Gallipoli peninsula on April 25, 1915.

    Example: April 25, Anzac Day, is a national day of remembrance in both countries.

    Apollo¶

    The Greek god of prophecy, music, healing, light and order, worshipped above all at Delphi, where he spoke through his priestess.

    Apollo's Winter Absence¶

    The ancient belief that Apollo left Delphi for three winter months each year, traveling to a distant northern land, during which the oracle gave no answers.

    Example: The book uses it to show that even the god of prophecy had a season when he could not see ahead.

    See also: Prediction Horizon

    Apollos¶

    Say it: uh-POL-us

    A Jewish believer from Alexandria, described in Acts as eloquent and learned in the scriptures, who taught in Ephesus and Corinth after Priscilla and Aquila instructed him more fully.

    Example: One faction at Corinth claimed "I belong to Apollos."

    Arachova¶

    Say it: ah-RAH-kho-vah

    A mountain town on the slopes of Mount Parnassus about 10 km east of Delphi, known for its stone houses, woven rugs, local cheese and winter ski crowds.

    Example: Our day trip stops here for coffee on the way to the sanctuary.

    Aratus¶

    Say it: uh-RAY-tus

    A Greek poet of the 3rd century BCE from Soli in Cilicia, whose astronomical poem Phaenomena contains the line "For we too are his offspring," quoted by Paul in Athens.

    See also: Areopagus Speech

    Archaeology¶

    The study of the human past through the physical things people left behind, such as buildings, tools, pottery, bones and writing, recovered by careful excavation and survey.

    It is the main way we know about periods, like the Bronze Age, that left few or no readable texts.

    Example: Digging at Hisarlik revealed nine superimposed settlements, giving a physical history for the place Homer called Troy.

    Archaic Period¶

    The phase of Greek history from about 800 to 480 BCE, when city-states formed, Greeks founded colonies across the Mediterranean, the alphabet spread and the first stone temples and life-size statues appeared.

    Example: The Oracle of Delphi rose to its greatest influence during these centuries.

    Areopagus¶

    Say it: air-ee-OP-uh-gus

    The rocky hill northwest of the Acropolis, the "Hill of Ares," and the ancient council of former magistrates that met there and judged homicide cases.

    Example: Paul's speech to the Athenians is set here in Acts 17.

    See also: Areopagus Speech

    Areopagus Bronze Plaque¶

    Say it: Areopagus air-ee-OP-uh-gus

    The large bronze tablet set into the rock at the foot of the Areopagus steps, engraved with the Greek text of Paul's speech from Acts 17.

    Example: Greek school groups and pilgrims often stop to read it in the original words.

    Areopagus Speech¶

    Say it: Areopagus air-ee-OP-uh-gus

    Paul's address to the Athenians in Acts 17:22–31, which begins with their altar "to an unknown god," quotes Greek poets and proclaims the God who made the world.

    Example: It quotes Aratus' line "For we too are his offspring."

    Aristotle¶

    A Greek philosopher and scientist (384–322 BCE), student of Plato and tutor of Alexander the Great, who wrote on logic, biology, ethics, politics and poetry and founded the Lyceum.

    See also: Lyceum

    Artemis of Ephesus¶

    Say it: Ephesus EF-uh-sus

    The local form of the goddess Artemis worshipped at Ephesus, shown as a stiff standing figure with a tall headdress and many egg-shaped forms on her chest, whose meaning scholars still debate.

    Example: Two marble statues of her stand in the Ephesus Museum in Selçuk.

    Arthur Evans¶

    The British archaeologist (1851–1941) who excavated Knossos from 1900, named the Minoan civilization, classified its scripts as Linear A and Linear B and rebuilt parts of the palace.

    Example: The concrete columns painted red at Knossos are his work, not the Minoans'.

    Artificial Intelligence¶

    The field of computing that builds systems able to do tasks that normally need human intelligence, such as understanding language, recognizing images, making predictions and solving problems.

    Example: A phone app that translates a Turkish menu aloud uses this kind of system.

    See also: Machine Learning, AI in Education

    Asia Minor Refugees¶

    The Greek Orthodox people who fled or were expelled from Anatolia in 1922–1923 and resettled in Greece, many in new neighborhoods around Athens and Piraeus.

    Example: Athens suburbs such as Nea Smyrni, "New Smyrna," are named after the towns they left.

    See also: Rebetiko

    Ataturk Memorial Day¶

    Say it: Atatürk ah-tah-TEWRK

    November 10, the anniversary of Atatürk's death at Dolmabahçe Palace at 9:05 AM in 1938, marked across Turkey by a minute of silence at that moment.

    Example: At 9:05 AM sirens sound and traffic in Istanbul comes to a stop.

    Ataturk's Reforms¶

    Say it: Atatürk ah-tah-TEWRK

    The sweeping changes of the 1920s and 1930s that remade Turkish law, education, dress and public life, including abolishing the caliphate, a new civil code, the Latin alphabet, surnames and votes for women.

    See also: Secularism in Turkey, Costs of Top-Down Reform

    Athena¶

    The Greek goddess of wisdom, crafts and strategic war, patron of Athens, often shown with helmet, spear and her sacred bird, the little owl.

    Example: The owl on the ancient Athenian silver coin, and on today's Greek one-euro coin, is her bird.

    See also: Glaux, Parthenon

    Athena Pronaia Sanctuary¶

    Say it: Pronaia proh-NAY-uh

    The lower sanctuary at Delphi, about a kilometer from Apollo's, dedicated to Athena "before the temple," which travelers passed on their way to the oracle.

    See also: Tholos of Delphi

    Athenian Citizenship¶

    The legal status that gave a person a share in governing classical Athens; after 451 BCE it required both parents to be Athenian, and full political rights belonged only to adult men.

    Athenian Democracy¶

    The system of government in Athens from about 508 to 322 BCE in which adult male citizens voted directly on laws and war in an Assembly and filled most offices by lottery.

    Example: Juries of hundreds of citizens, chosen by a stone allotment machine, decided cases in the Agora.

    See also: Pnyx, Who Democracy Left Out

    Athenian Treasury¶

    The small Doric building on the Sacred Way at Delphi built by Athens around 500–490 BCE to hold offerings, reconstructed by French archaeologists in the early 1900s.

    Example: Its walls carry carved hymns to Apollo with signs for musical notes, among the oldest written music.

    Athens¶

    The capital of modern Greece and in ancient times the leading city of Attica, home of the first democracy, the Parthenon, tragedy and the schools of Plato and Aristotle.

    Example: The city's 3.6 million people live around the same rock that held Athena's temple 2,400 years ago.

    See also: Acropolis, Athens as Modern Capital

    Athens as Modern Capital¶

    The choice in 1834 to make Athens, then a small town of a few thousand people, the capital of the new Greek kingdom, because of its ancient prestige.

    Example: The neoclassical buildings along Panepistimiou Street were built in this period to give the new capital a classical look.

    Athens Polytechnic Uprising¶

    The student occupation of the National Technical University of Athens in November 1973 against the military dictatorship, ended on November 17 when a tank broke through the gate.

    Example: Every November 17 Athenians march to the US embassy and lay flowers at the Polytechnic gate.

    See also: Exarchia

    Augustine of Hippo¶

    A North African bishop and theologian (354–430 CE) whose reading of Paul on grace, sin and the will shaped Western Christianity, and whose Confessions tells of his own conversion.

    Example: He wrote that a passage of Romans, read in a garden in Milan, changed his life.

    Ayasuluk¶

    Say it: ah-yah-soo-LOOK

    The name for centuries of the town now called Selçuk, a Turkish form of the Greek Hagios Theologos, "the Holy Theologian," the title of the apostle John.

    Baklava¶

    A pastry of many thin layers of phyllo dough, filled with chopped pistachios or walnuts, baked with butter and soaked in syrup, made across the former Ottoman lands.

    See also: Shared Greek-Turkish Dishes

    Balik Ekmek¶

    A grilled fish sandwich, usually mackerel with onions and lettuce in bread, sold from boats and stalls along the waterfront at Eminönü in Istanbul.

    Example: Eating one by the Galata Bridge, with ferries coming and going, is a classic Istanbul lunch.

    Bargaining in Bazaars¶

    The practice of negotiating a price between buyer and seller in a traditional market, through offers and counter-offers, often with conversation and tea.

    Example: In the Grand Bazaar a carpet price is a starting point, while packaged spices in the Spice Bazaar usually have fixed prices.

    See also: Grand Bazaar

    Basic Greek Phrases¶

    A small set of everyday expressions in modern Greek, such as greetings, thanks and requests, that let a visitor show courtesy and handle simple exchanges.

    Example: Kalimera, "good morning," and efcharistó, "thank you," are the two most useful phrases in Athens.

    Basic Turkish Phrases¶

    A small set of everyday expressions in Turkish, such as greetings, thanks and requests, that let a visitor show courtesy and handle simple exchanges.

    Example: Saying teşekkür ederim, "thank you," to a shopkeeper in the Spice Bazaar often earns a smile.

    Basilica Cistern¶

    A vast underground water reservoir built under Justinian in 532 CE across the street from Hagia Sophia, its roof held up by 336 columns.

    Ionia¶

    Iron Age¶

    The era that followed the Bronze Age, beginning in the Aegean around 1100 BCE, when iron replaced bronze for everyday tools and weapons because iron ore was common and widely available.

    Example: The Greek alphabet and Homer's epics took written form in the early part of this period.

    Islam¶

    The religion founded in Arabia in the 7th century CE through the prophet Muhammad, centered on submission to one God and on the Quran. It was the faith of the Ottoman state and is the majority religion of modern Turkey.

    Example: The call to prayer sounding five times a day over Istanbul is the most audible daily sign of this faith on our trip.

    Islamic Calligraphy¶

    The art of beautiful handwriting in the Arabic script, used to write the Quran and to decorate mosques, books and objects, valued as the highest visual art in Islamic culture.

    Example: Huge round wooden panels in Hagia Sophia carry the names of God, Muhammad and the first caliphs.

    Istanbul¶

    Turkey's largest city, with more than 15 million people, spanning the Bosphorus between Europe and Asia, formerly Byzantium and Constantinople, and capital of empires for nearly 1,600 years.

    Italian Rule of Rhodes¶

    The period from 1912 to 1943 when Italy held Rhodes and the other Dodecanese islands, building new public buildings and heavily restoring the medieval town.

    Example: The Italian-built buildings around Mandraki harbor date from this time.

    Iznik Tiles¶

    Glazed ceramic tiles made in the town of Iznik in northwest Anatolia, especially in the 16th century, famous for blue, turquoise, green and bright red patterns of tulips, carnations and vines.

    Example: The Rüstem Pasha Mosque near the Spice Bazaar is covered inside with some of the finest.

    Jesus of Nazareth¶

    A Jewish teacher and healer from Galilee, crucified by the Roman authorities around 30 CE, whose followers proclaimed that God had raised him from the dead and that he was the Messiah.

    See also: Early Jesus Movement, Christianity

    Jewish Law¶

    The commandments and teachings of the Torah, the first five books of the Hebrew Bible, together with their interpretation, governing worship, diet, Sabbath, purity and daily life.

    Example: The early church argued over whether Gentile believers had to keep these rules, such as circumcision.

    Jewish Quarter of Rhodes¶

    The eastern part of the walled medieval town, called La Juderia, where the Jewish community of Rhodes lived for centuries until its deportation in 1944.

    John the Apostle¶

    One of the twelve disciples of Jesus, whom later Christian tradition places in Ephesus in old age, caring for Mary and buried on the hill of Ayasuluk.

    Example: The Basilica of St. John in Selçuk was built over his traditional tomb.

    Johnnies and Mehmets Words¶

    A short message of reconciliation, traditionally attributed to Atatürk in 1934, telling the families of Allied soldiers buried at Gallipoli that their sons lie in a friendly country, with no difference between them and the Turkish dead.

    Historians debate the exact wording, but it is central to how Gallipoli is remembered.

    Judaism¶

    The religion and way of life of the Jewish people, based on the covenant with one God described in the Hebrew Bible and on the Torah, its law and teaching. Paul was raised in it and never stopped calling himself a Jew.

    Example: The Kahal Shalom Synagogue in Rhodes keeps alive the memory of a Sephardic Jewish community that lived there for centuries.

    Juderia¶

    Say it: hoo-deh-REE-ah

    La Juderia, the Jewish quarter in the eastern part of the medieval town of Rhodes, settled largely by Sephardic families after 1522.

    Justinian¶

    The Byzantine emperor (ruled 527–565 CE) who reconquered parts of the western empire, codified Roman law and rebuilt Hagia Sophia after the Nika Riots.

    Kafeneio¶

    Say it: kah-feh-NEE-oh

    The traditional Greek coffeehouse, especially in villages, where older men gather to drink coffee, play backgammon, read the news and argue about politics.

    Example: In a Cretan village, the coffeehouse on the square is often the first place to ask directions.

    Kahal Shalom Synagogue¶

    Say it: Kahal Shalom kah-HAHL shah-LOHM

    The synagogue in the Jewish Quarter of Rhodes, built in 1577, the oldest synagogue still in use in Greece, with a small museum of the community's history.

    Example: A wall plaque lists the families of Rhodes who were deported in 1944.

    Kahvaltı¶

    Say it: kah-vahl-TUH

    The Turkish word for breakfast, literally "before coffee," referring to the traditional morning spread of many small dishes with bread and tea.

    Kallimarmaro¶

    Say it: kah-lee-MAR-mah-roh

    The Athenian nickname, "beautiful marble," for the Panathenaic Stadium, an ancient stadium rebuilt in white marble for the first modern Olympic Games in 1896.

    Example: The Olympic flame is still handed over here to each host city.

    Kamares¶

    Say it: kah-MAH-res

    A style of fine Minoan pottery from about 2000 to 1700 BCE, named after a cave on Mount Ida, with thin walls painted white, red and orange on a dark background.

    Kapalıçarşı¶

    Say it: kah-PAH-luh-char-shuh

    The Turkish name for Istanbul's Grand Bazaar, meaning "covered market," from the vaulted roofs that cover its dozens of streets and thousands of shops.

    Kastri¶

    Say it: kah-STREE

    The mountain village, "the little castle," that stood on top of the ruins of Delphi until it was moved in the 1890s to allow excavation.

    Example: Its villagers had reused ancient stones in their house walls.

    See also: French Excavation of Delphi

    Kebab¶

    Any of many Turkish dishes of grilled or roasted meat, such as skewered pieces, minced meat on a skewer or meat sliced from a vertical rotisserie.

    Example: Döner kebab, sliced from a turning spit, is the cousin of Greek gyros.

    Kleroterion¶

    Say it: kleh-roh-TEER-ee-on

    A stone slab cut with rows of narrow slots, used in Athens to choose jurors and officials at random from citizens' name tickets.

    Example: A surviving example is displayed in the Agora Museum.

    See also: Athenian Democracy

    Knights Hospitaller¶

    Say it: Hospitaller HOS-pih-tuh-ler

    A Catholic military order, the Order of St. John, founded to care for pilgrims in Jerusalem, which ruled Rhodes from about 1310 to 1522 and then moved to Malta.

    Example: Each nationality of knights had its own lodging on the Street of the Knights in Rhodes.

    Knossos Palace¶

    Say it: Knossos NOSS-us

    The largest Minoan palace, a complex of about 1,300 rooms around a central court on a hill near Heraklion, occupied for centuries and partly rebuilt by Arthur Evans in the early 1900s.

    Example: Visitors walk past painted copies of frescoes and huge clay storage jars still standing in its storerooms.

    See also: Arthur Evans, Throne Room at Knossos

    Know Thyself¶

    The maxim gnōthi seauton, said to be inscribed on the porch of the Temple of Apollo, urging people to understand their own nature and limits before asking the god anything.

    See also: Nothing in Excess, Values Beyond Prediction

    Knowledge Gatekeepers¶

    People or institutions that control who can create, copy or reach knowledge, such as scribes, priests, guilds, publishers or, today, technology companies.

    See also: Widening the Circle

    Koine Greek¶

    Say it: Koine KOY-nay

    The "common" Greek that spread across the Eastern Mediterranean after Alexander, used for trade, government and writing from Egypt to the Black Sea, and the language of the New Testament.

    Example: Paul could write in it to churches in Galatia, Corinth and Rome and be understood in all of them.

    See also: Shared Language Spreads Ideas

    Komnenos¶

    Say it: kom-NEE-nos

    The name of a Byzantine ruling family that held the throne from 1081 to 1185, restoring the empire's strength for a century.

    Example: A mosaic in Hagia Sophia shows Emperor John II of this family with Empress Irene.

    Koules Fortress¶

    Say it: Koules koo-LESS

    The Venetian sea fortress guarding the entrance to Heraklion's old harbor, built in the 16th century and marked with reliefs of the lion of St. Mark.

    Example: From its roof, visitors look back over the harbor where Venetian galleys once docked.

    Kulliye Mosque Complex¶

    Say it: külliye kewl-lee-YEH

    An Ottoman group of buildings centered on a mosque and paid for by an endowment, including schools, a hospital, a soup kitchen, baths and tombs that served the surrounding neighborhood.

    Example: The Süleymaniye is the largest such complex in Istanbul.

    Kusadasi and Selcuk¶

    Say it: Kuşadası koo-SHAH-dah-suh · Selçuk SEL-chook

    Two neighboring towns near ancient Ephesus: Kuşadası, the seaside resort and cruise port where the ship docks, and Selçuk, the inland market town beside the ruins and the Ephesus Museum.

    Example: Selçuk's weekly market sells local olives, figs and cheeses.

    Labrys¶

    Say it: LAB-ris

    The Minoan double-headed axe, a sacred symbol carved on palace stones and found in shrines on Crete; some link it to the word "labyrinth."

    See also: Labyrinth

    Labyrinth¶

    In legend, the maze built by Daedalus at Knossos to hold the Minotaur; in general, any confusing network of passages. The name may come from labrys, the double axe.

    Example: The palace's many rooms and turning passages may have inspired later Greeks to imagine it as a maze.

    Ladino¶

    Say it: lah-DEE-noh

    The language of the Sephardic Jews, a form of medieval Spanish written in Hebrew letters, which they brought from Spain to the Ottoman Empire and kept alive for more than four hundred years.

    See also: Sephardic Jewish Refugees

    Laiklik¶

    Say it: lah-eek-LEEK

    The Turkish term, from the French laïcité, for the principle that the state and its law are not based on religion.

    See also: Secularism in Turkey

    Large Language Model¶

    A machine-learning system trained on vast amounts of text to predict the next word in a sequence, which lets it write, summarize, translate and answer questions in ordinary language.

    Larnakes¶

    Say it: LAR-nah-kes

    Minoan clay coffins shaped like chests or bathtubs, used from about 1400 to 1200 BCE and painted with octopuses, birds, fish and plants.

    Example: Several painted examples stand in the Heraklion Archaeological Museum.

    Layers of a City¶

    The idea that a long-lived city is built on top of its own earlier versions, so that streets, walls and buildings from many eras sit above, below and inside one another.

    Example: In Istanbul a Byzantine column, an Ottoman fountain and a modern tram stop can share one street corner.

    See also: Stratigraphy, Spolia

    Leadership Reflection¶

    A short written answer, in our own words, to the question of what a day's history means for the changes we are trying to make.

    See also: Travel Journal

    Learning for All¶

    The thread running through this book, a short note at the end of each daily chapter that ties a story from that day's place to the effort to bring a good education to every child on Earth.

    Example: At Ephesus, the note asks who could use the Library of Celsus and who could not.

    See also: Democratization of Education, Widening the Circle

    Lecture to Dialogue¶

    The shift from teaching mainly by a teacher talking to a class toward teaching through questions, discussion and conversation among students and teachers.

    See also: Socratic Method

    Legacy Institution¶

    An organization old and established enough that its structures, habits and identity were inherited from an earlier age, and that resists redesign because many people depend on it working as it does.

    The term borrows from computing, where "legacy systems" are old software still running critical work.

    Example: The book treats the modern school as one.

    Lessons Across the Route¶

    The conclusions drawn by reading all the trip's stories together rather than one at a time, and the final reflection questions they leave for our own work.

    Letter to the Ephesians¶

    A New Testament letter attributed to Paul, possibly written by a later follower, that speaks of the unity of Jews and Gentiles in one church. Some of the oldest manuscripts lack the words "in Ephesus."

    Letter to the Galatians¶

    Paul's letter to the communities of Galatia in central Anatolia, arguing that Gentile believers need not keep the Jewish law and declaring that in Christ "there is neither Jew nor Greek."

    See also: Neither Jew Nor Greek

    Letter to the Romans¶

    Paul's longest letter, written from Corinth around 57 CE to believers in Rome, setting out his thinking on faith, grace, the law and the place of Israel.

    Example: It ends with a list of greetings that begins by commending Phoebe of Cenchreae.

    See also: Grace and Faith, Phoebe

    Letters as Teaching Technology¶

    Paul's use of the letter to teach communities he could not visit in person, read aloud, copied and shared, with the strength of reach and the weakness of no immediate conversation.

    See also: Pauline Letters

    Library as Public Access¶

    A collection of books owned in common and opened to readers who could never afford the books themselves, at a time when every book was a costly handwritten scroll.

    Example: The Library of Celsus at Ephesus offered its city's readers thousands of scrolls they could not have bought.

    See also: Library of Celsus, Philanthropy for Knowledge

    Library of Celsus¶

    The two-story marble library at Ephesus, completed in the early 2nd century CE by Tiberius Julius Aquila in honor of his father Celsus, a governor of Asia, who was buried beneath it.

    Its facade was re-erected by archaeologists in the 1970s.

    Example: Statues in its niches represent Wisdom, Knowledge, Intelligence and Virtue.

    Limits of the Oracle Parallel¶

    The ways Delphi is not like a modern predictive model, and where reading one as a lesson about the other misleads.

    Example: The oracle claimed divine authority, while a model claims only measured probabilities.

    Limits of the Paul Parallel¶

    The ways Paul's mission is not like leading change in a school, and where reading him as a case study misleads.

    Example: Paul's hearers chose to join and could leave, while students and teachers often cannot opt out of a school reform.

    Lindos Acropolis¶

    The rocky citadel above the town of Lindos on Rhodes, crowned by a sanctuary of Athena Lindia and later enclosed by a castle of the Knights Hospitaller.

    Example: From the top there is a view down to St. Paul's Bay, a small round cove below the cliffs.

    Linear A¶

    The undeciphered script of the Minoans, used on Crete from about 1800 to 1450 BCE on clay tablets and ritual objects. Its signs resemble Linear B, but the language it records is unknown.

    Example: Tablets in the Heraklion museum show signs we can sound out but whose meaning no one can read.

    Linear B¶

    The syllable-based script used by Mycenaean palace scribes from about 1450 to 1200 BCE to record an early form of Greek, mainly in lists of goods, people and offerings. It was deciphered in 1952.

    Example: One Knossos tablet lists jars of honey sent to "all the gods."

    See also: Linear A, Decipherment of Linear B

    Literacy¶

    The ability to read and write a language with understanding, and in a wider sense the skills needed to use written information in daily life.

    Example: In Turkey before the 1928 reform, roughly one person in ten could read.

    Literacy Rate Uncertainty¶

    The margin of doubt in any count of how many people could read, which comes from how reading ability was defined, who was counted and who did the counting.

    Example: Turkey's 1927 census figure of about one in ten readers depends on how the question was asked.

    Loan and Hiring Algorithms¶

    Automated systems that score applicants for credit or jobs from past data, making high-stakes decisions about people that can repeat past discrimination if not checked.

    Local Guides¶

    Licensed guides who live in the region and lead visitors through sites, bringing knowledge of history, language and present-day life that a printed guide cannot.

    Example: A guide from Selçuk can point out which marble blocks at Ephesus were placed by modern restorers.

    Lokum¶

    Say it: loh-KOOM

    The Turkish word for Turkish delight, the soft sweet of cooked sugar and starch, flavored with rosewater, fruit or nuts and cut into cubes.

    Example: Shops in the Spice Bazaar offer free samples of pomegranate and pistachio flavors.

    See also: Turkish Delight

    Long-Term Consequences¶

    The effects of a decision that appear years or generations later, which are hard to predict and easy to discount but often matter most.

    Example: The silting of Ephesus's harbor came partly from centuries of forest clearing upriver.

    Loukoumades¶

    Say it: loo-koo-MAH-thes

    Small balls of fried dough soaked in honey or syrup and sprinkled with cinnamon or nuts, a Greek sweet with ancient roots.

    Love as Greatest Virtue¶

    Paul's teaching in 1 Corinthians 13 that among faith, hope and love, love is the greatest, describing it as patient, kind and not boastful.

    Example: The word he uses is agapē, a love of goodwill shown in action.

    Lowering the Cost of Reading¶

    Reducing the time, money and training a person needs before they can read, which widens the circle of people able to read at all.

    Example: The Greek alphabet's two dozen letters took far less time to learn than Linear B's many signs.

    Lyceum¶

    The school Aristotle founded in Athens in 335 BCE in a grove sacred to Apollo Lykeios, where he taught while walking with students. Its remains were found in central Athens in 1996.

    Example: The excavated site lies in a small park near the Byzantine and Christian Museum.

    Machine Learning¶

    A branch of artificial intelligence in which computer systems learn patterns from examples in data, rather than following rules written by hand, and use those patterns to make predictions.

    Mandraki¶

    Say it: man-DRAH-kee

    The old harbor of Rhodes town, beside the medieval walls, whose entrance is marked by two bronze deer on columns.

    Example: Popular legend places the feet of the Colossus here.

    See also: Colossus of Rhodes

    Manumission Records¶

    Inscriptions at Delphi, many on the Polygonal Wall, that record the freeing of enslaved people through a formal sale to Apollo, often with conditions they still had to meet.

    Example: Many name the freed person, the owner and the price, rare records of ordinary enslaved people.

    Marble Street¶

    The paved street of Ephesus that runs from the Library of Celsus north toward the Great Theater, along the edge of the commercial agora.

    Example: A carved footprint on one paving stone is often explained by guides as an ancient advertisement, a claim with little evidence.

    Marcus Musurus¶

    Say it: Musurus moo-SOO-rus

    A Cretan Greek scholar (about 1470–1517) who worked in Venice as chief editor of Greek texts for the printer Aldus Manutius, preparing first printed editions of Plato and other authors.

    Masakhane¶

    Say it: mah-sah-KAH-neh

    A grassroots research community, whose name is isiZulu for "we build together," that brings speakers of African languages together to build translation tools for their own languages.

    See also: Education With Communities, AI Translation

    Mastery Learning¶

    An approach in which students move to a new topic only after showing they have learned the current one, with extra time and help for those who need it, rather than a fixed pace for all.

    Measuring Who Is Left Out¶

    Counting the people a system does not reach, group by group, rather than judging the system only by those it serves.

    Example: By such a count, only about one in six people in classical Athens could vote.

    Mediterranean Climate¶

    A pattern of hot, dry summers and mild, wet winters typical of lands around the Mediterranean Sea, which shapes what grows there, above all olives, grapes and wheat.

    Example: November falls at the start of the rainy season, so olive harvests are under way while some days bring sudden showers.

    Mediterranean Diet¶

    The traditional eating pattern of Mediterranean countries, rich in vegetables, fruit, legumes, whole grains, olive oil and fish, with little red meat, linked by researchers to good heart health.

    Medrese¶

    Say it: med-reh-SEH

    An Islamic college attached to a mosque, where students studied the Quran, law, theology, Arabic and other subjects, often living in small rooms around a courtyard.

    See also: Endowed Free Education

    Medusa Heads¶

    Two large carved stone heads of the Gorgon Medusa, one upside down and one on its side, reused as column bases in the Basilica Cistern.

    See also: Spolia

    Meeting People Where They Are¶

    Starting from the beliefs, concerns, language and experience that people already have, rather than from the leader's own, when introducing a new idea.

    Example: Paul began his Athens speech with their own altar and quoted their own poets.

    See also: Altar to an Unknown God, Stages of Concern

    Megali Idea¶

    Say it: meh-GAH-lee ee-DEH-ah

    The "Great Idea," the 19th- and early 20th-century Greek national goal of uniting all Greek-speaking lands in one state with Constantinople as its capital, abandoned after defeat in 1922.

    Megara¶

    Say it: MEG-uh-ruh

    An ancient Greek city-state on the isthmus between Athens and Corinth, whose colonists founded Byzantium, the future Constantinople, around 657 BCE.

    Example: Megara lies about 40 km west of Athens on the road to Corinth.

    See also: Byzantium

    Mehmed the Conqueror¶

    Say it: Mehmed MEH-met

    The Ottoman sultan Mehmed II (1432–1481), who captured Constantinople in 1453 at age 21, made it his capital and began rebuilding and repopulating it.

    Example: He ordered the building of Topkapı Palace and the Grand Bazaar.

    Mehmetçik¶

    Say it: meh-MET-chik

    The affectionate Turkish name, "little Mehmet," for the ordinary Turkish soldier, used especially for the men who fought and died at Gallipoli in 1915.

    Example: Statues of these soldiers stand near the memorials on the Gallipoli peninsula.

    Menenius Agrippa¶

    Say it: meh-NEE-nee-us uh-GRIP-uh

    A Roman senator who, according to the historian Livy, persuaded the protesting common people of Rome to return in 494 BCE with a fable about limbs that refused to feed the belly.

    See also: One Body Many Parts

    Meze¶

    A selection of small cold and hot dishes in Turkish dining, such as eggplant purée, white cheese, stuffed vine leaves and seafood, shared before a main course, often with raki.

    Mezedes¶

    Say it: meh-ZEH-dhes

    Greek small plates, such as olives, cheese, grilled octopus, meatballs or dips, served to share, often with ouzo or wine.

    See also: Meze, Ouzo

    Michael Ventris¶

    An English architect (1922–1956), not a professional classicist, who deciphered Linear B in 1952 by showing that it recorded an early form of Greek, then worked with John Chadwick to publish the proof.

    Example: His success is the book's leading case of an outsider solving a problem experts had not cracked.

    Migration and Refugees Today¶

    The movement of people into and through Greece and Turkey in recent years, especially refugees from Syria, Afghanistan and Africa, and the emigration of Greeks and Turks seeking work abroad.

    Example: Turkey hosts several million Syrian refugees, many living in Istanbul.

    Mihrab and Minbar¶

    Say it: mihrab MEE-rahb

    Two key features of a mosque interior: the mihrab, a niche in the wall that shows the direction of Mecca, and the minbar, a tall stepped pulpit from which the Friday sermon is given.

    Example: In Hagia Sophia the mihrab sits slightly off-center in the apse because the church did not face Mecca.

    Millet Mektepleri¶

    Say it: mil-LET mek-tep-leh-REE

    The Turkish name for the Nation's Schools, the free adult literacy classes opened across Turkey in 1929 to teach the new alphabet.

    See also: Nation's Schools

    Millet System¶

    The Ottoman practice of organizing non-Muslim subjects into religious communities, such as Orthodox Christians, Armenians and Jews, that ran their own religious, educational and family-law affairs under their leaders.

    Mimar Sinan¶

    Say it: Sinan see-NAHN

    The chief architect of the Ottoman Empire (about 1490–1588), who designed hundreds of buildings, including the Süleymaniye Mosque in Istanbul, and perfected the domed Ottoman mosque.

    Example: He called the Süleymaniye the work of his journeyman years and the Selimiye in Edirne his masterpiece.

    Minaret¶

    The tall slender tower of a mosque from which the call to prayer is given, now usually broadcast by loudspeaker.

    Example: The Blue Mosque is known for its six.

    Minoan Civilization¶

    Say it: Minoan mih-NOH-un

    The Bronze Age culture of Crete, at its height from about 2000 to 1450 BCE, known for large palaces, nature-filled wall paintings, seafaring trade and the undeciphered Linear A script.

    The name was given by Arthur Evans, after the legendary King Minos.

    Example: The Heraklion Archaeological Museum holds the world's greatest collection of this culture's art.

    Minoan Frescoes¶

    Say it: Minoan mih-NOH-un

    The wall paintings of Bronze Age Crete, painted on wet plaster in bright colors with scenes of nature, ritual, processions, sea creatures and young men and women.

    Example: The Prince of the Lilies and the dolphin fresco are shown as copies at Knossos and in their original fragments in Heraklion.

    See also: Bull-Leaping Fresco, Fresco

    Model Calibration¶

    How well the probabilities a forecaster gives match how often events actually happen; a well-matched forecaster sees rain on about 70 percent of the days it says "70 percent."

    See also: Brier Score, Track Record and Trust

    Modern Delphi Village¶

    The small town just west of the ancient sanctuary, built in the 1890s when the village of Kastri was moved off the ruins to allow excavation, and now a base for visitors.

    See also: Kastri, French Excavation of Delphi

    Modern Greek State¶

    The independent Greek country formed after the war of independence, first a small kingdom in 1832 and later expanding to include Crete, the Dodecanese and other territories.

    See also: Megali Idea, Dodecanese Union With Greece

    Morosini Fountain¶

    The Venetian fountain in Heraklion's Lions Square, built in 1628 under the governor Francesco Morosini, with four stone lions spouting water into a basin.

    Example: It is a favorite meeting spot, surrounded by cafés selling bougatsa pastry.

    Mosaic¶

    A picture or pattern made by setting many small pieces of colored stone, glass or tile, called tesserae, into plaster or mortar on a floor, wall or ceiling.

    Example: The gold-glass portraits of Christ, Mary and John the Baptist in Hagia Sophia's upper gallery are among the finest Byzantine examples.

    Mosque Etiquette¶

    The customs visitors follow in a mosque: removing shoes, dressing modestly, women covering their hair, speaking quietly, not walking in front of people praying and avoiding visits during prayer.

    See also: Dress at Sacred Sites

    Mother-Tongue Learning¶

    Teaching and learning in the language a child speaks at home, especially in the early years, so that learning to read and learning new ideas need not wait until a second language is mastered.

    Mount Parnassus¶

    The mountain in central Greece, about 2,457 meters high, on whose southern slopes the sanctuary of Delphi stands, sacred in myth to Apollo and the Muses.

    Example: Snow can appear on its upper slopes by late November.

    Multi-Faith Empire¶

    A state that rules people of many religions and allows them to practice their faiths, usually with unequal rights, as the Ottoman Empire did with Muslims, Christians and Jews.

    Example: In 19th-century Istanbul, mosques, Orthodox churches and synagogues stood within a short walk of one another.

    Mustafa Kemal Ataturk¶

    Say it: Atatürk ah-tah-TEWRK

    The Ottoman officer who led the defense at Gallipoli and the Turkish War of Independence and became the founding president of the Republic of Turkey (1881–1938).

    Example: His portrait hangs in shops, offices and classrooms across Turkey.

    See also: Ataturk's Reforms, Anıtkabir

    Mycenaean Greeks¶

    Say it: Mycenaean my-suh-NEE-un

    The Bronze Age Greek-speaking people of the mainland, about 1600 to 1100 BCE, who built fortified palaces at Mycenae, Pylos and elsewhere and later took control of Knossos on Crete.

    They are the likely model for Homer's Achaeans who besieged Troy.

    Example: Their palace scribes wrote early Greek in Linear B on clay tablets found at both Pylos and Knossos.

    See also: Linear B, Ahhiyawa

    Myth of the Minotaur¶

    The Greek legend of a monster, half man and half bull, kept by King Minos of Crete in a maze and fed with young Athenians until the hero Theseus killed it with Ariadne's help.

    Example: Visitors to Knossos often look for the maze of the story in the palace's confusing corridors.

    See also: Labyrinth, Greek Mythology

    Myth Versus History¶

    The distinction between traditional stories told to explain origins, gods or heroes and accounts of the past that can be checked against evidence. Many sites on our route sit where the two overlap.

    Example: The Trojan Horse belongs to legend, while the burned layers of Troy VIIa are physical evidence of a real destruction.

    Nation's Schools¶

    The free adult literacy classes opened across Turkey on January 1, 1929, to teach the new Latin-based alphabet to the whole adult population.

    See also: Millet Mektepleri, Literacy Rate Uncertainty

    National Archaeological Museum¶

    The largest museum of ancient Greek art in the world, in central Athens, with collections from prehistory to late antiquity, including Mycenaean gold and the Antikythera mechanism.

    Example: The gold mask that Schliemann called "the mask of Agamemnon" is displayed here.

    Neither Jew Nor Greek¶

    Paul's declaration in Galatians 3:28 that in Christ the divisions between Jews and Greeks, slaves and free people, and male and female no longer determine who belongs, an early statement of shared standing.

    New Model Becomes Legacy¶

    The pattern in which a successful reform, once widely adopted, eventually hardens into the established system that a later generation of reformers must change.

    Example: The early house churches became, over centuries, the imperial church of Hagia Sophia.

    New Testament¶

    The second part of the Christian Bible, 27 writings in Koine Greek: four gospels, Acts, letters attributed to Paul and others, and Revelation.

    Example: The Book of Revelation addresses seven churches in Asia, starting with Ephesus.

    Next-Word Prediction¶

    The basic task a large language model is trained on: given the words so far, estimate which word or word-piece is most likely to come next, repeated to produce whole passages.

    Example: Given "The oracle of," the model rates "Delphi" far more likely than "Denver."

    Nika Riots¶

    The uprising of 532 CE in Constantinople, begun by rival chariot-racing fans, that burned much of the city center and was crushed by massacring about 30,000 people in the Hippodrome.

    Example: The fire destroyed the earlier Hagia Sophia, giving Justinian the chance to build the present one.

    See also: Site Museum

    Troy VI and VIIa¶

    The two settlement levels at Troy dated to the late Bronze Age, about 1700 to 1180 BCE, the main candidates for the city of the Trojan War. Troy VI had massive sloping walls; Troy VIIa ended in fire.

    Example: The smooth, angled stone walls near the site entrance belong to Troy VI.

    Tsikoudia¶

    Say it: tsee-koo-DYAH

    The clear Cretan spirit distilled from grape skins and seeds left after winemaking, often offered free at the end of a meal.

    Example: A taverna in Heraklion may bring a small carafe of it with fruit after dinner.

    Turkish Bath¶

    A public steam bath in the Ottoman tradition, with a hot marble platform, warm washing rooms and scrubbing and soaping by attendants, descended from the Roman baths.

    See also: Hamam, Roman Bath Culture

    Turkish Breakfast¶

    The traditional morning meal of many small dishes, such as cheeses, olives, tomatoes, cucumbers, eggs, honey and jam, eaten with bread and tea.

    Turkish Coffee¶

    Very finely ground coffee boiled slowly in a small pot with water and, if wanted, sugar, served unfiltered with the grounds settling in the cup. UNESCO lists its culture as intangible heritage.

    See also: Greek Coffee

    Turkish Cuisine¶

    The cooking of Turkey, drawing on Central Asian, Anatolian, Ottoman, Arab and Mediterranean traditions, with grilled meats, vegetables cooked in olive oil, yogurt, bread, rice and rich sweets.

    See also: Ottoman Palace Kitchen, Meze

    Turkish Delight¶

    A soft, chewy sweet made by cooking sugar and starch, often flavored with rosewater, lemon or fruit and sometimes filled with nuts, cut into cubes and dusted with powdered sugar.

    Example: We bought several kinds at the Spice Bazaar.

    See also: Lokum

    Turkish Economy Today¶

    The present state of Turkey's economy, a large manufacturing, agricultural and tourism economy marked in recent years by high inflation and a falling lira that raise the cost of living.

    Example: Menu prices in Istanbul may change between one season and the next.

    Turkish Hospitality¶

    The strong Turkish custom of welcoming guests generously, often with tea, food and conversation, rooted in rural and religious traditions that treat a guest as a gift.

    Example: A carpet seller may offer a glass of tea before any talk of buying, and accepting it is polite.

    Turkish Language¶

    The Turkic language of modern Turkey, brought to Anatolia from Central Asia from the 11th century. It was written in Arabic script until 1928 and in Latin letters since.

    Example: Signs in Istanbul use letters such as ç, ş and ğ, added to the Latin alphabet in the 1928 reform.

    Turkish Tea¶

    Black tea grown mainly on Turkey's Black Sea coast, brewed strong in a two-level pot and served in small tulip-shaped glasses, the country's everyday drink.

    Example: Shopkeepers in the Grand Bazaar send out for trays of it for visiting customers.

    Turkish War of Independence¶

    The struggle from 1919 to 1923 by Turkish nationalists under Mustafa Kemal against occupying Allied and Greek forces and the treaty that would have divided Anatolia.

    See also: Sèvres, Treaty of Lausanne

    Uluburun¶

    Say it: oo-loo-boo-ROON

    A rocky point near Kaş on Turkey's southern coast, where a merchant ship sank around 1300 BCE; its wreck, found in 1982, carried copper, tin, glass and luxury goods from many lands.

    Example: Its cargo shows how far Bronze Age trade networks reached.

    See also: Trade Routes

    UNESCO World Heritage Site¶

    A place listed by the United Nations cultural agency as having outstanding value to all humanity, which brings international recognition and commits the host country to protect it.

    Example: The historic areas of Istanbul, the Acropolis, Ephesus, Delphi and the Medieval City of Rhodes all hold this status.

    Üsküdar¶

    Say it: EWS-kew-dar

    A large residential district on the Asian shore of the Bosphorus, facing the historic peninsula, reached by ferry from the European side.

    Vakif Endowment¶

    Say it: vakıf vah-KUHF

    An Islamic charitable foundation in which property such as shops or farmland is given permanently and its income pays for mosques, schools, soup kitchens, fountains or hospitals.

    Example: Rent from shops in the Grand Bazaar paid for the upkeep of nearby religious buildings.

    Values Beyond Prediction¶

    The principle that a prediction can tell us what is likely to happen if we act, but not what we ought to want; choosing what matters is a question of values no model can answer for us.

    Venetian Crete¶

    The period from the early 13th century to 1669 when Crete was a colony of Venice, ruled from the city of Candia, and Greek and Italian cultures mixed in art, building and literature.

    Venetian Walls of Heraklion¶

    Say it: Heraklion heh-RAH-klee-on

    The massive bastioned walls, about 4.5 km around, that Venice built around Candia in the 16th and 17th centuries, which held off the Ottoman army for more than twenty years.

    Example: Nikos Kazantzakis is buried on the Martinengo Bastion at their southern edge.

    Veteran Teacher Champions¶

    Experienced, respected teachers who adopt a change early and help colleagues through it, the school's version of the insider change leader.

    Viking Star Cruise¶

    The seven-night sailing on Viking's small ocean ship from Istanbul to Piraeus, November 14 to 21, 2026, which carries us between the middle stops of the trip.

    The ship holds about 930 adult guests, small by modern cruise standards.

    Example: The ship sails from Galataport in Istanbul at 6:00 PM on November 15, just after sunset.

    Vitruvius¶

    Say it: vih-TROO-vee-us

    A Roman architect and engineer who wrote On Architecture around 25 BCE, the only complete book on architecture to survive from antiquity and the main source for the rules of the column styles.

    See also: Classical Orders

    Weather Forecasting¶

    Predicting future atmospheric conditions using observations and computer models of the atmosphere, usually stated as probabilities, one of the most tested kinds of prediction.

    See also: Model Calibration, Prediction Horizon

    Who Democracy Left Out¶

    The people living under Athenian democracy who had no share in it: all women, metics (resident foreigners), enslaved people and, in practice, many poor citizens far from the city.

    See also: Athenian Citizenship, Measuring Who Is Left Out

    Who Funds the Model¶

    The question of who pays to build and run a prediction system, and how that payment might shape whose interests it serves.

    Example: Croesus's gifts bought his Lydians the right to consult Delphi first and without fees.

    See also: Bribery of the Oracle, Fees and Sacrifice

    Who Is Hurt by Change¶

    The question a leader must ask about every change: who loses, how much, and whether the loss was intended, foreseen, shared fairly or could be reduced.

    Example: Paul's mission cost the silversmiths income, and his words were later turned against Jews for centuries.

    See also: Evangelism's Shadow

    Who Pays for Learning¶

    The question of where the cost of an education falls: on the learner's family, a patron, the state, or a teacher who gives time for free.

    Example: Athens tried every answer: the Sophists charged fees, while Socrates charged nothing.

    See also: Sophists, Endowed Free Education

    Widening the Circle¶

    The process by which access to learning spreads to more people than before, usually because a gatekeeper is bypassed or a gate is opened.

    Example: The alphabet, the endowed school and the printing press each widened who could read.

    Wilusa¶

    Say it: wih-LOO-sah

    A kingdom in western Anatolia named in Hittite records of the 14th and 13th centuries BCE, which most scholars identify with Troy, the Greek Wilios or Ilios.

    Example: Hittite letters describe a quarrel over this place with the king of Ahhiyawa, likely the Mycenaean Greeks.

    Wooden Walls Prophecy¶

    The oracle's answer to Athens before the Persian invasion of 480 BCE that only a "wooden wall" would not fall and that "divine Salamis" would destroy many sons of women.

    Example: Themistocles argued it meant the fleet, and the Greeks won at Salamis.

    See also: Themistocles, Battle of Salamis

    World War I¶

    The global war of 1914 to 1918 in which the Ottoman Empire fought alongside Germany against Britain, France and Russia, leading to the empire's defeat and breakup.

    See also: Gallipoli Campaign

    World War II¶

    The global war of 1939 to 1945, during which Greece was invaded and occupied by Germany and Italy and the Jews of Greece, including those of Rhodes, were deported to death camps. Turkey stayed neutral until early 1945.

    Writing Systems¶

    Sets of visible signs used to record language, including picture-based scripts, syllabaries where each sign stands for a syllable, and alphabets where each sign stands for a single sound.

    Example: Linear B used about 90 syllable signs, while the Greek alphabet needed only 24 letters.

    Yerebatan¶

    Say it: yeh-reh-bah-TAHN

    The Turkish name for the Basilica Cistern in Istanbul, Yerebatan Sarayı, "the Sunken Palace," referring to its forest of columns underground.
