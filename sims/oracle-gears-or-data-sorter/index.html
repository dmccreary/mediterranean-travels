    Oracle, Gears, or Data?¶

    Run the Oracle, Gears, or Data? MicroSim Fullscreen
    Edit in the p5.js Editor

    About This MicroSim¶

    People have asked about the future in three very different ways, and our trip has met all three. At Delphi, a person, the Pythia, gave an answer that the people asking had to interpret. In the National Archaeological Museum, the Antikythera mechanism turned centuries of counted sky records into gear teeth, so that anyone turning the crank got the same answer. On our phones, a learned model adjusts its own rules from millions of past examples.

    This MicroSim gives twelve predictions, four of each kind, and asks one question: how was each answer produced? The three bins are defined on screen:

    Bin How the answer is produced
    Oracle A person gives an answer that the people asking must interpret.
    Cycle model A rule built from regular cycles counted in past records, fixed in advance and the same for everyone.
    Learned model A rule that a computer adjusted for itself from a very large number of past examples.
    Prediction Bin
    Athens's envoys are told to trust the "wooden wall" (480 BCE). Oracle
    Croesus is told that if he attacks Persia, he will destroy a great empire. Oracle
    King Laius is told that his son will kill him. Oracle
    Chaerephon is told that no one is wiser than Socrates. Oracle
    The mechanism's lower back dial marks a possible eclipse in a month 18 years from now. Cycle model
    A Babylonian scribe's clay tablet lists the months, years ahead, when an eclipse may occur. Cycle model
    The mechanism's games dial shows that the Olympic Games fall in this year. Cycle model
    A stone peg calendar says that a certain star rises today and that a west wind will blow. Cycle model
    A phone keyboard suggests the next word of a message home. Learned model
    A bank's fraud system flags a card payment in Arachova as possibly not ours. Learned model
    A chatbot answers the question "Will the museum be open on Monday?" Learned model
    A map app predicts how long the drive back from Delphi will take. Learned model

    The cards are shuffled once each time the page loads. The lesson is in the middle bin: a bronze dial, a clay tablet, and a stone calendar end up together because the method, counting a cycle, decides the bin, not the material or the age of the object.

    Notes on the evidence. The four oracle stories are the ones told in Chapter 17. The eclipse dial on the mechanism's lower back is a spiral of 223 lunar months, the Saros cycle, and the games dial on the upper back follows the four-year cycle of the Panhellenic games, as read from the 2005 X-ray scans. Babylonian astronomers predicted possible eclipse months from cycles of this kind, and the parapegma paired star risings with expected weather. The four modern cards are the everyday predictions of Chapter 19.

    How to Use¶

    1. Read the question and the three bin definitions. All twelve cards start in the tray at the top.
    2. Click a card to read it in full in the white panel. Then drag it into a bin, or press one of the three colored bin buttons (or keys 1, 2, 3). The next card is selected automatically.
    3. A placed card can be dragged to another bin, or back to the tray, until you press Check. Arrow keys move the selection.
    4. When all twelve are placed, press Check (or Enter). Each card is marked ✓ or ✗, and the panel explains how the selected card's answer was produced. The score "First attempt: n of 12" is fixed by this first Check.
    5. Move the red cards and press Check again for practice. Practice checks do not change the first-attempt score. Reset clears every placement but keeps the same card order.

    Lesson Plan¶

    Audience: adult travelers reading Chapter 20, "Day 13: Athens, Museums, Tutors, and the City Today", after visiting the National Archaeological Museum.
    Duration: 10 minutes.
    Prerequisites: the oracle consultation and the Pythia (Chapters 17 and 18); the Antikythera mechanism, the Saros cycle, the four-year games cycle, Babylonian eclipse records, and the parapegma (Chapter 20, Part One); machine learning and the predictive model (Chapter 19).
    Learning objective (Bloom: Analyze): Differentiate twelve predictions by how each answer is produced, as an oracle's answer, a cycle model, or a learned model, sorting at least 10 of the 12 correctly on the first attempt.

    Activities¶

    1. Predict (1 min): Before starting, decide which bin the Antikythera mechanism belongs in, and why it is neither an oracle nor a learned model.
    2. Sort (6 min): Place all twelve cards and press Check once. Note the first-attempt score, then fix the red cards.
    3. Discuss (3 min): Which cards did the material or the age of the source tempt you to misplace? What can a learned model do that the gears could not, and what could the gears do that the Pythia could not?

    Assessment¶

    Mastery is at least 10 of 12 cards in the correct bin on the first Check. A follow-up question: A weather app forecasts rain for tomorrow. Which bin does it belong in, and what would you need to know to be sure? A strong answer says it depends on the method: a model fitted to many past observations is a learned model, while a fixed rule such as "rain follows this star's rising" is a cycle model, and the material of the device does not decide.

    References¶

    1. Herodotus, Histories 7.141 (Perseus Digital Library). The second oracle to the Athenians in 480 BCE and its "wooden wall".
    2. Herodotus, Histories 1.53 (Perseus Digital Library). The answer to Croesus that he would destroy a great empire.
    3. Sophocles, Oedipus Tyrannus 711–714 (Perseus Digital Library). Jocasta recalls the oracle that Laius would die at the hands of his son.
    4. Plato, Apology 21a (Perseus Digital Library). Chaerephon asks Delphi whether anyone is wiser than Socrates.
    5. T. Freeth and others, "Decoding the ancient Greek astronomical calculator known as the Antikythera Mechanism," Nature 444 (2006): 587–591 (doi:10.1038/nature05357). The 223-month Saros spiral on the lower back dial.
    6. T. Freeth, A. Jones, J. M. Steele and Y. Bitsakis, "Calendars with Olympiad display and eclipse prediction on the Antikythera Mechanism," Nature 454 (2008): 614–617 (doi:10.1038/nature07130). The four-year games dial and the Babylonian roots of the eclipse predictions.
    7. Daryn Lehoux, Astronomy, Weather, and Calendars in the Ancient World: Parapegmata and Related Texts in Classical and Near-Eastern Societies (Cambridge University Press, 2007). The stone peg calendars and their weather signs.
    8. Saros (astronomy) — Wikipedia. The 223-month eclipse cycle of about 18 years and its Babylonian use.
    9. Antikythera mechanism — Wikipedia. Overview of the device, its dials, and the research history.
