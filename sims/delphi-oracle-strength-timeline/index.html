    Eleven Centuries of the Oracle¶

    Run the Eleven Centuries of the Oracle MicroSim Fullscreen

    About This MicroSim¶

    The Oracle of Delphi answered questions from at least the 8th century BCE until the Theodosian Decrees of 391–392 CE banned sacrifice and closed the temples. Counting across the line between BCE and CE (there was no year zero), 750 BCE to 391 CE is 750 + 391 − 1 = 1,140 years. Its peak came in the 6th and 5th centuries BCE, when cities asked it where to found colonies and kings and allied Greek states filled the sanctuary with gifts. Its decline was not sudden, and it did not begin with Christianity.

    This MicroSim shows fourteen dated events one at a time, deliberately out of time order. For each, we decide whether it shows the oracle's strength or a weakness or decline. Feedback gives the reason, and the event drops onto a timeline from 800 BCE to 450 CE in the row of its correct category. Each category has three cues, not only color: its own row, a triangle (▲ for strength, ▼ for weakness), and a color. A shaded band marks the oracle's 1,140-year life.

    # Date Event Category
    1 c. 750 BCE The sanctuary grows; the earliest dedications accumulate Strength
    2 c. 630 BCE Settlers from Thera found Cyrene after repeated answers from Delphi Strength
    3 548 BCE The temple burns; the Greek world and the king of Egypt pay to rebuild it Strength (trap)
    4 c. 550 BCE Croesus sends a gold lion and great mixing bowls Strength
    5 c. 510 BCE The Alcmaeonids bribe the Pythia, Herodotus says Weakness (trap)
    6 479 BCE The Greek allies dedicate the Serpent Column after Plataea Strength
    7 279 BCE A Gaulish army attacks Delphi and is driven off Strength (trap)
    8 356–346 BCE The Phocians seize Delphi and melt its treasures Weakness
    9 c. 87–86 BCE Sulla takes the remaining valuables Weakness
    10 44 BCE Cicero writes that the oracle's answers are long silent and despised Weakness
    11 67 CE Nero carries off about 500 bronze statues Weakness
    12 c. 100 CE Plutarch: one Pythia is now enough where three were needed Weakness
    13 by 330 CE Constantine moves the Serpent Column to Constantinople Weakness
    14 391–392 CE The Theodosian Decrees end sacrifice; the Pythia falls silent Weakness

    Three cards are traps. The fire of 548 BCE was damage, but the rebuilding that followed shows how much the whole world valued the oracle. The Alcmaeonid bribery happened at the height of the oracle's power, but shows its voice could be bought. The defeat of the Gauls in 279 BCE is late, but it added to the god's prestige. After card 14, a final question (not scored) asks when the signs of weakness begin to cluster: from the Third Sacred War, about 360 BCE, they run one way, yet the end took more than 700 more years.

    What is uncertain. Several dates are approximate and are shown with "c.": the beginning of the oracle (legend and some archaeology push it earlier than 750 BCE), the foundation of Cyrene, and the date of Croesus' gifts, which Herodotus places before the fire of 548 BCE. The Alcmaeonid bribery and the Gauls' defeat by snow and earthquake are Herodotus' and later writers' reports, not independent records. The famous "last oracle" said to have been given to the emperor Julian (361–363 CE), announcing that Apollo's hall had fallen, survives only in much later Christian writers and is best treated as a legend; the silence after 391–392 is what the evidence supports.

    Some modern writers have linked the oracle's ups and downs to geology. The geologist Jelle Zeilinga de Boer and the archaeologist John Hale, with the chemist Jeffrey Chanton and the toxicologist Henry Spiller, argued around 2001 that faults cross beneath the temple and that light gases, possibly ethylene, once rose through them, with earthquakes opening and closing their path. The faults are widely accepted, but later measurements found little or no ethylene and critics doubt any gas could have caused a trance, so the gas explanation remains debated. This timeline does not depend on it: every event here is political, financial, or religious.

    How to Use¶

    1. Read the card: its number, date, and event. The cards are not in time order.
    2. Choose Shows strength or Shows a weakness or decline (or press keys 1 and 2). Only the first answer counts.
    3. Read the feedback. The card appears on the timeline in its correct row; a red dashed border marks a card you missed.
    4. Press Next card (or Enter). After card 14, press Final question and answer it with the buttons or keys 1 to 4.
    5. Use Full span and Close-up: 750–250 BCE to change the view, and drag the timeline to move it. Hover over or click a card to read it again.
    6. Try Again clears all fourteen cards.

    Lesson Plan¶

    Audience: adult travelers preparing to visit Delphi.
    Duration: 10–15 minutes.
    Prerequisites: the Oracle of Delphi, Greek colonization, Croesus of Lydia, the Temple of Apollo, bribery of the oracle, Delphic neutrality, the Amphictyonic League and the Sacred Wars, Plutarch, the decline and closing of the oracle, and the oracle's long lifespan (Chapter 17).
    Learning objective (Bloom: Analyze): Distinguish events that show the Oracle of Delphi's strength from events that show a weakness or decline, classifying at least 12 of 14 dated events correctly on the first attempt.

    Activities¶

    1. Predict (2 min): Before card 1, guess when the oracle's decline began. Many people guess "when Christianity arrived."
    2. Classify (8 min): For each card, ask what the event says about the institution, not just whether something bad happened.
    3. Read the shape (3 min): After card 14, look at where the ▼ markers crowd together and answer the final question.
    4. Discuss (2 min): Compare the oracle's 1,140 years with the Byzantine Empire (330–1453, 1,123 years). What kept people climbing the mountain after the treasures were gone?

    Assessment¶

    Mastery is at least 12 of 14 correct on the first attempt, with at least two of the three trap cards (3, 5 and 7) correct. As a follow-up, ask: Did the oracle decline suddenly when Christianity arrived? A strong answer says no: signs of decline begin with the Third Sacred War in 356–346 BCE, about 700 years before the Theodosian Decrees, and even then there were revivals, such as the defeat of the Gauls and the new building Plutarch saw around 100 CE.
