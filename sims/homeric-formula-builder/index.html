    Finish the Line Like a Homeric Singer¶

    Run Finish the Line Like a Homeric Singer Fullscreen

    About This MicroSim¶

    Homer's heroes come with fixed name phrases: "swift-footed Achilles," "much-enduring Odysseus." In the 1920s the American scholar Milman Parry showed that the choice between them follows the meter, not the meaning. The Iliad and the Odyssey are composed in dactylic hexameter, a line of six feet made of long and short syllables, and each name formula fills a different-sized space at the end of that line. For each hero there are three:

    Hero Formula Translation Syllables Pattern
    Achilles dios Achilleus brilliant Achilles 5 – ⏑ ⏑ – –
    Achilles podas ōkus Achilleus swift of feet, Achilles 7 ⏑ ⏑ – ⏑ ⏑ – –
    Achilles podarkēs dios Achilleus swift-footed, brilliant Achilles 8 ⏑ – – – ⏑ ⏑ – –
    Odysseus dios Odysseus brilliant Odysseus 5 – ⏑ ⏑ – –
    Odysseus polymētis Odysseus Odysseus of many counsels 7 ⏑ ⏑ – ⏑ ⏑ – –
    Odysseus polytlas dios Odysseus much-enduring, brilliant Odysseus 8 ⏑ – – – ⏑ ⏑ – –

    (– long, ⏑ short.) The MicroSim draws each line to scale on a six-foot ruler, counting a long syllable as two beats and a short as one, so a foot is four beats. The opening of the line is shown as already sung; the empty slots at the end are the space the singer must fill. When a formula is chosen, its syllables drop into the space. The right one fits exactly. A formula that is too long runs past the end of the line, and one that is too short leaves a gap.

    Four of the six lines are real Homeric lines, given in the chapter's English paraphrase: Iliad 1.84, Odyssey 6.1, Iliad 1.121 and Iliad 1.7. Lines 2 and 6 use real formulas with openings typical of Homer and are labeled "pattern example." The long and short marks are simplified: the last syllable of a line, which can be long or short, is always shown as long.

    How to Use¶

    1. Read the line's English opening and the hero's name, and count the empty slots at the end of the line.
    2. Compare the three formula cards, which show each formula split into syllables, and press the button for the one with the same number of syllables (or press 1, 2 or 3).
    3. A right choice completes the line in green and shows the full English line with its source. A wrong choice shows where the syllables fail to fit and tells you how to count. You get two tries; after a second miss the right formula is placed for you.
    4. Press Next line (or Enter) to go on. The counter at the top shows lines composed on the first try.
    5. After line 6, answer the last question: why is Achilles "swift of feet" while he sits in his hut? It is not scored.
    6. Start over begins again at line 1.

    Lesson Plan¶

    Audience: adult travelers preparing for Day 6 at Troy.
    Duration: 10–15 minutes.
    Prerequisites: oral tradition, formula, dactylic hexameter, long and short syllables, Achilles and Odysseus.
    Learning objective (Bloom: Apply): Use the size of the metrical space left at the end of a line to choose the Homeric name formula that completes it, for six lines naming Achilles or Odysseus, with at least 5 of 6 correct on the first attempt.

    Activities¶

    1. Count first (2 min): On line 1, count the empty slots aloud before looking at the buttons.
    2. Compose (6 min): Finish all six lines. Notice that lines 1 and 2 have the same space but different heroes, and lines 3 and 4 the same.
    3. Answer the last question (2 min): Explain in one sentence why Achilles is "swift of feet" while sitting still.
    4. Connect (3 min): Using the chapter's section on Parry and Lord, explain why a singer who stored formulas, not a fixed text, could compose at the speed of performance.

    Assessment¶

    Mastery is at least 5 of 6 lines composed on the first try. A follow-up question: If a singer needed to end a line about Odysseus with seven syllables, how many choices would the tradition give him? One: polymētis Odysseus. For each hero and each space there is usually just one formula, which is the economy Parry discovered.

    Common misconceptions this activity targets:

    • Homer chose each epithet for its meaning at that moment, so "swift-footed" means Achilles is running.
    • The singers memorized a fixed text word for word.
    • Any adjective could fill any gap.

    4. Epithets in Homer — Wikipedia. Homeric epithets, including podas ōkus and polymētis, and their metrical role.
