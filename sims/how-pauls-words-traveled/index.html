    How Paul's Words Traveled¶

    Run the How Paul's Words Traveled MicroSim Fullscreen

    About This MicroSim¶

    Paul's letters did not carry one message down the centuries. The same few chapters of Romans and Galatians fed movements that freed people and arguments that harmed them. This MicroSim lays out twelve moments from 386 to 1999 on a timeline with four strands:

    Strand What belongs in it
    Grace and faith Events in which Paul's teaching that people are put right with God by grace, through faith, was received or argued over
    Conscience and equality Events in which Paul's words were used for, or against, individual conscience or the equal worth of people
    Contempt and its repudiation Events in Christian treatment of Jews that drew on, or later corrected, readings of Paul
    Rereading Paul Scholarly work that changed how Paul is read

    Every card's date is fixed. The only choice is the strand, so the task is to break each event down to the Pauline idea it carried. Martin Luther appears three times, in three different strands, which is the clearest picture of what Chapter 11 calls Paul's contested legacy. The contempt strand turns toward repudiation only after the Holocaust, shown as a gray band.

    To keep events a few years apart from piling on top of each other, the axis hides three long stretches with no events (430–1500, 1560–1725, and 1820–1925). Hatched marks show where each gap falls, so distances on the axis are not to scale across them.

    Before the strands: how the letters themselves traveled¶

    None of these twelve events could have happened unless Paul's letters had first survived as letters. Paul wrote to particular communities, but his letters were read beyond them: Colossians asks that it be passed to the church at Laodicea (Colossians 4:16), and 2 Peter, probably written in the early 2nd century, already speaks of "all his letters" (2 Peter 3:15–16). By about 140 CE, Marcion of Sinope had assembled a collection of ten letters of Paul. The oldest surviving copy of a collection is Papyrus 46, a papyrus codex (a bound book, not a scroll) now divided between the Chester Beatty Library in Dublin and the University of Michigan. It is usually dated about 200 CE, though proposals range from the late 2nd to the 3rd century, and an early date near 80 CE put forward in 1988 has not won acceptance. It contains Romans, Hebrews, 1 and 2 Corinthians, Ephesians, Galatians, Philippians, Colossians, and part of 1 Thessalonians; its last leaves are lost, so whether it ever held 2 Thessalonians, Philemon, or the letters to Timothy and Titus is debated. Lists of accepted books followed: the Muratorian fragment names thirteen letters of Paul and is usually dated to the late 2nd century (a minority of scholars place it in the 4th), and in 367 CE Athanasius of Alexandria's Easter letter listed the 27 books of today's New Testament, including fourteen letters under Paul's name, counting Hebrews. Augustine's book in the garden in Milan, the first card on the timeline, was a copy at the end of that chain.

    How to Use¶

    1. Read the four strand definitions in the key under the timeline. The colored lanes on the timeline are the same four strands.
    2. Choose an event card in the tray. Its full text and date appear in the panel at the top.
    3. Place the card: drag it onto a strand, or click a strand on the timeline while the card is selected. Keys 1 to 4 also place the selected card, and the arrow keys move between cards. A numbered chip appears in that lane at the event's date. We can move a card as often as we like.
    4. When all twelve are placed, press Check. Correct chips turn green; wrong ones turn red, and a dashed green chip appears in the strand where the event belongs. Luther's three events get gold rings.
    5. Select any card to read why it belongs where it does. Try again returns every card to the tray in the same order.

    Lesson Plan¶

    Audience: adult travelers who have read Chapter 11 before the voyage to Rhodes.
    Duration: 10–15 minutes.
    Prerequisites: grace and faith, individual conscience, the idea of human equality, Christian anti-Judaism, the New Perspective on Paul, Augustine of Hippo, and the Protestant Reformation (all in Chapter 11).
    Learning objective (Bloom: Analyze): Organize twelve dated events from 386 CE to 1999 into four strands of Paul's later influence, placing at least 10 of 12 in the correct strand.

    Activities¶

    1. Predict (2 min): Before placing anything, guess which strand will hold the most events, and whether any one person will appear in more than one.
    2. Sort (6 min): For each card, ask one question: which of Paul's ideas did this event carry, or fight over? Grace, conscience and equality, the treatment of Jews, or the way scholars read him.
    3. Check and read (4 min): Press Check and read the reason for every red chip. Notice the two easy confusions: Luther at Worms is about conscience, not grace, and Stendahl's essay is about conscience but is scholarship about Paul, so it belongs in Rereading Paul.
    4. Look at the pattern (3 min): Follow the gold rings across three lanes. Then follow the contempt strand from Chrysostom to Nostra Aetate and notice where the gray Holocaust band falls.

    Assessment¶

    Mastery is 10 of 12 correct on the first Check. As a follow-up, ask: Is it fair to call Paul's influence good or bad? A strong answer uses the timeline: the same letters fed the Reformation, the defense of conscience, and the teaching of contempt, and the repudiation of that teaching in 1965 quoted Paul's own Romans 11.
