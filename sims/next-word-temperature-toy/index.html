    Next-Word Prediction and Temperature¶

    Run the Next-Word Prediction and Temperature MicroSim Fullscreen
    Edit in the p5.js Editor

    About This MicroSim¶

    A language model writes one word at a time. For each next word it assigns a probability to every candidate, chooses one, adds it to the text, and repeats. The temperature setting controls how that choice is made. This MicroSim gives an imaginary small model four prompts, each with five candidate words, and lets us predict what the temperature will do before we see it.

    The rule is the standard softmax-with-temperature rescaling. Raise each probability to the power \( 1/T \), where \( T \) is the temperature, and rescale so the five add up to 100 percent:

    \[ p_i(T) = \frac{p_i^{1/T}}{\sum_j p_j^{1/T}} \]

    This is the same as dividing the model's raw scores (its logits, here \( \ln p_i \)) by \( T \) before the softmax step. At \( T = 1 \) nothing changes. Below 1 the probabilities sharpen toward the favorite; above 1 they flatten. As \( T \) approaches 0 the favorite's share approaches 100 percent, so \( T = 0 \) is treated as "always take the most likely word" (greedy choice). The dashed outline on every bar keeps the temperature-1.0 probabilities in view, so we can see that the model's knowledge stays put while only its way of choosing moves.

    Prompt Five candidate words at temperature 1.0
    1. "Know ___" thyself 60, yourself 20, the 10, your 6, nothing 4
    2. "Nothing in ___" excess 55, the 15, particular 12, life 10, common 8
    3. "On Sundays the Grand Bazaar is ___" closed 45, open 30, busy 15, quiet 6, crowded 4
    4. "The oracle at Delphi was ___" consulted 30, famous 25, a 20, closed 15, silent 10

    Draw 20 makes 20 independent random picks, each weighted by the bars, and counts how often each word was chosen. The counts vary from draw to draw: at temperature 1.0, prompt 3 gives "open" about 6 times in 20 on average, but any one draw may give 3 or 9. The random numbers come from a fixed starting seed, so the same sequence of presses reproduces the same counts, and Try Again restarts the sequence.

    What is simplified. All probabilities are invented to show the rule; the sim labels them "Illustrative probabilities, not from a real model." A real model scores tens of thousands of tokens, not five words, and products often combine temperature with other limits, such as sampling only from the most likely few tokens (top-k or top-p). Even at temperature 0, some hosted systems are not perfectly repeatable, because of small numerical differences in how the computation is run; the idealized rule in the sim is exactly repeatable.

    How to Use¶

    1. Read the challenge and choose one of the three answers (or press keys 1 to 3). Only the first choice counts.
    2. Press Reveal (or Enter). The temperature moves to the challenge's setting, the filled bars show the adjusted probabilities, and a draw of 20 appears, followed by the feedback.
    3. Press Draw 20 to draw again and watch the counts change, then press Next challenge.
    4. After challenge 5, free exploration unlocks: choose any prompt, move the Temperature slider from 0 to 2.0, and press Draw 20 as often as you like. Changing the prompt or the temperature clears the counts.
    5. Try Again returns to challenge 1 and resets the score.

    Lesson Plan¶

    Audience: adult travelers who use, or are curious about, AI chatbots.
    Duration: 10–15 minutes.
    Prerequisites: large language model, token, next-word prediction, temperature, and probability (Chapter 19, "Large Language Model" and "Next-Word Prediction").
    Learning objective (Bloom: Understand): Infer how changing the temperature changes which next word a language model chooses and how often, answering at least 4 of 5 prediction questions correctly before the sampled results are revealed.

    Activities¶

    1. Predict (8 min): Work through the five challenges in order. Say the reason for each answer out loud, or write it down, before pressing Reveal.
    2. Explore (4 min): Choose prompt 3 at temperature 1.0 and draw five times. How many draws contain at least one "open"? Now set the temperature to 0 and draw again.
    3. Discuss (3 min): The model "knows" the Grand Bazaar is usually closed on Sundays only in the sense that "closed" is its most likely word. Why can the same question get different answers on different days?

    Assessment¶

    Mastery is at least 4 of 5 correct on the first attempt. Moving the slider and drawing samples in free exploration is not evidence. As a follow-up, ask: Does raising the temperature make a model more creative or more accurate? A strong answer says that it makes the output more varied by giving unlikely words more chances, which can help with brainstorming but also makes wrong or odd words more frequent; it does not change what the model learned.

    References¶

    1. Softmax function — Wikipedia. The softmax formula and the temperature parameter that sharpens or flattens it.
    2. Large language model — Wikipedia. Tokens, training to predict the next token, and how text is generated one token at a time.
    3. Daniel Jurafsky and James H. Martin, Speech and Language Processing, 3rd edition draft (online). The chapter on large language models explains greedy decoding, random sampling, and temperature sampling.
    4. Geoffrey Hinton, Oriol Vinyals and Jeff Dean, "Distilling the Knowledge in a Neural Network" (2015, arXiv:1503.02531). Uses a softmax temperature above 1 to produce a "softer" probability distribution.
    5. Ari Holtzman and others, "The Curious Case of Neural Text Degeneration" (ICLR 2020, arXiv:1904.09751). Why always taking the top word produces dull, repetitive text, and why pure sampling can wander; introduces top-p (nucleus) sampling.
    6. Emily M. Bender, Timnit Gebru, Angelina McMillan-Major and Shmargaret Shmitchell, "On the Dangers of Stochastic Parrots: Can Language Models Be Too Big?" (FAccT 2021). The "stochastic parrots" paper mentioned in the chapter.
    7. p5.js — p5.js 2.3.4, the library used to draw the MicroSim.
