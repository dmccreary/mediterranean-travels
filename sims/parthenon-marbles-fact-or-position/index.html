    Agreed Fact or Position?¶

    Run the Agreed Fact or Position? MicroSim Fullscreen

    About This MicroSim¶

    In a heated dispute it is tempting to think the facts themselves must be in doubt. The Parthenon Marbles dispute shows otherwise: Greece and the British Museum accept almost all the same facts and disagree about what those facts mean and where the sculptures should be. This MicroSim asks us to pull the argument apart into its pieces.

    Twelve statement cards come one at a time. We sort each into one of three bins:

    Bin Definition
    Agreed fact Something both sides accept as true.
    British Museum's position What the British Museum argues should be concluded or done.
    Greek government's position What the Greek government argues should be concluded or done.

    After each answer the card joins its correct bin as a short label. At the end the three bins stand side by side: six agreed facts, three statements of the Museum's position, and three of the Greek government's.

    Two cards are traps, and mastery requires both: the lost Ottoman permission document (card 6) and the British Museum Act of 1963 (card 10). Each is a fact that one side cites more often, but both sides accept it as true. A fact that helps one side is still a fact.

    Fairness. The sim takes neither side. Each position is paraphrased from the side's own published statements (see References), and each side gets the same number of cards. The positions are summarized as of late 2026; talks between Greek officials and the British Museum have been reported since about 2022, so a position card may date if an agreement is announced.

    Wording change. The chapter's specification phrased card 8 as In London the sculptures are seen free of charge beside the art of cultures from around the world. Read literally, that sentence is a fact both sides would accept (entry to the Museum is free), which would make the card unfair. The sim words it as the argument the Museum makes: The sculptures are best seen in London, free of charge, beside the art of cultures from around the world.

    How to Use¶

    1. Read the three bin definitions at the top.
    2. Read the card in the panel and choose a bin with the buttons below (or press 1, 2 or 3). Only the first choice counts.
    3. Read the feedback. The correct bin is outlined in green; a wrong choice is outlined in red. The card joins its correct bin, with a red dashed border if you missed it.
    4. Press Next card (or Enter). After card 12, press See the bins.
    5. On the result screen, click any label in a bin to read its statement and reason again.
    6. Try Again clears the bins and reshuffles the twelve cards.

    Lesson Plan¶

    Audience: adult travelers preparing to visit the Acropolis Museum.
    Duration: 10–15 minutes.
    Prerequisites: contested history (Chapter 4), the Parthenon sculptures, Lord Elgin, the Acropolis Museum, and the Parthenon Marbles dispute (Chapter 15).
    Learning objective (Bloom: Analyze): Differentiate agreed facts from the British Museum's position and the Greek government's position in twelve statements about the Parthenon Marbles dispute, sorting at least 10 of 12 correctly on the first attempt.

    Activities¶

    1. Test each card (8 min): Before choosing, ask: Would the other side deny this? If not, it is an agreed fact, even if one side quotes it more. If the sentence says what should happen or what the facts prove, it is a position.
    2. Read the bins (3 min): On the result screen, pair each position card with the facts it rests on. Which facts does the Museum use? Which does Greece use? Are any facts used by both?
    3. Discuss (4 min): Why will more evidence not settle this dispute? What kind of agreement could?

    Assessment¶

    Mastery is 10 of 12 correct on the first attempt, including cards 6 and 10. As a follow-up, ask: Is the British Museum Act of 1963 part of the Museum's position? A strong answer says no: the law's existence is an agreed fact; the Museum's position is the conclusion it draws from it, and Greece accepts that the law exists while arguing about what should be done.

    References¶

    1. The Parthenon Sculptures — The British Museum. The Museum's published position: the permit from the Ottoman authorities, the legality of the acquisition, the value of a world museum, and its stance on loans.
    2. Lina Mendoni, Minister of Culture of Greece, speech at the Cardozo School of Law (English text, hosted by the British Committee for the Reunification of the Parthenon Marbles). The Greek government's case: the removal under Ottoman occupation, the reunification of the monument, and its refusal to recognize British ownership.
    3. Elgin Marbles — Wikipedia. The removals of 1801 to 1812, the surviving Italian translation of the Ottoman document, the 1816 purchase for £35,000, and a summary of both sides' arguments.
    4. British Museum Act 1963 — legislation.gov.uk. The act that restricts the trustees from disposing of objects in the collection.
    5. Dyfri Williams, "Lord Elgin's firman," Journal of the History of Collections 21:1 (2009), 49–76. A study of the permission document and its surviving Italian translation.
    6. Acropolis Museum — Wikipedia. Opened in June 2009, with the Parthenon Gallery showing the London pieces as plaster casts.
    7. Permanent reunification of Parthenon fragments from the Vatican Museums — The Acropolis Museum, March 2023. One of the returns of Parthenon fragments by other museums mentioned in the chapter.
    8. p5.js — p5.js 2.3.4, the library used to draw the bins and the cards.
