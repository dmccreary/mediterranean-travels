    Mind the Gap: How Old Is the Evidence?¶

    Run Mind the Gap: How Old Is the Evidence? Fullscreen

    About This MicroSim¶

    How much should we trust a story about the past? The chapter offers a simple rule built on two questions: how long after the event was the earliest surviving evidence made, and did its author have a reason to shape it? This MicroSim applies the rule to eight stories from our two sites, Troy and Gallipoli, on a timeline that runs from the Late Bronze Age to the 20th century.

    Verdict Rule
    Firm history Evidence within 10 years, no known reason to invent
    Real core, shaped telling Evidence within 10 years, but the author had a reason to shape it, or a detail is known to be invented
    Late or unsupported Earliest evidence more than 10 years after the event

    For each story the event appears on the timeline as a blue dot. Before anything else is shown, we predict when the earliest surviving evidence was made and mark it with a gold ring. Then the evidence appears as a red square, joined to the event by a gap bar, together with the author and their stake. Only then do we judge.

    Story Event Earliest evidence Gap Verdict
    A city on the Hisarlık mound burned c. 1180 BCE The Troy VIIa destruction layer itself 0 years Firm history
    A Hittite treaty with Alaksandu of Wilusa c. 1280 BCE The treaty tablet from Hattusa 0 years Firm history
    A ten-year Greek siege to win back Helen c. 1180 BCE, if it happened Homer's Iliad, c. 700 BCE about 480 years Late or unsupported
    The wooden horse c. 1180 BCE, if it happened Brief mentions in the Odyssey, c. 700 BCE; fullest telling in Virgil, c. 19 BCE about 480 years Late or unsupported
    Mehmed II at Troy, avenging the Trojans 1462 Kritoboulos's history of Mehmed, c. 1467 about 5 years Real core, shaped telling
    Priam's Treasure and Sophia's shawl 1873 Schliemann's own account, 1874 1 year Real core, shaped telling
    Three Allied battleships lost on March 18, 1915 1915 Naval records of both sides 0 years Firm history
    The "Johnnies and Mehmets" words of 1934 1934 Şükrü Kaya's newspaper interview, 1953 19 years Late or unsupported

    When all eight are judged, the timeline shows every gap at once. The longest bars belong to the most famous stories, the siege and the wooden horse.

    Two stories ask for care. The losses of March 18, 1915, were men, not only ships: the French battleship Bouvet sank within minutes with most of her crew. And the "Johnnies and Mehmets" words are judged here only as a claim about who wrote them in 1934. As cultural memory, read at Anzac Day services and carved at Anzac Cove, they are real whoever wrote them, as the chapter explains.

    How to Use¶

    1. Pick a story card above the timeline. Its event appears as a blue dot in its own row. Hover over any marker for details.
    2. Predict: click the timeline where you think the earliest surviving evidence was made, or type a year and choose BCE or CE. Your guess appears as a gold ring. Press Reveal the evidence.
    3. The timeline zooms to the story and shows the evidence, the gap bar, and the author's stake. The panel says how close your guess was (within 50 years counts as close for a BCE event, within 5 years for a CE event). The prediction is not scored.
    4. Judge: choose one of the three verdicts and press Check. A wrong first answer gives a hint; you get two tries. The rule that decides the story is highlighted on the right.
    5. Press Next story to continue. After all eight, press See all eight for the full timeline, then click any story card to review it.
    6. Full span returns to the whole timeline; Start over reshuffles the stories. Drag the timeline sideways to pan.

    Lesson Plan¶

    Audience: adult travelers preparing for Day 6 at Troy and Gallipoli.
    Duration: 15–20 minutes.
    Prerequisites: primary source and myth versus history (Chapter 4); Troy VIIa, Wilusa, the Iliad, the Odyssey, the Trojan Horse and Priam's Treasure (this chapter); the naval attack of March 18, 1915 (Chapter 7); the Johnnies and Mehmets words (this chapter); and the three verdicts.
    Learning objective (Bloom: Evaluate): Judge each of eight stories about Troy and Gallipoli as firm history, a real core with a shaped telling, or late or unsupported, by comparing the date of the event with the date and author of its earliest surviving evidence, with at least 7 of 8 correct on the first try.

    Activities¶

    1. Predict (5 min): For each story, commit to a date for the earliest evidence before revealing it. Notice which famous stories you expected to have early sources.
    2. Judge (8 min): Apply both criteria, the gap and the author's stake, in that order.
    3. Look at the whole (3 min): On the final timeline, compare the longest bars with the shortest. Which stories are most famous, and which are best supported?
    4. Discuss (4 min): The Mehmed and Schliemann stories have tiny gaps but are still shaped. Why is an early source not automatically an honest one?

    Assessment¶

    Mastery is at least 7 of 8 verdicts correct on the first try. A follow-up question: Hisarlık is a real Late Bronze Age city that burned around 1180 BCE. Does that make the ten-year siege of the Iliad firm history? No: a real place does not prove the story told about it; the earliest account of the siege is about 480 years later.

    Common misconceptions this activity targets:

    • A famous story told everywhere must have an early source.
    • A source written by an eyewitness, or soon after, is automatically reliable.
    • A real place proves the story told about it.
    • A story with a late source must be false. (It may be true; it cannot be shown to be.)

    References¶

    1. Troy — Wikipedia. The layers of Hisarlık, the destruction of Troy VIIa around 1180 BCE, and Carl Blegen's excavations of 1932–1938.
    2. Wilusa and Alaksandu treaty — Wikipedia. The treaty between Muwatalli II and Alaksandu of Wilusa (c. 1280 BCE), preserved at Hattusa, and the identification of Wilusa with Troy.
    3. Trojan Horse — Wikipedia. The horse's brief mentions in the Odyssey (4.271–289, 8.492–520, 11.523–532) and its full telling in Virgil's Aeneid, Book 2.
    4. Historicity of the Iliad — Wikipedia. Dating the Homeric poems to the late 8th or 7th century BCE and the debate over a historical Trojan War.
    5. Kritoboulos of Imbros, History of Mehmed the Conqueror, trans. Charles T. Riggs (Princeton University Press, 1954). Mehmed II's visit to Ilion in 1462; the history covers the years 1451–1467.
    6. Priam's Treasure — Wikipedia. The 1873 find, Schliemann's account in Trojanische Alterthümer (1874), and his later admission that Sophia was not present.
    7. Naval operations in the Dardanelles campaign — Wikipedia. The attack of March 18, 1915, and the loss of Bouvet, Irresistible and Ocean to mines.
    8. Tracking Atatürk: Honest History research note — Honest History. The research on the earliest evidence for the words (Şükrü Kaya, 1953) and the debate over their authorship.
