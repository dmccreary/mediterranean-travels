# Who Created the [Antikythera Mechanism](https://havecamerawilltravel.com/go/alamy/?d=https%3A%2F%2Fwww.alamy.com%2Fstock-photo%2FAntikythera-Mechanism.html%3Fqn%3Ddavid%2Bcoleman%26sortBy%3Drelevant&s=antikythera-mechanism-athens-greece)

In 1900 a crew of sponge divers sheltering from a storm off the small island
of Antikythera, between Kythera and Crete, found a Roman-era shipwreck on the
seabed. Over the next year they brought up bronze and marble statues, glass,
pottery, and coins. They also brought up a lump of corroded bronze and wood
that nobody paid much attention to. In 1902 the archaeologist Valerios Stais
noticed a gear wheel in one of its broken pieces. That lump is the
**Antikythera mechanism**, and it now sits in the National Archaeological
Museum in Athens, which [Chapter 20](../chapters/20-athens-museums-city-today/index.md)
visits. The link in the title above opens close-up photographs of the
fragments.

The ship sank in about 70–60 BCE, dated by the coins from Pergamon and
Ephesus found with it. The mechanism itself is older, probably made sometime
in the 2nd century BCE. Nobody signed it, and no ancient writer mentions this
particular device. This appendix asks what it would have taken to make it,
where it was most likely made, and who was living there.

## What the Mechanism Is

The mechanism was a wooden box about the size of a shoebox, roughly
34 × 18 × 9 cm, with a bronze dial on the front, two on the back, and a
hand crank on the side. Turning the crank drove a train of bronze gears
inside, and the gears drove pointers on the dials. Turn the crank forward and
the dials showed the sky on a future date; turn it back and they showed a
past one.

What survives is 82 corroded fragments holding at least 30 gears. The gears
were cut from bronze sheet a couple of millimeters thick, with small
triangular teeth filed by hand. The largest is about the width of a hand.
Every surface of the box was covered with tiny Greek inscriptions, a built-in
user's manual of which thousands of characters have now been read. Most of
what we know comes from X-ray computed tomography scans made in 2005 by the
Antikythera Mechanism Research Project, which let researchers count teeth and
read letters hidden inside the corroded lumps.

| Where | Dial | What it showed |
|---|---|---|
| Front | Zodiac ring | The positions of the Sun and Moon among the constellations, and, in modern reconstructions, the five planets known to the Greeks |
| Front | Calendar ring | The 365-day Egyptian civil calendar, with the Egyptian month names inscribed |
| Front | Moon ball | The phase of the Moon, shown by a small half-silver, half-black ball that turned |
| Back, upper | Metonic spiral | A 19-year calendar of 235 lunar months, with month names from a real Greek city's calendar |
| Back, upper | Games dial | Which athletic festivals fell in each year of a four-year cycle, including the Olympic Games |
| Back, lower | Saros spiral | An eclipse calendar of 223 lunar months, with a symbol on each month that would have a solar or lunar eclipse |
| Back, lower | Exeligmos dial | A correction telling the user how many hours to add to the eclipse times |

The hardest part to build was the Moon. The Moon moves faster across the sky
at some points in its orbit than at others. The maker reproduced this with
two gears mounted slightly off-center, one driving the other through a pin
sliding in a slot, so that the Moon pointer sped up and slowed down once a
month. That matches the lunar theory worked out by the astronomer Hipparchus,
who did much of his observing on Rhodes.

## One of the Earliest Analog Computers

An **analog computer** calculates by building a physical model whose
quantities behave like the ones you want to know. A slide rule is one: the
lengths along its scales stand for logarithms, and sliding one scale along
the other adds them. In the Antikythera mechanism, the angle a gear has
turned stands for time passed, and each gear ratio stands for a ratio between
two cycles in the sky. Turning the crank does the arithmetic.

The historian of science Derek de Solla Price called it "an ancient Greek
computer" in *Scientific American* in 1959, and the label stuck. It is the
oldest known geared calculating device, and one of the most significant
early analog computers ever made. It is a computer in the old sense of the
word, a machine that computes answers, not one that runs programs.

**Worked example: why a 235-month spiral?** The Greeks needed a calendar
that kept lunar months and solar years in step. Here is the arithmetic behind
the Metonic dial, with modern values:

| Cycle | Count | Length in days |
|---|---|---|
| Solar years | 19 × 365.2422 | 6,939.60 |
| Lunar months | 235 × 29.5306 | 6,939.69 |

Nineteen years and 235 months differ by about two hours, so after 19 years
the Moon's phases fall on nearly the same calendar dates again. The maker had
to turn that ratio into teeth. Because 235 = 5 × 47, the dial is a spiral of
five turns with 47 months on each turn, and a follower pin in the pointer
tracks which turn it is on.

The eclipse dial works the same way. Eclipses repeat in a cycle of 223 lunar
months, called the **Saros**, about 18 years and 11 days. Because 223 is a
prime number, it cannot be built from smaller gears, so the mechanism has a
gear with 223 teeth. A Saros is also about 8 hours longer than a whole number
of days, so each repeat of an eclipse happens 8 hours later in the day. The
small exeligmos dial, marked for 0, 8, and 16 hours, tells the user how much
to add. Three Saros cycles, 54 years, bring the eclipse back to nearly the
same hour.

!!! mascot-warning "Calling it a computer"
    ![Glaux raising a cautionary wing](../img/mascot/warning.png){ class="mascot-admonition-img" }
    A common trap is to picture the mechanism as a general-purpose machine. Its answers were fixed in its gears when it was made, and wrong values in the gears stayed wrong forever. Think of it as a calculator with one job, the motions of the sky, built with great skill.

## A Machine for Predicting the Future

People have always wanted to know what comes next: whether the harvest will
be good, whether a voyage will be safe, whether a war will be won. The Greeks
asked gods. Travelers climbed to Delphi to ask the Pythia, as
[Chapter 17](../chapters/17-oracle/index.md) and
[Chapter 18](../chapters/18-delphi/index.md) describe, and her answers were
famously open to interpretation. Babylonian scribes watched the sky for
omens, and over centuries of nightly records they noticed that eclipses and
planetary positions repeat in regular cycles.

The mechanism takes those records and turns them into a machine. Its user
did not need to ask a god or read an omen. Turning a crank showed when the
next eclipse would come, in what month, and at about what hour. The future,
at least the part of it written in the sky, became something you could
calculate, and the calculation gave the same answer to everyone who turned
the crank.

!!! mascot-thinking "From oracle to model"
    ![Glaux thinking](../img/mascot/thinking.png){ class="mascot-admonition-img" }
    Notice the shift. The Pythia's answer depended on who she was and how you read her, while the mechanism's answer came from cycles anyone could count, set into a model of the sky. Modern weather models and AI forecasts follow the same idea: find the patterns in past data, build a model, and run it forward.

That is the thread from Delphi to the classroom. [Chapter 19](../chapters/19-algorithm/index.md)
follows it to algorithms, and *AI-Native Schools* takes it further in

which uses doubling curves to predict how quickly AI tools will spread.

## The Skills Needed to Build It

No single craft could have produced the mechanism. Its maker, or more likely
its workshop, had to combine at least six kinds of expertise.

| Skill | What it involved | Where it came from |
|---|---|---|
| Observational astronomy | Knowing the cycles of the Sun, Moon, and planets, including eclipse periods and planetary periods such as Venus repeating its pattern of appearances every 462 years | Centuries of Babylonian records, refined by Greek astronomers |
| Mathematical astronomy | A geometric theory of why the Moon speeds up and slows down, and how to model it | Hipparchus and his circle, 2nd century BCE |
| Arithmetic and number theory | Turning cycle ratios into whole-number gear teeth, factoring numbers such as 235 and 223, and keeping error small | Greek mathematics in the tradition of Archimedes |
| Precision metalworking | Cutting dozens of bronze gears by hand, with teeth evenly spaced, small enough to fit in a box | Bronze workshops that also made statues, weapons, and fittings |
| Mechanical design | Mounting gears on shared axles, the pin-and-slot Moon gears, spiral dials with followers, a ball that turned to show phases | Greek engineers who built water clocks, automata, and siege machines |
| Calendars and civic life | Setting the dials to a particular city's month names and festival calendar, and writing a clear manual | A maker who knew the customer's home city |

Two more things were needed that are easy to overlook. One was money: a
device like this was a luxury object, and someone had to pay months of a
skilled workshop's time. The other was a **tradition**. The mechanism is too
refined to be a first attempt, and Cicero mentions two other geared models
of the heavens, one built by Archimedes in Syracuse and taken to Rome in
212 BCE, and one built by the philosopher Posidonius on Rhodes in Cicero's own
day. The Antikythera mechanism is the only one that survived, probably
because it sank. Bronze on land was usually melted down and reused.

## Why Rhodes Is One of the Most Likely Places

Several cities have been proposed, including Syracuse (because of
Archimedes), Pergamon, Corinth's colonies in northwest Greece, and Rhodes.
Rhodes has the strongest combined case.

- **Hipparchus observed there.** The greatest astronomer of the age recorded
  observations on Rhodes between about 147 and 127 BCE, and the mechanism's
  Moon gears follow his lunar theory.
- **Posidonius built a similar machine there.** Cicero, who studied with
  Posidonius on Rhodes (see [Chapter 12](../chapters/12-rhodes/index.md)),
  wrote that his teacher had made a sphere showing the motions of the Sun,
  Moon, and five planets.
- **The games dial names a Rhodian festival.** The classicist Paul Iversen
  read one of the damaged names on the games dial as the **Halieia**, the
  games Rhodes held for its sun god Helios. A device made for a buyer far
  away would have little reason to list a minor Rhodian festival.
- **The latitude fits.** The star calendar inscribed on the front works best
  for a latitude between about 33 and 37 degrees north. Rhodes lies at about
  36 degrees north; Athens, Corinth, and Pergamon all lie north of that band.
- **The ship's cargo points east.** The wreck carried coins from Pergamon and
  Ephesus and pottery from the eastern Aegean, which suggests a voyage from
  that region, where Rhodes was the great harbor, toward Italy.

The main evidence pointing elsewhere is the calendar on the Metonic dial,
which uses month names from the family of calendars of Corinth and its
colonies. Iversen argues that this is probably the calendar of Epirus, in
northwest Greece, and proposes that the mechanism was built on Rhodes for a
customer from Epirus. A modern watchmaker in Switzerland setting a watch to
a buyer's time zone does much the same.

## Who Lived on Rhodes

In the 2nd and early 1st centuries BCE, Rhodes was one of the richest and
most cosmopolitan cities in the Greek world: an independent republic with a
famous navy and one of the busiest harbors in the eastern Mediterranean. The
people a maker of the mechanism would have met came from many places and
classes.

**Rhodian citizens and the merchant elite.** Power belonged to a citizen
class of shipowners, merchants, and naval commanders. Their wealth came from
trade, especially grain from Egypt and the Black Sea, and from the harbor
dues every passing cargo paid. Rhodian maritime custom was respected enough
that Roman law later quoted the "Rhodian law" on dividing losses when a ship
had to throw cargo overboard in a storm. That prosperity took a hit in
167 BCE, when Rome made Delos a free port to punish Rhodes; the historian
Polybius reports that Rhodes's harbor revenue fell from a million drachmas a
year to 150,000.

**Sailors, shipwrights, and engineers.** The Rhodian navy was the best in
the Aegean, and keeping it at sea took carpenters, rope makers, metalworkers,
and siege engineers. The skills that built warships and catapults were close
cousins of the skills that built gear trains.

**Craftsmen and artists.** Rhodes had famous bronze casters, the tradition
that had produced the Colossus a century earlier, and leading sculptors. The
marble base of the Winged Victory of Samothrace, carved as the prow of a
warship, is Rhodian marble. Potters in Rhodes stamped the handles of vast numbers
of wine jars with the name of the year's priest of Helios and the workshop owner,
a kind of official labeling.

**Scholars and teachers from abroad.** Many of the island's best-known
thinkers were not born there. Hipparchus came from Nicaea in Bithynia.
Posidonius, a Stoic philosopher, astronomer, and geographer, came from
Apamea in Syria and ran a school on Rhodes. The rhetoric teacher Apollonius
Molon came from Caria. The Stoic Panaetius was a native Rhodian who worked
in Rome and Athens. The astronomer Geminus, who may have been Rhodian, wrote
a textbook that explains the same cycles the mechanism displays, including
the exeligmos.

**Roman students and visitors.** Ambitious young Romans sailed to Rhodes to
study, as Chapter 12 describes: Cicero, traditionally Julius Caesar, and
later Pompey, who came to hear Posidonius. These were exactly the kind of
wealthy, educated clients who might want a model of the heavens.

**Foreign residents and enslaved people.** Inscriptions on Rhodes record
large numbers of resident foreigners from Asia Minor, Syria, Egypt, and
elsewhere, working as traders, bankers, and craftsmen. Much of the heavy
labor, in the harbor, the workshops, and the households, was done by
enslaved people, some of whom were themselves skilled workers. The hands
that filed the gear teeth may well have belonged to someone whose name no
one recorded.

## So, Who Created It?

We do not know the maker's name, and we may never know it. The evidence
points to a skilled workshop, quite possibly on Rhodes, working in a
tradition that ran from Archimedes through Hipparchus to Posidonius. Its
astronomy drew on Babylonian records, its lunar theory on Hipparchus, its
mathematics on Greek number theory, and its craftsmanship on Rhodian bronze
work. The dials were set to a customer's calendar, perhaps in northwest
Greece.

The dating is still argued over. The eclipse dial appears to start at a new
moon in 205 BCE, but a designer could choose a starting date in the past,
the way a calendar app lets you scroll back. The style of the lettering
suggests the box was made later, perhaps toward the end of the 2nd century
BCE. Either way, the mechanism was the work of many minds across several
cultures, which is the best answer to the question in this appendix's title.

## A Technology Lost for 1,500 Years

The strangest fact about the mechanism is what came after it. Simpler
geared devices did appear in later centuries, but nothing of comparable
complexity survives for well over a thousand years.

| Date | Device | Place |
|---|---|---|
| c. 205–100 BCE | Antikythera mechanism, at least 30 gears | Probably Rhodes |
| 6th century CE | Portable sundial with a small geared calendar, four surviving gears | Byzantine Empire |
| c. 1000 CE | A geared lunar calendar described by al-Biruni | Central Asia |
| 1088–1092 CE | Su Song's water-driven astronomical clock tower | Kaifeng, China |
| c. 1327–1336 CE | Richard of Wallingford's astronomical clock | St Albans, England |
| 1364 CE | Giovanni de' Dondi's astrarium, showing the Sun, Moon, and five planets | Padua, Italy |
| 1410 CE | The Prague astronomical clock, the Orloj | Prague, Bohemia |

Devices of similar complexity, gear trains that modeled the Sun, the Moon,
and the planets, returned only with the great astronomical clocks of Europe
in the 1300s and 1400s. Counted from the mechanism's likely date of about
150 BCE to de' Dondi's astrarium, the gap is about 1,500 years; counted from
its earliest proposed date of 205 BCE to the Prague clock, it is about 1,600.

Why was the knowledge lost? It lived in a few workshops and in the heads of
the people who worked there, not in printed books. Rome's conquest of the
Greek east, and wars such as the Roman general Cassius's sack of Rhodes in
43 BCE, broke up the centers where it was practiced. Bronze was valuable,
so old machines were melted down. Each time a master died without a
student, a little more was gone.

That is why this small box matters to a book about learning. Knowledge held
by a few experts can vanish in a generation. Knowledge that is written down,
copied, and taught widely survives, which is the story of the printing
presses in [Chapter 6](../chapters/06-bazaars-scripts-presses/index.md). AI
tutors now offer a chance to spread expert skill to anyone who wants to
learn it, so that the next Antikythera mechanism is not the last of its
kind for 1,500 years.
