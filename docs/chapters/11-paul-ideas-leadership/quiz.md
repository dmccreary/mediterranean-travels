# Quiz: Paul at Ephesus, Part 2: Ideas, Legacy, and Lessons in Leading Change

Test your understanding of Paul's central ideas, their contested legacy, and what his mission can and cannot teach about leading change in schools with these review questions.

---

#### 1. What did the leaders of the early Jesus movement decide at the Council of Jerusalem, about 48 or 49 CE?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. That Jewish believers must stop keeping the law of Moses
2. That Paul alone would lead the mission to the Gentiles, without consulting Jerusalem
3. That Gentile believers did not have to be circumcised or take on the whole Jewish law
4. That the movement would separate from Judaism and found a new religion
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **C**. Acts 15 and Galatians 2 tell the meeting differently, but they agree on the core: Gentiles could belong without circumcision. In Acts, James adds four rules, which Paul never mentions. For Jewish believers the law remained their way of life; the decision was about what could not be imposed on newcomers, so option A reverses it. The chapter calls it the movement's first recorded decision about what was essential and what was not, made by deciding what to stop requiring.

    **Concept Tested:** Council of Jerusalem

    **See:** [The Council of Jerusalem](index.md#the-council-of-jerusalem)

#### 2. Livy tells a fable of limbs that refuse to feed the belly; Paul uses the same body image in 1 Corinthians 12. How does Paul change its moral?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Livy's fable tells the common people to keep serving the powerful, while Paul says the weaker members are indispensable and honored
2. Paul uses it to tell the poor to return to work, just as the Roman senator did
3. Paul replaces the body with a building, so the image has nothing in common with Livy's
4. Both use it to argue that every member of a community should hold the same gift
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **A**. In Livy's story, the senator Menenius Agrippa persuaded Rome's protesting common people to return by telling of limbs that starved themselves by refusing to feed the belly: the hands should keep serving. Paul wrote to Corinthians who ranked one another by their gifts and left the poor hungry at shared suppers. He says the eye cannot tell the hand "I have no need of you," the weaker members are indispensable, and "if one member suffers, all suffer." Same image, opposite direction.

    **Concept Tested:** One Body, Many Parts

    **See:** [One Body, Many Parts](index.md#one-body-many-parts)

#### 3. How did the Protestant Reformation grow out of a reading of Paul?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Augustine nailed theses about Galatians to a church door in Milan in 386
2. The Council of Trent ordered every priest to preach only from Paul's letters
3. John Wesley translated Romans into German in 1738, starting the Lutheran church
4. Martin Luther, lecturing on Romans, came to read righteousness as God's gift and issued his 95 Theses in 1517
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **D**. Martin Luther, an Augustinian friar teaching the Bible at Wittenberg, lectured on Romans in 1515 and 1516 and came to read "the one who is righteous will live by faith" as a promise that God gives righteousness as a gift. On October 31, 1517, he issued his 95 Theses against the sale of indulgences, and printing spread his pamphlets within weeks. Augustine's 386 moment was a conversion in a Milan garden, and Wesley, two centuries later, heard Luther's preface to Romans read aloud.

    **Concept Tested:** Protestant Reformation

    **See:** [The Protestant Reformation](index.md#the-protestant-reformation)

#### 4. Paul wrote a harsh passage about Jews in 1 Thessalonians, yet in Romans 9 to 11 he pictured Gentile believers as branches grafted onto Israel's olive tree. How does the chapter analyze the role of his words in Christian anti-Judaism?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Paul's letters contain no harsh lines, so later anti-Judaism had no connection to them
2. His words point in more than one direction, and later Christians quoted the harsh lines while forgetting the olive tree
3. Paul intended to found a church that replaced Israel, so later anti-Judaism simply followed his plan
4. The harsh lines appear only in the disputed letters, so they can be set aside as not his
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **B**. In Romans 9 to 11, Paul writes of "great sorrow" for his people, asks whether God has rejected them, answers "By no means!", and warns Gentiles that "the root supports you." But the harsh passage in 1 Thessalonians, an undisputed letter, is also his, so option D fails. From John Chrysostom to Luther, Christians quoted the harsh lines and forgot the olive tree. After the Holocaust, churches began to repudiate "the teaching of contempt," and *Nostra Aetate* drew on Paul's olive tree.

    **Concept Tested:** Christian Anti-Judaism

    **See:** [Christian Anti-Judaism](index.md#christian-anti-judaism)

#### 5. Why does the chapter treat the Carnegie unit as an inherited form rather than an essential purpose of schooling?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. It was created in 1906 for pensions and transcripts, and it counts hours in class rather than learning
2. It was invented by Andrew Carnegie to measure how well students read
3. It requires every student to master a subject before earning credit
4. It was abolished in 1906, so modern schools no longer use it
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **A**. The Carnegie unit, defined in 1906 as 120 hours of class in one subject over a year, was created to decide which colleges qualified for the Carnegie Foundation's pensions and to give colleges a common way to read transcripts. Most American high schools still award credit on that basis. Its weakness is that it counts seat time, not learning, so a student who already knows the material and one who is lost earn the same credit. That is the opposite of mastery (option C).

    **Concept Tested:** Seat Time and the Carnegie Unit

    **See:** [Seat Time and the Carnegie Unit](index.md#seat-time-and-the-carnegie-unit)

#### 6. A school district wants to spread an AI-native model to many schools but has only a small team. Following Paul's Ephesian strategy, what should it do?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Send the team to visit every school once a year for a short training day
2. Launch the model in all schools at the same moment so no school feels left out
3. Establish the model firmly at one well-connected pilot school that others already visit, and let visitors and co-workers carry it home
4. Wait until a national mandate requires every school to change at once
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **C**. Hub-and-spoke diffusion establishes a change firmly at one central place, then lets people who travel between places carry it outward. Paul stayed in Ephesus, where roads, ships, courts, and festivals brought people anyway, while co-workers such as Epaphras carried the message to Colossae, Laodicea, and Hierapolis, places Paul never visited. A leader who chooses the hub well does not have to go everywhere. The pilot school as hub applies the same idea; option A spreads a small team too thin.

    **Concept Tested:** Hub-and-Spoke Diffusion

    **See:** [Hub-and-Spoke Diffusion](index.md#hub-and-spoke-diffusion)

#### 7. Around 112 CE, the Roman governor Pliny reported that once he had suppressed the Christians, temples filled again and sacrificial meat began selling. What does this evidence add to the story of Demetrius the silversmith?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. It proves that the riot in Ephesus happened word for word as Acts tells it
2. It shows that Demetrius invented the threat to his trade in order to attack Paul
3. It shows that Roman governors supported the Christians against the old cults
4. It confirms from an independent pagan source that the old cults' economy shrank where the movement grew, so Demetrius' resistance was rational
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **D**. The economics of resistance holds that people resist change partly because of what it costs them: income, skill, status, and identity. Acts, a Christian history, and Pliny, a pagan governor writing to Trajan, independently describe the same pattern: where the new movement grew, the old cults' trade shrank. So Demetrius was right about his market. The lesson for schools is to treat a teacher's fear for her job as a legitimate concern that deserves a straight answer, not to call it irrational.

    **Concept Tested:** Economics of Resistance

    **See:** [The Economics of Resistance](index.md#the-economics-of-resistance)

#### 8. Three months into an AI-tutoring pilot, the share of questions students answer correctly has fallen from 70 to 63 percent. What does the idea of the implementation dip advise?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Cancel the pilot at once, since the new method has clearly made students worse
2. Expect a temporary drop while people build new skills, and stay with the change, as Paul stayed with Corinth
3. Hide the results from teachers so that they do not lose confidence
4. Return to the old method for three months, then try the new one again from the start
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **B**. Michael Fullan found that before people are fluent in a new practice, they are worse at it than at the old one. In the illustrative pilot, scores fall to 63 percent by month three, pass the baseline by month six, and reach 78 percent by month twelve, so a leader who judges the pilot at month three cancels it just before the payoff. Corinth was Paul's dip: factions and lawsuits a year or two in. He did not abandon it; he wrote, sent Timothy, visited, and wrote again.

    **Concept Tested:** Implementation Dip

    **See:** [The Implementation Dip](index.md#the-implementation-dip)

#### 9. A principal must choose one teacher to champion a new approach among cautious colleagues. Which choice fits the chapter's advice?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. The respected veteran who was skeptical at first and changed her mind publicly, given protected time and a lighter load
2. The newest teacher, who is most excited about technology and has no old habits to unlearn
3. An outside consultant who has led the change in many other districts
4. The biggest enthusiast on staff, asked to take on the role on top of a full teaching load
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **A**. Like Paul, a Pharisee who knew the law and had opposed the movement, an insider change leader carries credibility outsiders lack. Veteran teacher champions are the school's version: colleagues believe someone like themselves, so the respected skeptic who changed her mind publicly persuades better than the biggest enthusiast. The outside consultant who "flies in for a day" lacks that credibility. Champions also need protected time and a lighter load, or the first adopter burns out, which rules out option D.

    **Concept Tested:** Veteran Teacher Champions

    **See:** [Veteran Teacher Champions](index.md#veteran-teacher-champions)

#### 10. The chapter admits the Paul parallel has limits. Which judgment best explains why a school leader cannot simply copy Paul's approach?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Paul's letters are too old to say anything useful about organizations today
2. Paul's mission failed, so its strategies should be avoided
3. Schools change faster than first-century communities, so patience is no longer needed
4. Paul's hearers chose to join and needed no data, while students cannot opt out and a school must show a change helps and be ready to stop
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **D**. Among the limits the chapter lists, Paul believed he carried a message from God, and his hearers chose to join and could leave. Students and teachers in a public school do not choose most changes made to it. Paul also had no data and needed none, while a school must show that a change helps students and be ready to stop if it does not. The chapter still finds the case study useful, which rules out options A and B.

    **Concept Tested:** Limits of the Paul Parallel

    **See:** [The Limits of the Paul Parallel](index.md#the-limits-of-the-paul-parallel)
