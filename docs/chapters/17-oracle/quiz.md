# Quiz: The Oracle and the Algorithm, Part 1: The Oracle

Test your understanding of prediction, how the Oracle of Delphi worked, its famous answers and flaws, and the sayings over its door with these review questions.

---

#### 1. According to the chapter, what three things make a statement a real prediction?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. It makes a claim, it is made before the outcome, and some possible outcome could prove it wrong
2. It comes from a god, it is spoken in verse, and it is recorded by a priest
3. It states a probability, it uses numbers, and it is made by an expert
4. It gives advice, it is easy to remember, and it comes true in the end
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **A**. A prediction makes a claim about something not yet known, is made before the outcome, and can be checked: some outcome could show it to be wrong. A forecast adds a stated likelihood, and a prophecy is a prediction said to come from a god, so neither probability nor divine origin is required (options B and C). Many statements that sound like predictions are really commands or advice. The chapter's motto: a prediction has to be able to lose.

    **Concept Tested:** Prediction

    **See:** [Prediction](index.md#prediction)

#### 2. Using the chapter's three-part test, which of these statements is a real prediction?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. "If you make war, you will destroy a great empire."
2. "Found a city in Libya."
3. "Know thyself."
4. "It will rain at Delphi tomorrow afternoon."
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **D**. "Rain at Delphi tomorrow afternoon" makes a claim before the outcome, and a dry afternoon would prove it wrong, so it can lose. The Croesus answer (option A) is hedged: it comes true whether Lydia or Persia wins, so it predicts nothing. "Found a city in Libya" is a command that tells us what to do, not what will happen. "Know thyself" is advice carved on the temple porch. Only option D passes all three parts of the test.

    **Concept Tested:** Prediction

    **See:** [Prediction](index.md#prediction)

#### 3. Joseph Fontenrose sorted about 535 recorded Delphic responses by how close the record was to the event. What pattern did he find?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. The answers recorded closest to the event were mostly elaborate riddles in verse
2. The answers recorded closest to the event were mostly short, practical commands and approvals, while the famous riddles cluster in later stories
3. Almost every recorded answer turned out to be a forgery written by Roman authors
4. The oracle answered only state questions, never private ones
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **B**. Fontenrose found that the answers recorded nearest the time are mostly short and practical, such as "it is better to sacrifice to this god" or "yes, the law is approved." The famous double-edged riddles cluster in stories written down later. Scholars still argue about individual judgments, but the shape of the result has held: the riddling oracle of legend and the practical oracle of the inscriptions are not the same. Most consultations were in fact private, which rules out option D.

    **Concept Tested:** Oracle of Delphi

    **See:** [The Oracle of Delphi](index.md#the-oracle-of-delphi)

#### 4. Apply the chapter's "both outcomes" test to the oracle's answer that no one was wiser than Socrates. What does it show?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. It is a hedge, because it comes true whether or not a wiser person exists
2. It is a command, because it tells Socrates to become wise
3. It could lose, since finding someone wiser would prove it false, so it makes a real claim
4. It is meaningless, because wisdom cannot be measured at all
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **C**. The test lists the possible outcomes and asks which would make the answer true. For Croesus, both a Lydian and a Persian victory make it true, so it cannot lose. For Socrates, finding someone wiser would make the answer false, so it could lose. That is why Socrates spent decades trying to prove the god wrong, questioning politicians, poets, and craftsmen. He found each thought he knew things he did not. Option A wrongly treats it like the Croesus answer.

    **Concept Tested:** Hedging Answers

    **See:** [Hedging Answers](index.md#hedging-answers)

#### 5. Before Salamis, the oracle told Athens that a "wooden wall" alone would not fall and spoke of "divine Salamis." Analyzing how Athens used the answer, which conclusion fits the chapter?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. The oracle told the Athenians exactly what to do, and they simply obeyed
2. The professional interpreters read the answer correctly, and the Assembly followed them
3. The answer was so clear that no debate was needed
4. The Assembly chose between rival readings and voted for Themistocles' view that "divine" meant the dead would be the enemy's and the wall was the fleet
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **D**. In the Assembly, some said the wooden wall meant the old thorn hedge around the Acropolis; the professional interpreters said "divine Salamis" foretold an Athenian defeat at sea. Themistocles argued that a god meaning Athenian deaths would have said "cruel," not "divine," so the wall was the ships. The Assembly voted with him. The Athenians needed both the god's answer and their best forecaster's judgment, and the decision was still theirs. Herodotus wrote about fifty years later, and some suspect the answer was polished afterward.

    **Concept Tested:** Wooden Walls Prophecy

    **See:** [The Wooden Walls Prophecy](index.md#the-wooden-walls-prophecy)

#### 6. During an oracle consultation, how did the priests test whether Apollo was present that day?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. They sprinkled a goat with cold water; if it trembled through its whole body, the god was present
2. They watched whether the sacred flame in the temple burned blue
3. They asked the Pythia to name a stranger she had never met
4. They listened for thunder from Mount Parnassus at dawn
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **A**. The consultation steps are pieced together from Plutarch, Herodotus, Euripides, inscriptions, and the temple layout. Consultants arrived on a consultation day, purified with water from the Castalian Spring, and paid the fee, the *pelanos*, with foreigners presented by a Delphian sponsor, a *proxenos*. Then the priests sprinkled a goat with cold water. If it trembled through its whole body, Apollo was present and consultations could go ahead; if not, there would be none that day.

    **Concept Tested:** Oracle Consultation

    **See:** [The Oracle Consultation](index.md#the-oracle-consultation)

#### 7. Herodotus reports that the Pythia Perialla was bribed to say Demaratus was not a true king's son, and that she was removed from office when the plot came out. What does this case reveal about the oracle?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. The oracle was a fraud from the start, so no Greek trusted it again
2. Bribery was legal at Delphi, since the priests openly sold favorable answers
3. The oracle could be corrupted through human hands, but when found out it punished its own
4. Only foreign kings, never Greeks, ever tried to influence the Pythia
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **C**. Around 491 BCE, the Spartan king Cleomenes had a powerful Delphian, Cobon, persuade the Pythia to declare that Demaratus was not the true son of the previous king. When the plot came out, Cobon fled and Perialla was removed. The story shows both the flaw and a defense against it. Greeks kept consulting the oracle for centuries, so option A is wrong, and the Spartans and the Athenian Alcmaeonids, who were both Greek, were the bribers, so option D is wrong too.

    **Concept Tested:** Bribery of the Oracle

    **See:** [Bribery of the Oracle](index.md#bribery-of-the-oracle)

#### 8. De Boer and Hale argued that ethylene rising through faults beneath the temple inspired the Pythia. Weighing the evidence, which judgment is best supported?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. The theory is proven: measured ethylene levels at Delphi are high enough to cause a trance
2. Ancient writers reported a vapor and the faults are real, but the gas is disputed, since later measurements found little or no ethylene
3. The theory is a hoax, because there are no faults anywhere near Delphi
4. The question is closed, because ancient writers never mentioned anything rising from the ground
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **B**. Strabo describes a breath rising from a cave, and Plutarch mentions a sweet smell and a weakening vapor, so option D is wrong. De Boer and Hale showed that two faults cross beneath the temple and that a nearby spring held traces of light gases, so option C is wrong. But later measurements found little or no ethylene, and critics argued a trance would need far more gas than any plausible leak. Two of three claims hold; the gas itself is still debated.

    **Concept Tested:** Geological Fault Theory

    **See:** [The Geological Fault Theory](index.md#the-geological-fault-theory)

#### 9. How did the meaning of "Know thyself," carved on the temple porch, change over six centuries?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. It always meant "know your enemies," as generals understood it
2. It began as Plato's call to self-examination and later became a warning about mortal limits
3. It was a password that let paying visitors into the inner room of the temple
4. It moved from "know you are a mortal, not a god," to Socrates' self-examination, to Plutarch's reading as the god's reply, "you are not"
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **D**. In the Archaic age, scholars think it meant: know that you are mortal, and know your limits before approaching Apollo. Around 380 BCE, Plato's Socrates turned it inward, toward examining one's own beliefs and ignorance. Around 100 CE, a speaker in Plutarch's *The E at Delphi* read it as the god's reply to a worshipper's "you are": you are not. Option B reverses the order. The sayings were free advice for everyone, read before anyone paid for an answer.

    **Concept Tested:** Know Thyself

    **See:** [Know Thyself](index.md#know-thyself)

#### 10. We visit Delphi on November 22. In the oracle's great days, what would we most likely have found?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. The god away for the winter and the Pythia silent, with Dionysus holding the sanctuary
2. The busiest consultation day of the year, on Apollo's birthday
3. A special winter oracle given only to foreign visitors
4. The Pythia answering daily, since consultations ran every day of the year
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **A**. Greeks believed Apollo left Delphi for the three winter months, usually to the Hyperboreans "beyond the north wind," and the oracle gave no answers while Dionysus held the sanctuary. Greek months were lunar and cannot be converted exactly, but the silence is generally put from about November or December to February, so we would very probably have found the god away. Apollo's birthday fell in Bysios, in early spring, which rules out option B, and consultations were monthly at most, not daily.

    **Concept Tested:** Apollo's Winter Absence

    **See:** [Apollo's Winter Absence](index.md#apollos-winter-absence)
