# Quiz: Day 7: Ephesus, a Greco-Roman City

Test your understanding of Ephesus, its columns and inscriptions, its sacred sites and traditions, and the Library of Celsus with these review questions.

---

#### 1. Why does ancient Ephesus now stand several kilometers from the sea, with its old harbor a field?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. The Cayster River carried soil down every winter and dropped it at its mouth, pushing the coast west
2. An earthquake raised the land and drained the harbor in a single day
3. The Romans filled in the harbor to build Marble Street and the theater
4. The sea level of the Aegean has fallen steadily since Roman times
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **A**. Ephesus harbor silting was the slow filling of the harbor with mud carried by the Küçük Menderes, the Cayster of the Greeks. Its winter floods dropped soil where the current slowed, so the coastline crept west and the city chased it. The archaic city stood near the Temple of Artemis, Lysimachus refounded it around 290 BCE nearer the water, and Byzantine and Turkish Ayasuluk took over as the harbor failed. The chapter describes a gradual process, not a single earthquake.

    **Concept Tested:** Ephesus Harbor Silting

    **See:** [Ephesus Harbor Silting](index.md#ephesus-harbor-silting)

#### 2. Statues of Artemis of Ephesus have a mass of rounded, egg-shaped objects on the chest. What do scholars actually know about them?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. They are breasts, as an ancient inscription at the temple explains
2. They are bees, the symbol of the city on its coins
3. They are the eggs of the griffins shown on her skirt
4. No ancient text explains them, so their meaning is unknown
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **D**. Visitors are often told the objects are breasts, but they have no nipples, and scholars have proposed eggs, gourds, the testicles of sacrificed bulls, or amber ornaments sewn onto a garment. No ancient text explains them, so the honest answer is that we do not know. The bee was one of her symbols, but it appears among the animals on her skirt and on coins, not as the objects on her chest. She was likely an older Anatolian mother goddess under a Greek name.

    **Concept Tested:** Artemis of Ephesus

    **See:** [Artemis of Ephesus](index.md#artemis-of-ephesus)

#### 3. At the Basilica Stoa, you see a column standing on a molded base, with a pair of spiral scrolls on its capital. Which order is it?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Doric, because it has a plain cushion-shaped capital
2. Ionic, because it has a base and volutes on its capital
3. Corinthian, because it has acanthus leaves around a tall bell
4. Tuscan, because it is the plainest of the Roman orders
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **B**. Reading a column from the bottom up, the molded base rules out Doric, which has no base. The pair of spiral scrolls, called volutes, on the capital makes it Ionic, the order born in Ionia on this coast. A Corinthian capital is a tall bell wrapped in acanthus leaves, and Doric has a simple cushion under a square slab. The chapter's rule of thumb: base, shaft, capital, and entablature are four questions, and the capital alone usually answers the main one.

    **Concept Tested:** Ionic Order

    **See:** [Classical Orders](index.md#classical-orders)

#### 4. Near the Prytaneion you pass the ruined Memmius Monument, carved with figures in relief. Its inscription names Gaius Memmius, a grandson of the Roman dictator Sulla. What does reading that inscription add to your visit?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. It gives the exact date the monument fell in an earthquake
2. It proves the monument was moved here from Rome
3. It identifies whom the monument honored and reveals who held power in the city
4. It lists the craftsmen who carved the relief figures
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **C**. Inscriptions are Ephesus's richest primary sources: they name who paid, who was honored, and why. Without its inscription, the Memmius Monument would be just a handsome ruin. With it, we know it honored the grandson of Sulla, who had punished the cities of this province after the massacre of 88 BCE. A monument to the dictator's grandson in the heart of Ephesus tells us who held power here. The chapter does not say the inscription dates its fall or names its carvers.

    **Concept Tested:** Inscription

    **See:** [Inscription](index.md#inscription)

#### 5. Why does a true fresco, like those in the terrace houses, last so much better than ordinary paint?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. The colors were mixed with oil, which seals the wall against water
2. Pigments in water are applied to wet lime plaster, and as it dries the color is bound into the wall itself
3. The painting was baked in a kiln before being set into the wall
4. A layer of glass tesserae was laid over the paint to protect it
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **B**. "Fresco" is Italian for "fresh." The painter coats the wall in lime plaster and, while the top layer is still wet, applies pigments mixed with water. As the lime dries, a chemical reaction binds the color into the surface, so a true fresco does not flake off like surface paint and lasts as long as the plaster. Because only as much wall is plastered as can be painted before it sets, large frescoes were made in daily patches whose joins can sometimes be seen. Glass tesserae make a mosaic, not a fresco.

    **Concept Tested:** Fresco

    **See:** [Fresco](index.md#fresco)

#### 6. Every local detail in Acts' story of the silversmiths' riot fits what the inscriptions of Ephesus say. What does that evidence allow us to conclude?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. The author knew how Ephesus worked, though that does not prove every word of the scene happened as told
2. The riot happened exactly as told, including every word of the town clerk's speech
3. Acts must have been written by an Ephesian official who kept the city's records
4. The story was invented, because accurate details are what a skilled forger would include
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **A**. Details such as the town clerk, the Asiarchs, and Ephesus as temple warden of Artemis match the city's own inscriptions, which shows the author knew how Ephesus worked. But Acts was written a generation later, and its speeches are the author's own composition, so the match does not prove every word happened as told (option B). Accurate background makes the story plausible rather than proven, and nothing in the evidence points to an official author or to forgery.

    **Concept Tested:** Riot of the Silversmiths

    **See:** [The Riot of the Silversmiths](index.md#the-riot-of-the-silversmiths)

#### 7. From the lower part of the site, your guide points to a square stone tower on a western hilltop called St. Paul's Prison. How should you describe it?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. The cell where Acts says Paul was held before the silversmiths' riot
2. A Byzantine chapel built over the place where Paul was executed
3. A modern replica built for pilgrims in the 20th century
4. A real tower from Lysimachus' Hellenistic city wall, whose link to Paul is tradition only
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **D**. The tower is real and old: it was part of the city wall built by Lysimachus in the early 3rd century BCE. The identification with Paul is tradition only. Acts mentions no imprisonment in Ephesus, which rules out option A, though Paul's own letters lead many scholars to think he was imprisoned somewhere in the province. Even so, nothing ties him to this tower, which was a fortification, not a jail or a chapel. It is a real Hellenistic tower with a name added much later.

    **Concept Tested:** St. Paul's Prison Tower

    **See:** [St. Paul's Prison Tower](index.md#st-pauls-prison-tower)

#### 8. What did the Council of Ephesus, meeting in the Church of Mary in 431 CE, decide?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. That the Temple of Artemis should be closed and its stones reused for churches
2. That the Book of Acts and Paul's letters should be added to the Bible
3. That Mary could rightly be called *Theotokos*, "God-bearer," because Jesus was one person, both God and man
4. That Ephesus, not Jerusalem, was the place where Mary was buried
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **C**. Summoned by Theodosius II and counted as the third great council, it met in the Church of Mary in 431. Nestorius of Constantinople preferred "Christ-bearer" to keep Jesus' human and divine natures distinct, while Cyril of Alexandria insisted that because Jesus was one person, his mother could be called the bearer of God. The council, led by Cyril, upheld *Theotokos*, which became a central Orthodox title of Mary. The council made no ruling on Mary's burial place.

    **Concept Tested:** Council of Ephesus

    **See:** [The Council of Ephesus](index.md#the-council-of-ephesus)

#### 9. How did the Asia Minor refugees of 1922–1923 change Greece?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. About 1.2 million people arrived in a country of about five million, founding new neighborhoods and bringing music such as rebetiko and Anatolian dishes
2. They stayed only a few years and then returned to their homes in Anatolia
3. They settled mainly on the islands of the Dodecanese, which were empty at the time
4. They were absorbed so quickly that they left no trace on Greek culture
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **A**. About 1.2 million Greek Orthodox refugees arrived in a country of about five million, and many lived for years in tents and shacks. New neighborhoods around Athens and Piraeus were named for lost homes, such as Nea Smyrni and Nea Ionia, and others settled farmland in northern Greece. They brought the music that grew into rebetiko, dishes such as *soutzoukakia smyrneika*, and skills in trade and crafts. The exchange was compulsory and permanent, so none could return (option B).

    **Concept Tested:** Asia Minor Refugees

    **See:** [Asia Minor Refugees](index.md#asia-minor-refugees)

#### 10. Compare Aquila's gift of the Library of Celsus with Andrew Carnegie's libraries. Which analysis of their designs fits the chapter?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Both donors paid all costs forever, so neither library ever depended on its city
2. Aquila's library failed because he gave no money for books, while Carnegie's succeeded because he bought every book
3. Carnegie's libraries were open to all readers, while the Library of Celsus allowed only scholars inside
4. Carnegie required towns to pay yearly upkeep, which spread libraries and kept towns invested, while Aquila endowed books and upkeep, which lasted only as long as his money
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **D**. Aquila left 25,000 denarii to buy books and maintain the building, so his library ran on his own money and then depended on the city. Carnegie paid for more than 2,500 buildings but required each town to provide the land and pay about a tenth of his gift yearly for books, staff, and upkeep, so the idea spread and towns stayed invested. Option B reverses the facts. Neither went all the way: a library serves only those who can read and reach its door.

    **Concept Tested:** Philanthropy for Knowledge

    **See:** [Learning for All: A Son's Gift of Books](index.md#learning-for-all-a-sons-gift-of-books)
