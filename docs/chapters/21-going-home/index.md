## Part One: What We Found Behind the Scripts

### Seeing Past the Tourist Script

Chapter 2 defined the **tourist script** as the standard story told to visitors in the standard order: the shortest story that sells. **Seeing past the tourist script** is the habit of asking, at every stop, what the standard story leaves out, and in this guide the question was most often "who got to learn here, and who did not?" The script of the Library of Celsus is a beautiful facade and a number of scrolls; behind it was a son's endowment of 25,000 denarii and a reading room that most Ephesians probably could not use, because they could not read. The script of the Grand Bazaar is carpets; right beside it was a book market where copyists sold handmade books for almost three centuries after Gutenberg.

### Cost of Delaying Technology

The **cost of delaying technology** is what a society loses in the years between the arrival of a better way to store or spread knowledge and its adoption: the books not printed, the readers not made, the ideas that traveled more slowly than they could have. The clearest case on our route is Chapter 6's gap of about 275 years between Gutenberg's Bible and İbrahim Müteferrika's first book in the Arabic script, in 1729. Istanbul had Jewish, Armenian, and Greek presses long before that, so the delay was not ignorance; Chapter 6 found several reasons working together, from reverence for the handwritten Quran to the copyists' livelihoods and the authorities' wish to control what was written.

Chapter 6 also warned against the easy moral: even after the press came, most people could not read the script, so printing made copies cheap but did not make readers. And waiting has an opposite cost. *AI-Native Schools* opens its chapter [Implementing the AI-Native Model Responsibly](https://dmccreary.github.io/ai-native-schools/chapters/21-implementing-ai-native-model-responsibly/) with the risk of rushed procurement, a school buying AI faster than it can judge it. The lesson of the delayed press is not "adopt everything at once," but "count what waiting costs, and who pays it."

### Scribal Resistance

**Scribal resistance** is the opposition to a new knowledge technology from the people whose skill, income, or prestige depended on the old one. Our route was full of it, and four cases stand out.

- **Homer's reciters,** the *rhapsodes*, kept their prestige for centuries after the poems were written down (Chapter 8).
- **Demetrius the silversmith** called together his fellow craftsmen at Ephesus against a message that threatened their trade in silver shrines, and the riot filled the Great Theater (Chapters 10 and 11).
- **Ottoman copyists and calligraphers** lived by handwriting, and the craft's prestige and jobs were at stake when printing came (Chapter 6).
- **The abbot Trithemius** wrote *In Praise of Scribes* in 1492, urging monks to keep copying by hand, and had it printed (Chapter 20).

Chapter 11 drew the lesson that matters most: these people were not irrational. Demetrius was right about his market, and the copyists were right that the press would end their way of life. Chapter 4 added that scribes were also gatekeepers, choosing in the 9th century which books to copy into the new script. Resistance is information about who stands to lose, and the scribes' successors today, teachers, aides, tutoring companies, and textbook publishers, are owed what Chapter 11 called a hearing.

## Part Two: Good News and Its Shadow

### Costs of Top-Down Reform

The **costs of top-down reform** are the losses that come with a change decided by a ruler or government and imposed on the people it affects, without their choice or consent: the knowledge, trust, and ways of life that a faster, more uniform change sweeps away. Top-down reform can work, and quickly. It can also leave people feeling that the new learning belongs to someone else, the difference Chapter 2 drew between doing something *with* a community and doing it *to* one.

Our route held several examples of different sizes. In 529, Justinian decided by law who could teach, and the school that had taught philosophy in Athens for centuries came to an end soon afterward, though historians debate how directly the law caused it (Chapter 4). In 161 BCE the Roman Senate expelled Greek philosophers and teachers of rhetoric from Rome (Chapter 12). And in 1923, two governments agreed on the compulsory exchange of about 1.2 million Orthodox Christians and about 400,000 Muslims, none of whom could return (Chapter 7). That convention sorted people by religion, not by the language they spoke, so Turkish-speaking Christians from Cappadocia went to Greece and Greek-speaking Muslims from Crete went to Turkey. A rule drawn far away decided where millions would live, and it did not ask what language they spoke at home. The largest education reform we met, the alphabet of 1928, is the hardest to judge, because it worked.

**Worked example: a ledger for the 1928 alphabet.** Let's set the reform's gains and costs side by side, using only what Chapter 7 reported, and then ask which costs came from the new letters and which from the way the change was made.

| Gains | Costs |
|---|---|
| A script whose first step takes weeks, not years | Within one generation, most Turks could not read their grandparents' letters or the inscriptions on fountains and gravestones |
| Free evening classes for adults across the country from January 1, 1929, and, by the government's own count, more than a million certificates in the first years | The classes reached towns faster than villages and men faster than women, and many adults lost the skill without books to practice on |
| Literacy from about one person in ten (1927) to one in five (1935), and well over 95 percent today | Imposed by a one-party government without a vote; the Arabic script banned from public use |
| From 1940, Village Institutes trained teachers from village families to go home and teach | A campaign from 1932 to replace Arabic and Persian words widened the gap with the past |

Now sort the costs. Some came with any change of script: once schools teach new letters, children stop learning the old ones unless someone teaches both. Others came from the method. The ban, the absence of a vote, and the speed set by a one-party state were choices, and they are why the reform is still argued over, now that Ottoman Turkish has returned as a subject in some Turkish schools. Notice, too, that even this reform held a piece built closer to communities: the Village Institutes, which trained teachers from village families and sent them home to teach. A ledger like this does not say whether the reform was right. It says that speed bought by command is paid for later, often by the grandchildren.

The AI parallel is close. *AI-Native Schools* describes, in [Sustaining Change: Systems Thinking and National Adoption](https://dmccreary.github.io/ai-native-schools/chapters/18-sustaining-change-systems-thinking/), a national rollout of AI textbooks that, as reported, met such strong objections from teachers and parents that within months they were reclassified as supplementary materials. A mandate can move a country fast. It cannot make people trust what it brings.

### Education Evangelism

Chapter 9 traced the word *euangelion*, "good news," from Homer's reward for a messenger, through a calendar inscription at Priene in honor of Augustus, to Paul's letters, and the word **evangelist**, a bearer of good news, to Apple's "software evangelists" in the 1980s. **Education evangelism** is the work of persuading schools, families, and governments to adopt a new way of teaching that the evangelist believes will help learners, today most often AI tutoring and intelligent textbooks. Dan, as Chapter 9 said, would describe himself as an evangelist for AI in education. Chapter 9 also offered a test for any evangelist, that news is only good news if the hearer finds it good, and read Paul's Ephesian years for method alone, setting his message aside.

**Worked example: running Paul's method on a school pitch.** Let's take a pitch an evangelist might give a rural school district, "AI tutors will give every child a personal tutor," and ask of it the questions that each of Paul's practices suggests.

| Paul's practice (Chapter 9) | The question for an AI evangelist | What a good answer sounds like |
|---|---|---|
| Went to a hub | Where will others see it working? | One pilot school that nearby schools can visit |
| Worked alongside people in their trade | Who in the district does this with us? | Two veteran teachers who help design it and can say no |
| Spoke their language | Does the tutor speak the children's language? | Yes, checked by teachers who speak it |
| Stayed long enough | Who keeps it going after the launch? | A local team and a budget for year four |
| Built a team that led without him | Who leads it if the evangelist leaves? | A teacher named in the plan |
| Answered actual questions | What do families here worry about? | We asked them first, and the plan answers privacy and screen time |
| Met opposition | Who stands to lose? | Aides and tutors, given new roles in writing |

The pitch itself does not change. What changes is that every answer in the last column involves someone from the district. That is the difference between announcing good news and spreading it, and it leads straight to the shadow.

### Evangelism's Shadow

**Evangelism's shadow** is the harm that follows when good news is imposed on people rather than chosen by them, or when the people who carry it come to treat those who decline it with contempt. Chapter 11 named it as a limit of the Paul parallel: Paul's hearers chose to join and could leave, but good news has too often been imposed, "in the history of missions and of school reform alike."

The darkest form of the shadow on our route was the one Chapter 11 told with care. Paul wrote of his own people with "great sorrow," and pictured Gentile believers as branches grafted onto Israel's olive tree, warned not to boast. Later Christians quoted his harshest lines and forgot the olive tree, and what the historian Jules Isaac called "the teaching of contempt" ran from John Chrysostom's sermons in Antioch, through Luther's tract of 1543, to the Nazis who reprinted it, and to Rhodes, whose Jewish children were deported in 1944 (Chapter 12). After the Holocaust, churches began to repudiate that teaching, as *Nostra Aetate* did in 1965. Chapter 11 showed that Paul's own words point in more than one direction; the shadow grew when later carriers of the message chose the harshest lines and came to see the other as an error to be corrected rather than a people to be heard.

Schooling has its own version. Missions have often printed the first books in a people's language, and mission and state schools have also, too often, punished children for speaking it. In Canada, for example, the Truth and Reconciliation Commission reported in 2015 that many Indigenous children in the church-run residential schools were punished for speaking their own languages. The intention was to bring education. The method taught children that their language, and so their families, stood in the way of it.

!!! mascot-warning "The Evangelist's Blind Spot"
    ![Glaux warning](../../img/mascot/warning.png){ class="mascot-admonition-img" }
    A common trap for anyone sure their news is good is to hear every objection as misunderstanding. When someone resists, before explaining again, write down what they would lose and what they know about their own place that we don't; then answer that list, or change the plan.

**Worked example: the shadow test.** Let's take three questions that would have caught each shadow on our route, and put them to the same AI pitch from the section above.

1. **Who chose?** The alphabet of 1928 was chosen for Turks, not by them. For the AI pitch: did the district ask for it, or was it offered as a gift that cannot be refused?
2. **What is treated as the obstacle?** The residential schools treated a child's language as the obstacle to learning. For the AI pitch: does the plan treat the community's language, teachers, or ways of teaching as something to work through, or something to get around?
3. **What happens to those who say no?** Paul's later readers turned his words against those who declined the message. For the AI pitch: can a teacher or family opt out without being labeled backward, and will the plan still serve their children?

A pitch that passes all three can still fail, but it will not fail in the shadow's way.

## Part Three: Learning With Communities

### Mother-Tongue Learning

**Mother-tongue learning** is teaching and learning in the language a child speaks at home, especially in the early years of school, so that learning to read and learning new ideas do not both have to wait until the child has learned a second language. Chapter 9 showed the opposite approach: Koine carried Paul's letters because many peoples had learned one common language, and over centuries it crowded out the older languages of Anatolia. Our route showed a smaller version of the same divide: the *Erotokritos*, a Cretan poem printed only in 1713, lived for centuries in villagers' memories and songs, while the printed classics of Chapter 14 spoke Latin and Greek to men who had been to school.

UNESCO's position has been consistent for a long time. In 1953 a UNESCO report on vernacular languages in education called it axiomatic that "the best medium for teaching is the mother tongue of the pupil." UNESCO's Global Education Monitoring Report returned to the point in a 2016 policy paper whose title asks, "If you don't understand, how can you learn?", and estimated that as much as 40 percent of the world's people do not have access to education in a language they speak or understand. The research behind this position generally finds that children learn to read more easily in a language they already speak, and that a strong start in the first language supports learning a second, rather than holding it back. It also finds that mother-tongue programs succeed only with trained teachers and books in that language, which is exactly where the copyists' bottleneck of Chapter 4 reappears: most of the world's languages have little written material, in print or online.

### AI Translation

**AI translation** is the use of machine-learning systems, now mostly large language models and related translation models, to translate speech and writing between languages, and to read text aloud or transcribe it. Chapter 9 named its promise: instead of everyone learning one common language, as Paul's world learned Koine, each learner can keep their own language and still reach ideas written in any other. For mother-tongue learning that changes the arithmetic. A story, a science lesson, or a page of this guide could be put into a small language for a tiny fraction of the cost of a human translation, and read aloud to a child who cannot yet read it.

The risk is the copyists' filter of Chapter 4. AI systems learn from digitized text, most of it in a handful of large languages, so they translate those well and smaller languages poorly, often fluently and wrongly. The remedy is to involve the speakers. Chapter 7 offered a hopeful twist in the same spirit: researchers are now training text-recognition systems on the Ottoman script, so the tools of our time may reopen archives that the reform of 1928 closed.

    ![Glaux warning](../../img/mascot/warning.png){ class="mascot-admonition-img" }
    Watch out for machine translation into a language you can't read: it will sound smooth whether or not it is correct, especially in languages with little text online. Before any translated lesson reaches a child, have a fluent speaker from that community read or listen to a sample, as *AI-Native Schools* advises in [Measuring AI Capability: The METR Task-Horizon Studies](https://dmccreary.github.io/ai-native-schools/chapters/04-measuring-ai-capability-metr/), and fix what they find.

### Education With Communities

**Education with communities** is the practice of designing, running, and judging an effort to widen learning together with the people it is meant to serve, in their language, with their consent, and with their hands on how it continues, rather than delivering it to them from outside. It is the positive half of Chapter 2's distinction between *with* and *to*, and the trip offered both halves. The Greeks borrowed Phoenician letters for their own purposes; as Chapter 2 put it, the alphabet spread because people chose it, not because it was imposed. The ibn Nahmias brothers, refugees from Spain, printed a code of Jewish law for their own community, in its own script, in 1493 or perhaps a decade later (Chapter 6). Andrew Carnegie paid for library buildings only if the town provided the land and promised to pay for books, staff, and upkeep every year, so that each town was invested in its library (Chapter 10). And Turkey's Village Institutes trained village teachers to go home and teach.

*AI-Native Schools* reaches the same conclusion from modern cases. Its chapter [AI-First Case Studies II: Lessons for Practice](https://dmccreary.github.io/ai-native-schools/chapters/23-ai-first-case-studies-ii-lessons/) finds, looking across a run of failed school-technology projects, that the technology mostly worked as designed: what failed was planning, trust, and listening, as when students walked out of school and told a district, loudly, what a program had missed.

## Writing Our Own Good News

### Good News Message

A **good news message** is a short statement, in our own words, of why something about learning is good news, drawn from one real story and written so that the people it concerns would find it good. Chapter 11 promised that on the flight home we would write our own, and the travel journal of Chapter 2 has been practice for it: one thing we noticed, written the same day, in our own words. A good news message adds three tests from this chapter: it comes from a story we actually saw, it is honest about the cost, and it names who the good news is with. Chapter 9 supplies a fourth: the people it concerns must hear it as good.

**Worked example: the fountain we could not read.** Let's write one from Day 3. Just outside the Imperial Gate of Topkapı stands the Fountain of Ahmed III, carved with a poem that most Istanbulites today cannot read (Chapter 7). Here is a first draft, under a hundred words.

> *Outside the gate of Topkapı Palace is a fountain carved with a poem that most people in Istanbul can no longer read, because in 1928 their country changed its letters. The change taught a nation to read, and it cost them their grandparents' handwriting. Researchers are now teaching machines to read the old script. The good news is not that a machine can read the fountain. It is that a Turkish student, with her teacher beside her and the tool speaking her language, might choose to read it herself.*

Now test it. *A story we saw:* yes, the fountain, on Day 3. *Honest about the cost:* yes, it names what the reform took as well as what it gave. *With whom:* a student and her teacher, in Turkish, by her own choice. *Heard as good:* here the draft is careful on purpose. How much Ottoman Turkish every Turk should learn is part of today's politics, so a visitor's message should not take a side; it says "might choose," and leaves the choice with the people whose history it is.

!!! mascot-tip "Write It for One Reader"
    ![Glaux giving a tip](../../img/mascot/tip.png){ class="mascot-admonition-img" }
    Here's a way to get the draft right on the first try: before writing, name one real person the story concerns, a teacher, a grandmother, a student, and write the message as if they will read it over your shoulder. Then cut every sentence they would not find good.

### Lessons Across the Route

**Lessons across the route** are the conclusions we draw by reading all of the trip's stories together rather than one at a time, and the questions they leave for our own work. Chapter 11 ended with three leadership questions and Chapter 19 with seven Delphi questions; these are the last ones in the guide. Each takes a sentence or two in the travel journal, and the flight is long enough for all of them.

1. **Which story will I retell first,** and what will I say behind its tourist script?
2. **Where did a delay cost the most,** in the trip's stories or in my own work, and who paid for the waiting?
3. **Who is my scribe?** Whose skill or living depends on the way things are done now, and what would a fair hearing look like?
4. **Where have I been tempted to move fast by command,** and what would the ledger of that change look like in a generation?
5. **In whose language** would my good news have to be told, and who would I ask to check the translation?
6. **For Dan, the evangelist's question:** in the next school I talk to about AI, what will I ask before I explain, and how will I know whether the people there hear it as good news?
7. **My own good news message:** from one story I actually saw on this trip, write it in under a hundred words, and test it the way the worked example did.

## Key Takeaways

- **Seeing past the tourist script** meant asking at every stop who got to learn and who was left out; gathered in one table, the Learning for All stories follow Chapter 2's four-step pattern almost everywhere.
- The **cost of delaying technology** is real, as Chapter 6's press about 275 years late shows, but printing made copies, not readers, and rushing has costs too.
- **Scribal resistance**, from Homer's reciters to Demetrius and Trithemius, is information about who stands to lose, and they are owed a hearing.
- The **costs of top-down reform** show in the 1928 alphabet's ledger: fast gains, and a generation cut off from its past, partly by choices of method.
- **Education evangelism** persuades people to adopt a better way of learning; Paul's method turns a pitch into questions that involve the people it concerns.
- **Evangelism's shadow** falls when good news is imposed or its hearers despised. Ask who chose, what is treated as the obstacle, and what happens to those who say no.
- **Mother-tongue learning** has long been UNESCO's position, and **AI translation** could make it affordable in small languages, if fluent speakers check its work.
- **Education with communities** designs, runs, and judges a change with the people it serves; those left out at the end are usually those absent at the start. The **AI-Native Schools companion** works this out for schools.
- A **good news message** comes from a story we saw, is honest about the cost, and names who it is with; the **lessons across the route** end the guide with questions for our own work.

## Where to Go Next

This trip took us through two countries that share a sea, a long history, and many of the same roots. There are many places in the world we have yet to visit, and each could give us a deeper appreciation for other cultures and traditions. The best ones to choose are the places where people **do not think like you**. In a place like that, the tourist script runs out sooner. Habits we took for granted turn out to be choices, and we have to ask the question this guide asked at every stop: who got to learn here, and how?

Three places are at the top of my own list.

- **Egypt.** We have already stood beside an Egyptian stone: the obelisk of Thutmose III in the Hippodrome, carved about 1450 BCE. In Egypt it would stand among the temples it was made for, carved in hieroglyphs, a writing system that only a small class of trained scribes could read for most of its three thousand years.
- **China.** Chinese writing is not an alphabet; each character carries meaning, and learning to read means learning thousands of them. China also had a long tradition of choosing officials by written examination, which put a particular idea of education at the center of public life for more than a thousand years.
- **Thailand.** Thailand is a Buddhist country, and for centuries its monasteries were also village schools, where boys learned to read alongside their religious training. Watching how learning and faith fit together there would set the Christian and Muslim stories from this route in a new light.

Japan and India are not on the list, but only because I have been there already. I traveled to Japan, and I lived in India as a child. Both taught me early what this guide has tried to teach since Chapter 2: the most valuable part of a trip is finding out that sensible people can see the world very differently from the way you do.

One more place is in Turkey itself. **Göbekli Tepe**, a hilltop near Şanlıurfa in the southeast, holds circles of carved T-shaped limestone pillars, some over five meters tall, decorated with foxes, boars, snakes, and vultures. Radiocarbon dates show the oldest structures were built about 9500 BCE, roughly 11,500 years ago. That is about 6,500 years before Stonehenge, and before farming, pottery, or writing. On Chapter 1's long timeline it would sit far to the left of anything we visited on this trip. The research there is still changing what we know. The site's first excavator, Klaus Schmidt, read it as a temple where no one lived. Later digs found houses and cisterns for collecting rain, so people did live there. Since 2021, a project called *Taş Tepeler* ("Stone Hills") has been excavating a dozen related sites nearby, including Karahan Tepe. The dates and their meaning are still debated, which is part of the attraction: it is a place where the story is being written while we watch.

I wish all of you happy travels. We sincerely hope this book has inspired you not only to know thyself better, as the inscription at Delphi advised, but also to appreciate the diversity of our world.
