# Quiz: Before We Go, Part 1: The Long Timeline of the Eastern Mediterranean

Test your understanding of the map, the eras, and the civilizations of the Eastern Mediterranean with these review questions.

---

#### 1. How do historians in this book define a civilization?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. A society whose art, music, and moral ideas are more advanced than its neighbors'
2. A complex society with cities, a food surplus, specialists, government, records, and monumental buildings
3. Any group of people who share one language, one religion, and one ruling family
4. A society that has learned to work iron and trade it across the sea
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **B**. The chapter uses "civilization" in a narrow, neutral sense: a complex society built around cities, where a food surplus supports specialists, a government organizes large projects, people keep records, and monumental buildings express shared beliefs. It is a checklist, not a ranking, so option A, which treats civilization as a grade of advancement, is exactly the everyday meaning the chapter sets aside. Language, religion, or iron-working alone do not make a civilization.

    **Concept Tested:** Civilization

    **See:** [Civilization](index.md#civilization)

#### 2. What is stratigraphy, the most important idea in archaeology?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Dating a site by counting the coins and inscriptions found on its surface
2. Measuring how fast carbon decays in seeds, bones, and charcoal
3. Comparing the stories in Homer's poems with the ruins found at a site
4. Studying the layers of a site, where deeper layers are older in undisturbed ground
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **D**. Stratigraphy is the study of layers. When people live in one place for a long time they build on the rubble of earlier buildings, so each layer holds the remains of one period, and in undisturbed ground the deeper layers are older. Radiocarbon dating (option B) and coins and inscriptions (option A) are tools archaeologists use to date those layers, but they are not stratigraphy itself. Matching ruins to Homer is what led Schliemann astray.

    **Concept Tested:** Archaeology

    **See:** [Archaeology](index.md#archaeology)

#### 3. Why do archaeologists consider Heinrich Schliemann's "Priam's Treasure" at Troy a famous error?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. The gold came from Troy II, about a thousand years too early, and his trench damaged the layers closer to Homer's world
2. The gold turned out to be a modern forgery that Schliemann had bought from local dealers
3. The gold came from Troy IX, a Roman city built long after the Trojan War
4. The gold was found at Knossos on Crete and only later moved to Troy
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **A**. Schliemann dug a huge trench toward the deepest layers, expecting Homer's Troy there. The hoard he named after King Priam actually came from Troy II, about a thousand years before any plausible date for the Trojan War, and his trench destroyed parts of Troy VI and VII, the layers closer to Homer's world. The chapter never calls the gold a forgery, and Troy IX is the top, latest layer, not the source of the hoard.

    **Concept Tested:** Archaeology

    **See:** [Archaeology](index.md#archaeology)

#### 4. A sign at the Acropolis notes an event of 480 BCE. Using the chapter's rule for spans that cross from BCE to CE, how many years separate that event from our trip in 2026 CE?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. 2,506 years
2. 1,546 years
3. 2,505 years
4. 2,507 years
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **C**. Because there was no year zero, 1 BCE is followed directly by 1 CE. To measure a span that crosses the line, add the two numbers and subtract one: 480 + 2026 − 1 = 2,505 years. Option A, 2,506, is the common mistake of simply adding the two years. Option B subtracts 480 from 2026, which treats a BCE year as if it were a CE year. The same rule produces the 5,225 years used in the chapter's 24-hour clock.

    **Concept Tested:** BCE and CE Dating

    **See:** [BCE and CE Dating](index.md#bce-and-ce-dating)

#### 5. What is the main lesson of squeezing 5,225 years of history into a single 24-hour clock?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Most important events in the region happened in the last few hundred years
2. Every period on the timeline lasted roughly the same amount of time
3. Proportion: the Bronze Age fills the morning, while the Classical period lasts only 43 minutes
4. The Byzantine period was too short to leave many buildings on our route
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **C**. A timeline's value lies more in proportion than in any single date. On the 24-hour clock the Bronze Age fills the whole morning, the Classical period that most people mean by "ancient Greece" lasts only 43 minutes, and the Byzantine period takes more than five hours of the evening. That directly contradicts option D, and it shows the periods were very unequal in length, which rules out option B.

    **Concept Tested:** Historical Timeline

    **See:** [The Historical Timeline](index.md#the-historical-timeline)

#### 6. Which description best fits the Pax Romana?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. About two centuries of unusual internal peace, from Augustus in 27 BCE to the death of Marcus Aurelius in 180 CE
2. A peace treaty between Rome and Persia that ended the wars of Alexander's successors
3. The years after Constantine founded Constantinople, when the empire became Christian
4. A short truce in 67 BCE after Pompey cleared the Mediterranean of pirates
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **A**. The Pax Romana, "Roman peace," was a period of about two centuries, from Augustus becoming emperor in 27 BCE to the death of Marcus Aurelius in 180 CE, when most provinces enjoyed internal stability. Pompey's pirate campaign of 67 BCE (option D) was one of its practical foundations, not the peace itself. The chapter also stresses its price: the peace was imposed by force and paid for with heavy provincial taxes.

    **Concept Tested:** Pax Romana

    **See:** [The Pax Romana](index.md#the-pax-romana)

#### 7. Paul was a Jew from Tarsus writing to communities ruled by Rome, including Rome itself. Why did he write his letters in Greek?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Latin was forbidden for religious writing in the first century CE
2. He had been trained as a philosopher in Athens, where only Greek was spoken
3. Greek was the official language of the Roman government in Rome
4. Alexander's conquests had made Koine Greek the shared language of the eastern Mediterranean
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **D**. The Hellenistic period answers the puzzle. Three centuries before Paul, Alexander's conquests had spread Greek across the East, and Koine, "common" Greek, became the everyday language of trade and government. Even under Rome the East kept speaking it, and many people in Rome itself spoke it too, so one letter could be read aloud in Ephesus, Corinth, and Rome. Option C misses the point: Greek spread through everyday trade and travel as a lingua franca, the way English does today, not by Roman decree.

    **Concept Tested:** Hellenistic Period

    **See:** [The Hellenistic Period](index.md#the-hellenistic-period)

#### 8. What is Anatolia?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. The European part of Turkey around Istanbul, west of the Bosphorus
2. The great peninsula forming the Asian part of Turkey, named from the Greek word for "sunrise"
3. The chain of islands in the southeastern Aegean that includes Rhodes
4. The region around Athens that formed the territory of the ancient city
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **B**. Anatolia is the great peninsula that makes up the Asian part of Turkey, about 97 percent of the country. Its name comes from the Greek *anatolē*, "sunrise," because it was the land toward the rising sun, and older books call it Asia Minor. Option C describes the Dodecanese, and option D describes Attica. The chapter stresses that some of the most important Greek cities, such as Troy, Ephesus, and Miletus, were in Anatolia.

    **Concept Tested:** Anatolia

    **See:** [Anatolia](index.md#anatolia)

#### 9. In 405 BCE Sparta destroyed the Athenian fleet on the Dardanelles, and in 220 BCE Rhodes went to war over a toll on the Bosphorus. What idea do both stories illustrate?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Whoever held a narrow chokepoint on the sea lanes could control trade, food, and power
2. Large land armies, not navies, decided most wars in the ancient Aegean
3. Greek city-states rarely fought each other once Persia had been defeated
4. Tolls on shipping made the Bosphorus too costly for traders to use
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **A**. Both stories show the power of chokepoints, narrow places where land squeezes the sea. With its fleet gone, Athens could not protect the grain ships coming through the Dardanelles and surrendered. Rhodes, whose wealth came from free trade, fought Byzantium until the Bosphorus tolls were dropped. In both cases the decisive weapon was a strip of water, not an army, which rules out option B. The Rhodian victory also means the tolls did not shut the strait (option D).

    **Concept Tested:** Trade Routes

    **See:** [Trade Routes: The Thread Through Every Era](index.md#trade-routes-the-thread-through-every-era)

#### 10. At Ephesus a site guide calls a building from 100 BCE "Roman," while our timeline places the same date in the Hellenistic period. What is the best way to analyze the disagreement?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. The guide is wrong, because the Roman period everywhere begins with the Battle of Actium in 31 BCE
2. Our timeline is wrong, because the Hellenistic period ended when Alexander died in 323 BCE
3. The building must have been rebuilt by the Romans, so the guide is describing a later layer
4. Both are using a valid definition: Rome reached Ephesus before 31 BCE, while our timeline uses one region-wide boundary
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **D**. The Roman period is an era label for a place: the years when that place was under Roman rule. Rome arrived at different stops at different times, reaching Ephesus about a century before Rhodes, so a local label can say "Roman" for a date our region-wide timeline still calls Hellenistic. Neither is wrong, which rules out options A and B. The first question is always which definition a label is using; nothing suggests the building was rebuilt.

    **Concept Tested:** Roman Period

    **See:** [The Roman Period](index.md#the-roman-period)
