# Quiz: Day 9, Part 1: Knossos, the Minoans, and Linear B

Test your understanding of the palace of Knossos, Minoan art and myth, the scripts of Bronze Age Crete, and the decipherment of Linear B with these review questions.

---

#### 1. On the Grand Staircase at Knossos you see red columns that are wider at the top than at the bottom, holding up an upper floor. How should you read what is in front of you?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. As original Minoan wooden columns, preserved by the fire that ended the palace
2. As Arthur Evans's reconstruction, mainly in reinforced concrete from the 1920s, painted in the Minoan column shape
3. As a Roman rebuilding of the palace for visitors in the 2nd century CE
4. As a modern copy built after 2000 to replace stone columns that collapsed
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **B**. The palace's wooden columns and beams had burned or rotted away, so Evans rebuilt rooms, stairways, and upper floors between about 1905 and 1930, first with wood and stone and from the 1920s mainly in reinforced concrete. His architects rebuilt the Grand Staircase and painted concrete columns red, in the Minoan shape that widens toward the top. So the site must be read as three things together: Minoan remains, Evans's rebuilding, and modern copies. No original wooden column survives.

    **Concept Tested:** Evans Reconstructions

    **See:** [Evans Reconstructions](index.md#evans-reconstructions)

#### 2. Evans called the gypsum seat in the Throne Room "the throne of Minos." What does the chapter say we actually know about it?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. King Minos sat there, as an inscription on the seat records
2. It was brought from mainland Greece by the Mycenaeans as war booty
3. The seat is original and in place, but the room's present form dates after about 1450 BCE, and who sat there is unknown
4. It is a concrete copy made by Evans, since the original seat was destroyed
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **C**. The high-backed gypsum seat is one of the few things in the palace exactly where the Bronze Age left it, so option D is wrong. But the room in its present form dates from the last phase, after about 1450 BCE, when Mycenaeans ruled Knossos. Who sat there is unknown; some scholars suggest a priestess appearing as a goddess between her guardian griffins, which fits the many Minoan images of powerful women and the absence of any clear image of a king. No inscription names Minos.

    **Concept Tested:** Throne Room at Knossos

    **See:** [The Throne Room at Knossos](index.md#the-throne-room-at-knossos)

#### 3. How did the palace economy of Knossos work?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. The palace collected produce and labor, stored and recorded them, and paid them out as rations, wages, and offerings
2. Farmers sold their olive oil in a central market and paid the palace a tax in silver coins
3. Each village kept its own harvest, and the palace traded only in foreign luxuries
4. Merchants owned the storerooms and rented space to the palace officials
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **A**. The palace economy worked by redistribution: goods flowed into a center and back out, directed by officials, rather than being bought and sold in a market. A village's share of olive oil arrived in jars, a scribe recorded it on a clay tablet, and the jars went into the west storerooms. Later the oil went out as rations to workers, raw material for perfume makers, and offerings to the gods, each issue with its own tablet. No market sale was involved, which rules out option B.

    **Concept Tested:** Palace Economy

    **See:** [Palace Economy](index.md#palace-economy)

#### 4. In 1939 Spyridon Marinatos proposed that the Thera eruption destroyed Minoan civilization. Why has better dating changed that verdict?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. The eruption turned out to be small, leaving only a thin layer of ash on Thera
2. The eruption happened after the palaces were already abandoned, around 1100 BCE
3. Akrotiri was found to be a Mycenaean town, so its loss did not affect the Minoans
4. The palaces carried on for more than a century after about 1600 BCE, so the eruption weakened Minoan Crete but did not end it
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **D**. The Thera eruption, around 1600 BCE, was one of the largest of the last ten thousand years. It buried the Minoan-style town at Akrotiri, ash fell on eastern Crete, and debris suggests a tsunami reached the north coast, so the claim that it weakened Minoan Crete is likely. But the palaces carried on for more than a century afterward, and most were destroyed about 1450 BCE, so the claim that it destroyed Minoan civilization is not supported. Option A contradicts the eruption's enormous scale.

    **Concept Tested:** Thera Eruption

    **See:** [Thera Eruption](index.md#thera-eruption)

#### 5. In the Heraklion museum you stand before a complete-looking Minoan fresco, bright from edge to edge. What should you assume about it?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. It was lifted whole from a palace wall, where the ash of Thera protected it
2. It is a modern painting with no ancient material in it
3. It is a reconstruction: real fragments set into new plaster, with the missing areas painted in by a restorer
4. It was repainted by the Mycenaeans after they took over Knossos
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **C**. Almost no Minoan paintings survived on the walls. They fell when the palaces burned and collapsed and came out of the ground as heaps of small fragments, so every fresco in the museum is a reconstruction of real fragments in new plaster. The "Saffron Gatherer," restored as a boy picking crocuses, turned out to be a blue monkey. The ash of Thera protected paintings at Akrotiri, not at Knossos, which rules out option A. Option B goes too far, since real fragments are included.

    **Concept Tested:** Minoan Frescoes

    **See:** [Minoan Frescoes](index.md#minoan-frescoes)

#### 6. What is the firmest evidence that a place called the Labyrinth really existed at Mycenaean Knossos?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. A Linear A tablet that draws a map of the maze beneath the palace
2. A Linear B tablet recording an offering of honey to "the Mistress of the Labyrinth"
3. Homer's description of Daedalus building the maze for King Minos
4. The double axes carved on the palace blocks, which prove the name means "house of the double axe"
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **B**. A Linear B tablet from Knossos records an offering of honey to "the Mistress of the Labyrinth," so a place or shrine with that name existed at Mycenaean Knossos. The link between "labyrinth" and the *labrys*, the Minoan double axe, is popular, but linguists are divided on it, so option D overstates it. The myth of the Minotaur and the maze was written down centuries after the palace fell. Linear A is undeciphered, so no tablet in it can be read as a map.

    **Concept Tested:** Labyrinth

    **See:** [Labyrinth](index.md#labyrinth)

#### 7. Linear A is undeciphered, yet scholars agree that the word *ku-ro* means "total." How did they work this out?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. A bilingual tablet gave the Greek word for "total" beside it
2. Michael Ventris guessed it from a Greek word that sounds similar
3. *Ku-ro* is written in large letters, so it must be the most important word on each tablet
4. The number after *ku-ro* at the bottom of account tablets equals the sum of the numbers above it
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **D**. Many Linear A signs look like Linear B signs with known sounds, so scholars can sound out *ku-ro* without knowing the Minoan language. Its meaning came from arithmetic: on account tablets from Hagia Triada, the number after *ku-ro* at the bottom equals the sum of the entries above. A scribe's habit of adding up a column told more than staring at the signs. Linear A has no bilingual text and no strong guess about its language, which is why it remains unread.

    **Concept Tested:** Linear A

    **See:** [Linear A](index.md#linear-a)

#### 8. What makes the Phaistos Disc unique among ancient texts?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Its signs were pressed into wet clay with carved stamps, making it the oldest known text printed with reusable "type"
2. It is the longest Minoan text ever found, with thousands of signs
3. It is the bilingual tablet that allowed Linear B to be deciphered
4. It was written in the Greek alphabet centuries before Homer
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **A**. The Phaistos Disc, found in 1908 and usually dated to about 1700 BCE, carries 241 signs of 45 kinds in a spiral on both sides. Each sign was pressed into the wet clay with a carved stamp, one stamp per kind, so it is the oldest known text printed with reusable type, more than 3,000 years before Gutenberg. But it is the only object with these signs, and 241 signs are far too few to decipher, so it is neither long (option B) nor a key to Linear B.

    **Concept Tested:** Phaistos Disc

    **See:** [Phaistos Disc](index.md#phaistos-disc)

#### 9. A school plans to run all of its core teaching on one company's AI system, with student work stored in that company's formats. Applying the lesson of Linear B, what should the school do?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Proceed as planned, since a large company is unlikely to fail
2. Avoid AI entirely, since any shared tool will eventually be lost
3. Spread the knowledge widely, keep work in open formats, keep teachers skilled in what the machines do, and know how to leave
4. Restrict the AI to a few trusted staff so the knowledge stays under close control
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **C**. Linear B, held by a few dozen palace scribes for one narrow purpose, vanished when the palaces fell. The modern form is concentrated AI risk: teaching held inside one company's system can be lost, priced out of reach, or controlled overnight. The defenses are the ones the scribes lacked: spread knowledge widely, keep it in open formats, keep teachers skilled, and know how to leave. Option D repeats the scribes' mistake by concentrating knowledge in a few hands.

    **Concept Tested:** Concentrated AI Risk

    **See:** [What It Means for AI](index.md#what-it-means-for-ai)

#### 10. Why does the chapter insist that the decipherment of Linear B be told with Alice Kober in it?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. She was the first to read Linear B as Greek, before Ventris
2. Her years of groundwork on about 180,000 index cards revealed the script's structure, and Ventris used her method
3. She found the Pylos tripod tablet that proved the decipherment
4. She controlled the Knossos tablets and published them all in her lifetime
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **B**. Kober refused to guess the language and instead found its structure from sign patterns, building her own database of about 180,000 hand-cut index cards. Her "triplets" showed the language was inflected and that signs were syllables sharing consonants or vowels, the start of the grid. Ventris read the script as Greek in 1952 using her method, but for decades the story was told as his alone. Credit for groundwork matters today too, for the people who label AI training data. Blegen found the Pylos tablet, and Evans controlled the Knossos texts.

    **Concept Tested:** Credit for Groundwork

    **See:** [Alice Kober](index.md#alice-kober)
