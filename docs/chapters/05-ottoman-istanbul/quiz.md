# Quiz: Day 3: Ottoman Istanbul, Topkapı, and Friday Prayer

Test your understanding of Ottoman Istanbul, Islam as it is lived, Sinan's mosques, and the vakıf endowments with these review questions.

---

#### 1. What does the sequence of gates and courts at Topkapı Palace show about how the palace worked?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Each court was built by a different sultan, so the gates mark the order of construction
2. The gates were defensive, built to stop attackers who had already breached the city walls
3. Each gate filtered who could pass, so the courts grew more private from the public First Court to the sultan's Third Court
4. The courts separated the palace by religion, with Christians and Muslims housed in different courts
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **C**. Topkapı is a sequence of courtyards, each more private than the last. The First Court, through the Imperial Gate, was open to the public. Beyond the Gate of Salutation, only officials on business could enter the Second Court, where the Imperial Council met. The Gate of Felicity led to the Third Court, the sultan's private world. The gates filtered access; the chapter does not describe them as military defenses, construction phases, or religious divisions.

    **Concept Tested:** Topkapı Palace

    **See:** [Topkapı Palace](index.md#topkap-palace)

#### 2. How did the Ottoman sultans come to claim the caliphate, the leadership of the whole Muslim community?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Mehmed II claimed it when he took Constantinople in 1453
2. Suleiman the Magnificent was elected caliph by the scholars of Mecca
3. Osman received it from the last Byzantine emperor around 1299
4. Selim I took over the claim when he conquered Egypt in 1517
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **D**. When Selim I conquered Egypt in 1517, the Ottomans took over the claim to the caliphate, along with the Prophet's relics and the protection of the holy cities, and later sultans used the title to speak for Muslims far beyond their borders. The Turkish Republic abolished the caliphate on March 3, 1924. Mehmed II's conquest of 1453 (option A) brought Ottoman titles such as "Caesar of Rome," not the caliphate.

    **Concept Tested:** Caliphate

    **See:** [The Caliphate](index.md#the-caliphate)

#### 3. How does the chapter correct the Western painters' image of the Harem of Topkapı?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. It shows the Harem was a small set of guest rooms rarely used by the sultan's family
2. It describes the Harem as a household, a training school, and a center of politics, led by the sultan's mother
3. It explains that the Harem was a religious school where women studied the Quran
4. It shows the Harem was a prison for princes and had nothing to do with the sultan's family
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **B**. Western painters imagined the Harem as a place of idle pleasure, an image that says more about European fantasies than about the Ottomans. In reality the Harem, a maze of more than 300 rooms, was a household, a training school, and a political center. Its most powerful figure was the valide sultan, the sultan's mother, and during the "Sultanate of Women," figures such as Hürrem and Kösem shaped policy and succession. It housed the whole family, not just guests or princes.

    **Concept Tested:** Harem of Topkapı

    **See:** [The Harem of Topkapı](index.md#the-harem-of-topkap)

#### 4. It is Friday, November 13, and you hope to see the inside of the Süleymaniye Mosque. When should you plan to visit?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Spend midday in a museum, bazaar, or café, and visit the mosque later, after the Friday prayer ends
2. Arrive just before noon, when the doors open wide for the sermon
3. Wait outside the main door during the Friday prayer so you are first in line
4. Visit at sunset, since mosques close to visitors only on Friday mornings
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **A**. Friday prayer, held at midday in place of the ordinary noon prayer, includes a sermon and fills the big mosques well before noon, with worshippers praying in the courtyard and street. It is the longest closure to visitors of the week. The chapter advises being in a museum, bazaar, or café at midday rather than outside a mosque door, then visiting between prayers. Sunset (option D) is itself a prayer time, so it is a poor choice too.

    **Concept Tested:** Friday Prayer

    **See:** [Friday Prayer](index.md#friday-prayer)

#### 5. Inside the Blue Mosque, a man is praying on the carpet between you and the tiles you want to see up close. What does mosque etiquette ask of you?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Walk quickly in front of him, since visitors are allowed anywhere outside prayer times
2. Take a quiet photograph of him praying, as long as the flash is off
3. Ask him to move aside so other visitors can reach the tiles
4. Stay in the visitors' area and never walk directly in front of someone who is praying
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **D**. Mosque etiquette asks visitors to come outside prayer times, dress modestly, take off their shoes before stepping on the carpet, use the visitors' entrance, stay in the visitors' area, and never walk directly in front of someone praying. Photography etiquette adds that we never photograph people at prayer, flash or no flash, which rules out option B. Interrupting a worshipper (option C) would put our visit ahead of his worship, the opposite of being a good guest.

    **Concept Tested:** Mosque Etiquette

    **See:** [Mosque Etiquette](index.md#mosque-etiquette)

#### 6. In Hagia Sophia, the mihrab stands in the old Christian apse but is turned a few degrees off-center. What does that angle reveal?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. The building settled unevenly after the earthquake of 558
2. Ottoman builders placed it to line up with the Blue Mosque across the park
3. Justinian's altar faced east, while prayer faces Mecca to the south-southeast, so the conversion left a readable layer
4. The mihrab was moved during the museum years and put back slightly wrong in 2020
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **C**. The mihrab is a niche in the qibla wall marking the direction of Mecca. In Hagia Sophia it sits in Justinian's apse but is turned slightly to the south, because a church's altar faces east while Muslim prayer faces Mecca, south-southeast of Istanbul. That small angle is a layer we can read without a guide: the evidence of a church converted into a mosque. Nothing in the chapter links it to earthquakes or to the Blue Mosque.

    **Concept Tested:** Mihrab and Minbar

    **See:** [Mihrab and Minbar](index.md#mihrab-and-minbar)

#### 7. Which building did Mimar Sinan call his masterpiece?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. The Selimiye Mosque in Edirne
2. The Süleymaniye Mosque in Istanbul
3. The Şehzade Mosque in Istanbul
4. The Sultan Ahmed (Blue) Mosque in Istanbul
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **A**. Sinan described his career in three buildings. The Şehzade Mosque (1543–1548) was his apprentice work, the Süleymaniye (1550–1557) his journeyman work, and the Selimiye in Edirne (1568–1575) his masterpiece, with a single dome about 31 m across, as wide as Hagia Sophia's, on eight slim piers. The Blue Mosque (1609–1616) was built after Sinan's death by Sedefkâr Mehmed Ağa, who had trained under him.

    **Concept Tested:** Mimar Sinan

    **See:** [Mimar Sinan](index.md#mimar-sinan)

#### 8. The Süleymaniye's dome is narrower than Hagia Sophia's but almost as high. How does that design reflect what a mosque needs?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. A narrow dome forced worshippers into a long line toward the altar, like a church
2. A steeper dome pushes outward less, and with piers built into the walls it creates one wide hall where long rows can face Mecca
3. Sinan lacked the skill to build a dome as wide as Hagia Sophia's at any point in his career
4. A higher dome was needed so that the call to prayer could be given from inside it
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **B**. A church leads the eye along a long axis to the altar; a mosque needs a wide, unified hall where long rows of worshippers stand side by side facing Mecca. The Süleymaniye's dome, about 26.5 m across and 53 m high, is proportionally steeper and pushes outward less. Its piers are partly built into the side walls, so we see one great room. Option C is wrong: Sinan matched Hagia Sophia's width later at Edirne. Option A describes a church plan.

    **Concept Tested:** Ottoman Mosque Architecture

    **See:** [Ottoman Mosque Architecture](index.md#ottoman-mosque-architecture)

#### 9. Walking around the Süleymaniye, you see medreses, a hospital, a soup kitchen, a bath, and rows of shops. Which feature of the vakıf explains how the teachers and cooks were paid for centuries?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. The sultan's treasury paid the staff each year from taxes approved by the council
2. Students paid tuition, and the fees were shared among the staff
3. Property such as the shops was dedicated forever, could never be sold, and its rents paid the staff named in the deed
4. Worshippers left coins at the mihrab each Friday to cover the week's wages
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **C**. A vakıf permanently dedicates property, such as shops, farmland, or baths, so its income pays forever for a stated purpose. Once dedicated, it can never be sold or inherited. The deed, the vakfiye, lists every staff position and its pay, and a trustee manages the property. There was no ministry of education or health, so option A is wrong, and the schools were free, with stipends for students, so option B is wrong. Some vakıfs ran for four hundred years.

    **Concept Tested:** Vakif Endowment

    **See:** [The Vakıf Endowment](index.md#the-vakf-endowment)

#### 10. What lesson for AI in education does the chapter draw from the vakıf?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Free learning should be run directly by the government's budget, not by charities
2. Educational technology should charge fees so that it can pay its own way
3. A school's curriculum should be fixed forever by its founder so that it cannot be corrupted
4. Free learning needs a lasting source of income independent of any one ruler, plus a way to keep changing what it teaches
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **D**. Much educational technology is paid for by grants or investors and disappears when the money stops. The vakıf solved that with a permanent engine of income separated from the whims of any one ruler, providing endowed free education. But the same deeds made the schools hard to change, so the curriculum stayed fixed while the world moved on to print. An AI tutor would need lasting income and what the vakıf lacked, a way to keep changing. Option C repeats the vakıf's weakness.

    **Concept Tested:** Endowed Free Education

    **See:** [Learning for All: Soup, Stipends, and Schools That Lasted Centuries](index.md#learning-for-all-soup-stipends-and-schools-that-lasted-centuries)
