# Quiz: Day 11: Athens, the Agora, Democracy, and Paul on the Areopagus

Test your understanding of the Agora, Athenian democracy, the Stoics, Paul's speech on the Areopagus, and who Athens left out with these review questions.

---

#### 1. Why is the Temple of Hephaestus above the Agora the best-preserved temple of the classical Greek world?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. It was buried by a landslide and dug out whole in the 20th century
2. It was rebuilt in concrete by Arthur Evans's architects in the 1920s
3. It became a Christian church dedicated to St. George and was used as one until 1834
4. It was built of bronze rather than marble, so it could not be burned for lime
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **C**. The Temple of Hephaestus, begun around 449 BCE, honored the god of fire and the forge and overlooked a district of foundries and potters. It survived almost whole because it became a Christian church dedicated to St. George and was used as one until 1834. Standing beside it, we see a Doric temple with its columns, entablature, and pediments still whole. Its continuous use, not burial or a modern rebuilding, is what saved it, metopes of Heracles and Theseus included.

    **Concept Tested:** Temple of Hephaestus

    **See:** [The Temple of Hephaestus](index.md#the-temple-of-hephaestus)

#### 2. What made Athenian democracy direct rather than representative?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Citizens decided in person in the Assembly, and councillors and jurors were chosen by lot rather than elected
2. Citizens elected a parliament that made laws on their behalf for ten-year terms
3. A single elected leader governed with the advice of a council of priests
4. All residents of Attica, including women and enslaved people, voted on every law
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **A**. From 508 to 322 BCE, every adult male citizen could attend, speak, and vote in the Assembly, which met about 40 times a year on the Pnyx. There was no elected parliament. The Council of 500, which prepared the agenda, was chosen by lot for one year, and juries of hundreds were chosen by lot on the morning of a trial. The city paid citizens for public service so the poor could take part. But women, metics, and enslaved people had no vote, which rules out option D.

    **Concept Tested:** Athenian Democracy

    **See:** [Athenian Democracy](index.md#athenian-democracy)

#### 3. Excavators found about 190 ostraka with Themistocles' name, written by only a few hands and never used. What can you infer from this find?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Themistocles wrote the ballots himself to prove how popular he was
2. Someone organized a campaign against him, and some voters needed ready-made ballots because they could not write
3. Ostracism votes were always rigged, so the system never reflected real opinion
4. The ballots were practice pieces from a school where children learned to write
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **B**. Ostracism exiled a leading politician for ten years by a vote scratched on potsherds. Ballots prepared in a few hands, apparently to hand out, show that someone was organizing a campaign against Themistocles, and that some voters needed a ballot already written because they could not write the name themselves. A democracy that voted in writing quietly assumed a literacy not every citizen had. One find of unused ballots does not show every vote was rigged (option C).

    **Concept Tested:** Ostracism

    **See:** [Ostracism](index.md#ostracism)

#### 4. Why does the chapter warn against reading the trial of Socrates in 399 BCE as "democracy versus philosophy"?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Socrates was tried by a Spartan court, not by the Athenian democracy
2. Socrates was acquitted, and the death sentence is a later legend
3. The charge was only about unpaid taxes and had nothing to do with religion
4. Athens had just lost a war and suffered the Thirty Tyrants, and philosophy went on flourishing there for centuries afterward
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **D**. Athens lost the Peloponnesian War in 404 BCE, and the Thirty Tyrants ruled by terror until 403; their leader Critias had been Socrates' companion. Because an amnesty barred prosecutions for acts under the Thirty, an impiety charge could carry an unspoken political one. Socrates had questioned Athenians for decades, his students kept teaching, and Athens remained the philosophy capital for nine centuries. A jury of about 500 citizens convicted him, and he drank hemlock about a month later.

    **Concept Tested:** Trial of Socrates

    **See:** [The Trial of Socrates](index.md#the-trial-of-socrates)

#### 5. Which idea was part of the Stoic teaching that Zeno began in the Painted Stoa around 300 BCE?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Pleasure is the highest good, so a wise person seeks a quiet, comfortable life
2. All humans share in reason, so every person is a citizen of one world, a *kosmopolitēs*
3. Health, wealth, and reputation are the true goods that make a life happy
4. The gods do not exist, so the world runs by pure chance
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **B**. Zeno of Citium taught in the Painted Stoa, which gave the Stoics their name, "the porch people." Three Stoic ideas matter here: the world is ordered by reason, the *logos*; virtue is the only good, while health, wealth, and reputation are "indifferent," which rules out option C; and all humans form one community, since every person shares in reason, making each a *kosmopolitēs*, the source of "cosmopolitan." The Stoics often called that rational force god or Zeus, so option D is wrong.

    **Concept Tested:** Stoicism

    **See:** [Stoicism](index.md#stoicism)

#### 6. Both Epictetus and Paul use the Stoic word for contentment. How do their reasons for being content differ?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Epictetus is content because he is wealthy, while Paul is content because he is poor
2. Both find their strength in the same source, the divine reason inside every person
3. For the Stoic, contentment comes from within, wanting only what is up to him; for Paul, from outside, "the one who strengthens him"
4. Epictetus rejected contentment as a goal, while Paul made it the center of his teaching
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **C**. Epictetus would say a wise person is content because he wants only what is up to him, his own judgments, so nothing outside can take his peace. Writing to the Philippians from prison, Paul uses the same Stoic word, *autarkēs*, but says he can do all things through the one who strengthens him. Same word, similar circumstances, opposite source of strength. That pattern, borrowed vocabulary carrying a new meaning, is exactly what Paul does on the Areopagus.

    **Concept Tested:** Paul and the Stoics

    **See:** [Paul and the Stoics](index.md#paul-and-the-stoics)

#### 7. On the Areopagus, a guide tells you that archaeologists found the very altar inscribed "To an unknown god" that Paul mentions. How should you weigh that claim?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Accept it, because Acts is the earliest source and names the altar exactly
2. Reject the whole story, because no Greek ever built an altar to an unnamed god
3. Accept it, because the bronze plaque on the hill marks the altar's original spot
4. No such altar has been found; independent writers attest altars to unknown gods in the plural, and only Acts has the singular
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **D**. No altar with exactly that wording has been found in Athens. Pausanias and Philostratus independently describe altars to unknown gods, plural, and Jerome thought Paul turned a plural into a singular to suit his argument. So altars to unknown gods are well attested, which rules out option B, but the singular form appears only in Acts. The bronze plaque carries the text of the speech in Greek; it does not mark an altar. Either way, Paul starts from something the listeners built.

    **Concept Tested:** Altar to an Unknown God

    **See:** [The Altar to an Unknown God](index.md#the-altar-to-an-unknown-god)

#### 8. A teacher hearing about a new AI tutor asks, "Will I be able to do this?" Using the stages of concern, which response fits best?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Offer a smaller first step that answers her worry about her own ability
2. Give her a long presentation on research showing the tutor's effect on test scores
3. Tell her the decision has been made and her concerns do not matter
4. Wait until she stops asking questions before introducing the tool
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **A**. The Concerns-Based Adoption Model, developed by Gene Hall, Shirley Hord, and colleagues, finds that concerns move from the self, to the task, to the impact on others. A leader helps most by answering the concern a person actually holds. *AI-Native Schools* pairs "Will I be able to do this?" with a smaller first step, a classroom visit with "What does it look like?", and coaching with "How do I fit it in?" Research on student impact (option B) answers a later concern she does not yet hold.

    **Concept Tested:** Stages of Concern

    **See:** [Stages of Concern](index.md#stages-of-concern)

#### 9. What does the ancient symposium show about the chapter's larger theme?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. That Athenian women led the city's philosophy, since they hosted the parties
2. That wine was always drunk unmixed, as a sign of Greek refinement
3. That brilliant conversation belonged to free citizen men, while women came as hired musicians and enslaved servants filled the cups
4. That every Athenian, rich or poor, attended a symposium each evening
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **C**. The symposium, "drinking together," followed dinner in a Greek house. Male guests reclined on couches, wine was mixed with water in a *krater*, since unmixed wine was thought barbaric (option B), and talk, songs, and games followed. Plato's *Symposium* has Socrates outtalk everyone. But the women present were hired musicians and companions, and enslaved servants filled the cups. It shows the chapter in miniature: brilliant conversation within a narrow circle. Tonight's open taverna table is a democratic improvement.

    **Concept Tested:** Symposium

    **See:** [The Symposium](index.md#the-symposium)

#### 10. A skilled craftsman in Athens earned about 1 drachma a day, and a mina was 100 drachmas. Evenus of Paros charged 5 minae to teach virtue. How many days of a craftsman's wages was that?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. About 5 days
2. About 50 days
3. About 100 days
4. About 500 days, well over a year of work
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **D**. Five minae at 100 drachmas each is 500 drachmas, and at about 1 drachma a day that is about 500 days of a craftsman's wages, well over a year of work. That figure comes from Plato's *Apology*. Option A forgets to convert minae to drachmas. A much later report says Protagoras charged 100 minae, more than 25 years of wages, though that may be exaggerated. Either way, a Sophist's course was out of reach for any working family, while Socrates charged nothing.

    **Concept Tested:** Who Pays for Learning

    **See:** [Who Pays for Learning](index.md#who-pays-for-learning)
