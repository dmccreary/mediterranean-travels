# Quiz: Day 10: Athens, the Acropolis, and the Persian Wars

Test your understanding of the Persian Wars, the buildings of the Acropolis and their later lives, Greek tragedy, and the Greek table with these review questions.

---

#### 1. Which description of Herodotus is supported by the chapter?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. An Athenian general who fought at Marathon and wrote a diary of the battle
2. A Roman historian who translated Persian records into Latin
3. A Spartan poet who sang the Persian Wars in the style of Homer
4. A writer born a Persian subject at Halicarnassus, whose *Histories* present his *historia*, "inquiry"
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **D**. Herodotus was born about 484 BCE at Halicarnassus, modern Bodrum, so he grew up a subject of the Persian king. His *Histories*, finished around 430 BCE, open by presenting his *historia*, Greek for "inquiry," the source of our word *history*. He names his sources, gives Persian, Phoenician, and Greek versions side by side, and reports what people say without believing all of it. Cicero called him the father of history. It was the playwright Aeschylus, not Herodotus, who fought at Marathon.

    **Concept Tested:** Herodotus

    **See:** [Herodotus](index.md#herodotus)

#### 2. Herodotus says Xerxes' land army had 1,700,000 infantry. How does the chapter suggest we treat that number?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. As reported rather than believed, since feeding it would take about 1,700 tonnes of grain a day, and modern estimates are far smaller
2. As exact, since Herodotus copied it from the Persian army's own records
3. As proof that his whole account of the war is invented and should be ignored
4. As too small, since modern historians think the Persians brought even more men
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **A**. At about 1 kg of grain per soldier per day, 1.7 million soldiers would eat about 1,700 tonnes a day, roughly 50,000 tonnes a month, all moved along one coast road. Working from what roads, water, and food could support, modern historians usually estimate about 100,000 to 300,000 fighting men. So we keep Herodotus' account of what happened and where, and treat his numbers the way he treated his own sources: reported, not believed. Doubting one figure does not mean discarding the whole history (option C).

    **Concept Tested:** Herodotus

    **See:** [Herodotus](index.md#herodotus)

#### 3. A tour guide at Marathon says a runner carried news of the victory to Athens, cried "We have won!", and died. Applying the myth test to the sources, what should you conclude?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. It is firm history, because Herodotus describes the run in detail
2. The death-run is a legend first written 550 to 650 years later; the real feat was Pheidippides' run of about 240 km to Sparta before the battle
3. It is a modern invention made up for the first Olympic marathon in 1896
4. It is firm history, because the length of the modern marathon matches the road from Marathon to Athens
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **B**. Herodotus, writing about 60 years after the battle, tells of a runner named Pheidippides who ran from Athens to Sparta before the battle to ask for help, about 240 km in two days. He never mentions a death-run to Athens. That story first appears in Plutarch and Lucian, 550 to 650 years after the battle. So the real feat is the one fewer people know. The race distance of 42.195 km is a modern convention set for London in 1908, not evidence about 490 BCE.

    **Concept Tested:** Battle of Marathon

    **See:** [The Battle of Marathon](index.md#the-battle-of-marathon)

#### 4. What lesson does the chapter draw from Herodotus for students who use AI systems?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Trust an AI answer if it is written in smooth, confident prose
2. Avoid AI entirely, because it cannot write history as well as Herodotus did
3. Ask of any answer "Who says so? How do they know? What do other sources say?", the habit of *historia*
4. Prefer poets and royal inscriptions, since they record the past most faithfully
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **C**. Before Herodotus, the past was told by poets singing what the Muses gave them and by kings carving what they wished remembered, neither inviting questions. Herodotus wrote in prose and showed his work: who told him what, what he saw, and where sources disagreed. An AI system usually answers in one smooth, confident paragraph without separating sourced facts, passed-on reports, and its own conclusions. A student who asks "Who says so?" is practicing *historia*, which rules out option A.

    **Concept Tested:** Herodotus

    **See:** [Learning for All: History Anyone Can Check](index.md#learning-for-all-history-anyone-can-check)

#### 5. Pericles said Phidias' statue of Athena Parthenos carried 40 talents of refined gold, all removable. If an Attic talent weighed about 26 kg, roughly how much gold was on the statue?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. About 1,040 kg, roughly a tonne
2. About 66 kg, the weight of an adult
3. About 400 kg, less than half a tonne
4. About 10,400 kg, more than ten tonnes
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **A**. Forty talents at about 26 kg each gives 40 × 26 = 1,040 kg, roughly a tonne of gold. Thucydides reports that Pericles counted it among the city's emergency reserves, to be borrowed and replaced. The removable design also protected Phidias: Plutarch says that when enemies accused him of stealing gold, the plates were taken off and weighed, and the charge failed. Option B adds the numbers instead of multiplying them, and option D is ten times too large.

    **Concept Tested:** Phidias

    **See:** [Phidias](index.md#phidias)

#### 6. What is the most surprising feature of the Parthenon's geometry?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Every block is identical, so damaged pieces can be swapped easily
2. It was laid out exactly on the golden ratio in every dimension
3. It has no columns on its long sides, only solid walls
4. Almost nothing in it is straight: the platform rises toward the middle, and the columns lean inward and swell slightly
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **D**. The Parthenon's platform rises gently toward the middle of each side, about 6 cm on the ends and 11 cm on the long sides, so it does not look as if it sags. Its columns lean slightly inward and swell a little in the middle, a curve called *entasis*, and the corner columns are a bit thicker. Because every block was cut for its own place, no two are quite alike, which rules out option A. The chapter says it was built on a 4-to-9 proportion, not the golden ratio.

    **Concept Tested:** Parthenon

    **See:** [The Parthenon](index.md#the-parthenon)

#### 7. You photograph the six Caryatids holding up the Erechtheion's south porch. What are you actually looking at?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. The original statues, cleaned with lasers and returned to the porch in 2009
2. Statues of women from Caryae, carved as a punishment, as the building's accounts record
3. Copies put in place in 1979; five originals are in the Acropolis Museum and the sixth is in the British Museum
4. Roman replacements made after the originals were destroyed in 1687
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **C**. The Caryatids on the building are copies, put in place in 1979 to save the originals from air pollution. Five originals stand in the Acropolis Museum, cleaned with lasers, and the sixth is in London, taken by Lord Elgin's agents. Option B fails the myth test: the punishment story comes from Vitruvius, while the Erechtheion's own building accounts simply call the statues *korai*, "maidens," and the name may come from Caryae's dances for Artemis.

    **Concept Tested:** Caryatids

    **See:** [The Caryatids](index.md#the-caryatids)

#### 8. Why do Greek and Turkish kitchens share so many dishes, often under nearly the same names?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Both countries adopted the same French cookbooks in the 20th century
2. The two peoples lived in one empire for centuries, and the 1923 exchange carried Anatolian cooking to Greece
3. Turkish cooks copied Greek recipes when Greece ruled Istanbul in the 1800s
4. The dishes all come from ancient Athens and spread east with Alexander
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **B**. Greeks and Turks lived in one empire for centuries, and the population exchange of 1923 sent refugees west with their cooking: *soutzoukakia smyrneika*, meatballs with cumin, are named for Smyrna. Who invented each dish is argued with feeling in both countries, a gentle case of contested history. Moussaka has an Arabic-derived name and versions across the Ottoman world, and coffee reached Istanbul from Yemen. The chapter's answer is that each dish belongs to everyone who cooked it. Greece never ruled Istanbul (option C).

    **Concept Tested:** Shared Greek-Turkish Dishes

    **See:** [Shared Greek-Turkish Dishes](index.md#shared-greek-turkish-dishes)

#### 9. In the Parthenon Marbles dispute, which statement belongs to the agreed facts rather than to the disputed meaning?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. The sculptures belong in London because more visitors can see them there
2. The sculptures belong in Athens because they are part of one building
3. Elgin's permission was clearly illegal under Ottoman law
4. Between 1801 and 1812 Elgin's agents removed about half the surviving sculpture, and the original permission document has never been found
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **D**. The dispute is contested history: the basic facts are largely agreed, and the argument is over what they mean and where the sculptures should be. Agreed facts include that Elgin's agents removed about half the surviving sculpture between 1801 and 1812, while Athens was Ottoman, and that the original permission document has never been found, though an Italian translation survives. Options A and B are the two sides' positions, not facts. Option C states a conclusion the agreed facts do not settle.

    **Concept Tested:** Parthenon Marbles Dispute

    **See:** [The Parthenon Marbles Dispute](index.md#the-parthenon-marbles-dispute)

#### 10. What is the difference between hubris and hamartia in Greek tragedy?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Hubris is arrogant overreaching that invites ruin; hamartia is an error or mistake, often made in ignorance
2. Hubris is a mistake made in ignorance; hamartia is arrogant pride against the gods
3. Both words mean the same "tragic flaw" that every hero has
4. Hubris is a type of chorus song; hamartia is the final scene of a tragedy
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **A**. Hubris is arrogant overreaching, pride that exceeds human limits or treats the gods' order with contempt; in Athenian law it was also a crime of humiliating assault. Herodotus' Xerxes, who had the Hellespont lashed, fits the pattern. Aristotle's word for what brings a hero down is *hamartia*, an error or mistake, often in ignorance, as when Oedipus does not know his parents. The chapter warns against treating hubris as every hero's "tragic flaw" (option C), and option B swaps the two.

    **Concept Tested:** Hubris

    **See:** [Hubris](index.md#hubris)
