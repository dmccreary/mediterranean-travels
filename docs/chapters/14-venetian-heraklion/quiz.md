# Quiz: Day 9, Part 2: Venetian Heraklion and Crete Today

Test your understanding of Venetian Candia, the Cretan Renaissance and the Aldine press, modern Crete, and the Cretan table with these review questions.

---

#### 1. Where does the name "Candia," which the Venetians used for Heraklion and for all of Crete, ultimately come from?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. From the Arabic *Rabdh al-Khandaq*, "the castle of the moat," the fortified town the Arabs built in the 820s
2. From the Latin word for "white," after the island's limestone cliffs
3. From the name of the Minoan palace's harbor recorded on Linear B tablets
4. From the Turkish *Kandiye*, given by the Ottomans after 1669
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **A**. The Arabs who took Crete in the 820s built a fortified town protected by a great ditch and called it *Rabdh al-Khandaq*, "the castle of the moat." After the Byzantine reconquest of 961 the Greeks called it Chandax, the Venetians turned that into Candia, and the Ottomans into Kandiye, so option D reverses the order. In the 19th century Greeks revived the ancient name Herakleion, after a harbor of Knossos. Each ruler renamed the place, and the Arab moat set the line later walls followed.

    **Concept Tested:** Candia

    **See:** [Candia](index.md#candia)

#### 2. Inside the Church of Agios Titos, you notice a broad, open, domed hall, filled with icons. Which history explains what you see?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. It was always an Orthodox church, built in this shape to copy Hagia Sophia
2. It was built by the Venetians as a Catholic cathedral and never changed hands
3. It was a church, then a mosque rebuilt after an 1856 earthquake, and became an Orthodox church again in 1925
4. It was built as a mosque in 1925 and later given to the Orthodox Church
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **C**. Agios Titos records every ruler in the chapter. It was first built after the Byzantine reconquest of 961, became a Catholic church under Venice and a mosque under the Ottomans, and was rebuilt as a mosque after an earthquake destroyed it in 1856. It served Heraklion's Muslims until they left in the 1923 population exchange, and in 1925 it was consecrated as an Orthodox church again. That is why its domed hall keeps the broad, open shape of a mosque, now filled with icons.

    **Concept Tested:** Titus in Crete

    **See:** [Titus in Crete](index.md#titus-in-crete)

#### 3. Why are the bastions of Heraklion's Venetian walls shaped like arrowheads rather than round towers?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Arrowhead shapes used less stone, which was scarce on Crete
2. The shape copied the Minoan double axe, the ancient symbol of Crete
3. Pointed bastions could ram enemy ships that came too close to the shore
4. Guns on each bastion's flanks fire along the faces of its neighbors, so no dead ground is left for attackers
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **D**. Defenders on a round tower cannot see the foot of their own tower or much of the wall between towers; that hidden "dead ground" is where attackers place ladders or dig mines. An arrowhead bastion's two short flanks carry guns that fire sideways along the faces of the neighboring bastions and the wall between, so each bastion covers the next and every meter of the ditch is under fire. Draw the lines of fire, and the arrowhead is the shape left when no dead ground remains.

    **Concept Tested:** Venetian Walls of Heraklion

    **See:** [The Venetian Walls of Heraklion](index.md#the-venetian-walls-of-heraklion)

#### 4. How did the twenty-year Siege of Candia end in 1669?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. The city was stormed and sacked, and every defender was killed
2. Francesco Morosini negotiated an honorable surrender: the garrison and inhabitants could leave with their property
3. A French army broke the siege, and Venice kept Crete until 1913
4. The Ottomans gave up and sailed home after a plague struck their camp
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **B**. The Ottomans dug in before Candia in 1648, and the city held for more than twenty years, supplied by sea, in a war of mines and countermines. After the grand vizier Köprülü Fazıl Ahmed Pasha took command and a French expedition failed, Morosini negotiated a surrender in September 1669. The terms were generous: the garrison and any inhabitants who wished could leave with their property, and Venice kept three small island fortresses. Ottoman Crete followed until 1898.

    **Concept Tested:** Siege of Candia

    **See:** [The Siege of Candia](index.md#the-siege-of-candia)

#### 5. At the lion fountain in Lions Square, a fellow traveler says, "The Morosini who built this later blew up the Parthenon." How should you correct them?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. The fountain was built by the Ottomans, and Morosini only repaired it
2. Nobody named Morosini was ever involved with the Parthenon
3. The fountain's builder was the doge of Venice, who never visited Crete
4. The fountain was built in 1628 by the governor Francesco Morosini, a different man from the namesake who surrendered Candia and led the army that hit the Parthenon in 1687
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **D**. The Morosini Fountain was built in 1628 by the Venetian governor Francesco Morosini at the end of an aqueduct bringing water from Mount Juktas. He is easy to confuse with his namesake, Francesco Morosini (1619–1694), who commanded Candia late in the siege, surrendered it in 1669, and in 1687 led the Venetian army whose shell blew up the Parthenon. So option B is wrong too: a Morosini did hit the Parthenon, just not this one. The square is officially Venizelou Square, but everyone calls it "the Lions."

    **Concept Tested:** Morosini Fountain

    **See:** [The Morosini Fountain](index.md#the-morosini-fountain)

#### 6. Which statement about El Greco is supported by the chapter?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Born in Candia in 1541, he trained as an icon painter in the Cretan tradition before working in Venice, Rome, and Toledo
2. He was a Spanish painter who moved to Crete late in life and painted its monasteries
3. His most famous paintings hang in Heraklion, where he worked all his life
4. He was a Venetian governor of Crete who painted as a hobby
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **A**. El Greco, "the Greek," was Domenikos Theotokopoulos, born in Candia in 1541. He trained as an icon painter in the Cretan tradition, was a master painter there by 1563, moved to Venice about 1567, to Rome in 1570, and settled in Toledo in 1577. He signed in Greek letters, sometimes adding *Kres*, "Cretan." His famous canvases, such as *The Burial of the Count of Orgaz*, are in Spain; Heraklion's Historical Museum holds two small paintings, which rules out option C.

    **Concept Tested:** El Greco

    **See:** [El Greco](index.md#el-greco)

#### 7. Virgil's works run to about 12,900 lines. An Aldine-style octavo fits 30 lines on each page and 16 pages on each sheet. About how many sheets of paper does each copy need?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. About 215 sheets
2. About 27 sheets
3. About 54 sheets
4. About 430 sheets
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **B**. At 30 lines a page, 12,900 ÷ 30 = 430 pages, and at 16 pages to a sheet, 430 ÷ 16 is about 26.9, so 27 sheets. Option D confuses pages with sheets. Option A is the chapter's folio edition with commentary: 15 lines a page gives 860 pages, and at 4 pages a sheet, 215 sheets. So the octavo carries every line of Virgil on about one-eighth of the paper, and since handmade paper was one of the largest costs, the price fell with it.

    **Concept Tested:** Aldine Editions

    **See:** [Aldine Editions](index.md#aldine-editions)

#### 8. Why does the chapter call the Battle of Crete in May 1941 a turning point in the history of warfare?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. It was the last battle in which cavalry charged against tanks
2. It was won by Cretan villagers alone, without any Allied troops
3. It was the first large invasion carried out mainly from the air, and German paratrooper losses were so heavy that Hitler never ordered another
4. It was the first battle in which submarines landed an invading army
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **C**. On May 20, 1941, German paratroopers and glider troops landed around the north-coast airfields and towns. They took terrible losses, but once they held the airfield at Maleme, reinforcements flew in, and within about ten days the Allies were evacuating to Egypt. German losses among their elite paratroopers were so heavy that Hitler never again ordered a large airborne operation. British, Australian, New Zealand, and Greek troops fought alongside Cretan civilians, so option B is wrong.

    **Concept Tested:** Battle of Crete

    **See:** [The Battle of Crete](index.md#the-battle-of-crete)

#### 9. Which two forces does the chapter say shaped the traditional Cretan diet?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Poverty and the land, and the Orthodox calendar of fasting from meat, dairy, and eggs
2. Venetian trade in spices, and Ottoman palace cooking
3. Modern nutrition research, and the tourist demand for healthy food
4. The Minoan palace economy, and the rations of the Battle of Crete
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **A**. Farm families ate what their own fields, trees, flocks, and hillsides gave them, with meat kept for feast days. The Orthodox fasting calendar asks the faithful to give up meat, dairy, and eggs on most Wednesdays and Fridays and through long seasons; the Nativity fast begins on November 15, so late-November menus mark *nistisima*, fasting dishes. A cuisine that must feed people well without animal products becomes very good at vegetables, pulses, and olive oil. Researchers recorded the diet in the mid-20th century; they did not create it (option C).

    **Concept Tested:** Cretan Diet

    **See:** [The Cretan Diet](index.md#the-cretan-diet)

#### 10. Aldus's pocket classics were cheap, but his privileges made the printers of Lyon who copied them into pirates. What does this contrast show about open educational resources?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Cheap books and open resources are the same thing, so Aldus already made his books open
2. Open licenses are dangerous because they let anyone spread errors, so they should be avoided
3. Low prices alone guarantee that everyone can learn, so licenses do not matter
4. An open license turns copiers into partners who spread and fix a text, and because copies then cost nothing, careful editing matters more
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **D**. Affordable course materials lower the price; open educational resources also carry a license that lets anyone copy, adapt, translate, and share. Aldus's books were cheap but not open, so under an open license the Lyon printers would have been partners, spreading the text and fixing its errors. When copies cost nothing, an editor's mistakes multiply as freely as the truth, as Musurus's did in 1513. The chapter's answer is more careful editing, not avoiding openness (option B).

    **Concept Tested:** Open Educational Resources

    **See:** [Learning for All: Cretan Scholars and Pocket Classics](index.md#learning-for-all-cretan-scholars-and-pocket-classics)
