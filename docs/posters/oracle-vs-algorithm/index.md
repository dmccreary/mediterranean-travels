## About This MicroSim

For about eleven centuries, people climbed to Delphi to ask Apollo's priestess
what their choices would bring. Today we ask machine-learning models the same
kind of question. Chapters [17](../../chapters/17-oracle/index.md) and
[19](../../chapters/19-algorithm/index.md) walk through the parallel in
detail. This poster puts it in one picture: six tiles, each with Delphi on the
left and today on the right, following the life of a single question.

Each tile also says where the parallel breaks. For the people who climbed to
Delphi, consulting the god was an act of piety, and calling it "an early
algorithm" misses what it meant to them. The six comparisons are lenses for
asking better questions of any source, not a claim that the two are the same
thing.

## How to Use

**Explore mode** (the default):

1. **Hover** over a tile to see a short tooltip with its name and its idea.
2. **Click** a tile to open an infobox below the image with what happened at
   Delphi, what happens today, and where the parallel breaks. On a tablet or
   phone, tap a tile instead.

**Quiz mode:** press **Quiz Mode** at the bottom. The name pills disappear, and
a question appears below the image. Click the tile that answers it. A correct
answer shows a short explanation; a wrong one shakes the tile so you can try
again. The score counts your correct answers, and **Try Again** starts a new
round with the questions in a new order.

**Edit mode:** add `?edit=true` to the address of `main.html` to drag the
corners of each tile's zone, then press **Copy JSON** and paste the zones into
`data.json`.

## The Six Tiles

1. **Who asks**: a petitioner who climbed, paid a fee, and sacrificed an
   animal, and a user typing a prompt, while others are scored without asking.
2. **Where knowledge comes from**: envoys bringing news from many cities, and
   training data from many voices. Both know the most about those who can reach
   them.
3. **The form of the answer**: a verse that came true whichever side won, and a
   fluent, hedged paragraph. An answer that cannot lose predicts nothing.
4. **Who interprets**: the Athenian Assembly arguing over the wooden wall, and a
   human in the loop who is, or is not, free to disagree.
5. **How we mishear**: Croesus hearing the victory he wanted, and a leading
   question that gets an agreeable answer.
6. **When the answer changes the future**: Oedipus at the crossroads, and a
   prediction that feeds, or defeats, itself.

## Lesson Plan

### Learning Objective

The learner will compare how the Delphic oracle and a modern predictive model
answer questions across six matched stages, and name, for each stage, one way
the two are alike and one way the parallel breaks.

### Grade Level

Adult travelers on the trip (Mediterranean Travels).

### Duration

10–15 minutes

### Prerequisites

How the oracle worked, from [Chapter 17](../../chapters/17-oracle/index.md),
and machine learning, training data, and language models, from
[Chapter 19](../../chapters/19-algorithm/index.md).

### Activities

1. **Exploration** (5 min): Hover over all six tiles, then click each one and
   read its infobox. For each tile, find the line that says where the parallel
   breaks.
2. **Guided Practice** (5 min): Compare tiles 3 and 5. Both involve an answer we
   misread. What is the difference between an answer that cannot lose and a
   question that gets the answer we wanted?
3. **Assessment** (5 min): Switch to Quiz mode and answer all nine questions.

### Assessment

The learner answers at least 8 of the 9 quiz questions correctly on the first
try and can state, in one sentence, why the Athenian Assembly was a meaningful
human in the loop.
