## About This MicroSim

In a heated dispute it is tempting to think the facts themselves must be in
doubt. The Parthenon Marbles dispute shows otherwise: Greece and the British
Museum accept almost all the same facts and disagree about what those facts
mean and where the sculptures should be. This MicroSim asks us to pull the
argument apart into its pieces.

Twelve statement cards come one at a time. We sort each into one of three
bins:

| Bin | Definition |
|---|---|
| Agreed fact | Something both sides accept as true. |
| British Museum's position | What the British Museum argues should be concluded or done. |
| Greek government's position | What the Greek government argues should be concluded or done. |

After each answer the card joins its correct bin as a short label. At the end
the three bins stand side by side: six agreed facts, three statements of the
Museum's position, and three of the Greek government's.

Two cards are traps, and mastery requires both: **the lost Ottoman permission
document** (card 6) and **the British Museum Act of 1963** (card 10). Each is a
fact that one side cites more often, but both sides accept it as true. A fact
that helps one side is still a fact.

**Fairness.** The sim takes neither side. Each position is paraphrased from
the side's own published statements (see References), and each side gets the
same number of cards. The positions are summarized as of late 2026; talks
between Greek officials and the British Museum have been reported since about
2022, so a position card may date if an agreement is announced.

**Wording change.** The chapter's specification phrased card 8 as *In London
the sculptures are seen free of charge beside the art of cultures from around
the world.* Read literally, that sentence is a fact both sides would accept
(entry to the Museum is free), which would make the card unfair. The sim words
it as the argument the Museum makes: *The sculptures are best seen in London,
free of charge, beside the art of cultures from around the world.*

## How to Use

1. Read the three bin definitions at the top.
2. Read the card in the panel and choose a bin with the buttons below (or press
   1, 2 or 3). Only the first choice counts.
3. Read the feedback. The correct bin is outlined in green; a wrong choice is
   outlined in red. The card joins its correct bin, with a red dashed border if
   you missed it.
4. Press **Next card** (or Enter). After card 12, press **See the bins**.
5. On the result screen, click any label in a bin to read its statement and
   reason again.
6. **Try Again** clears the bins and reshuffles the twelve cards.

## Lesson Plan

**Audience:** adult travelers preparing to visit the Acropolis Museum.  
**Duration:** 10–15 minutes.  
**Prerequisites:** contested history (Chapter 4), the Parthenon sculptures,
Lord Elgin, the Acropolis Museum, and the Parthenon Marbles dispute (Chapter
15).  
**Learning objective (Bloom: Analyze):** Differentiate agreed facts from the
British Museum's position and the Greek government's position in twelve
statements about the Parthenon Marbles dispute, sorting at least 10 of 12
correctly on the first attempt.

### Activities

1. **Test each card (8 min):** Before choosing, ask: *Would the other side
   deny this?* If not, it is an agreed fact, even if one side quotes it more.
   If the sentence says what *should* happen or what the facts *prove*, it is a
   position.
2. **Read the bins (3 min):** On the result screen, pair each position card
   with the facts it rests on. Which facts does the Museum use? Which does
   Greece use? Are any facts used by both?
3. **Discuss (4 min):** Why will more evidence not settle this dispute? What
   kind of agreement could?

### Assessment

Mastery is 10 of 12 correct on the first attempt, including cards 6 and 10. As
a follow-up, ask: *Is the British Museum Act of 1963 part of the Museum's
position?* A strong answer says no: the law's existence is an agreed fact; the
Museum's position is the conclusion it draws from it, and Greece accepts that
the law exists while arguing about what should be done.
