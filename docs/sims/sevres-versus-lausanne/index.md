# Sèvres or Lausanne?

## About This MicroSim

Two treaties ended the Ottoman Empire's war with the Allies. The sultan's
government signed the **Treaty of Sèvres** on August 10, 1920, but the Grand
National Assembly in Ankara rejected it and it never took effect. After the
nationalists' victories of 1921 and 1922, the Allies negotiated again, and the
**Treaty of Lausanne**, signed on July 24, 1923, recognized an independent
Turkey with roughly its present borders.

It is tempting to say that Lausanne simply cancelled Sèvres. It did not. This
MicroSim shows ten terms of Sèvres with the Lausanne column hidden. For each
term we predict **Reversed** (Lausanne undid the Sèvres term) or **Kept**
(Lausanne left it in place, in substance), and only then see what Lausanne
said. Six terms were reversed and four kept.

After the ten, we choose which of three explanations accounts for the pattern.
The answer is the **National Pact** (*Misak-ı Millî*) of January 1920, which
claimed the lands with an Ottoman Muslim majority still held by Ottoman forces
at the armistice of October 1918, demanded the security of Istanbul and the
Sea of Marmara, and left the Arab provinces to their inhabitants. Every term
Lausanne reversed lay inside that claim, and every term it kept lay outside
it.

The sim names some limits of that rule. The Straits stayed under an
international commission until the Montreux Convention of 1936. The border
with Iraq around Mosul, which Ankara did claim, was left open at Lausanne and
settled in Iraq's favor in 1926. And the Pact's claim to eastern Anatolia as
Muslim-majority land rested on a majority that existed only after most of the
region's Armenians had been deported or killed in 1915–1916, as the chapter's
World War I section describes. For Armenians and Kurds, Lausanne is remembered
as the treaty that dropped the states Sèvres had promised; for Turks, it is
the founding charter of an independent republic. The sim asks us to explain
the treaty, not to judge it, but both memories are part of its history.

## How to Use

1. Read the prompt and the Sèvres column. The Lausanne column is hidden, and
   the current term's row is highlighted in yellow.
2. Press **Reversed** or **Kept** (or press R or K). Each term has one
   prediction.
3. The Lausanne term appears in the table with a badge, a green check if we
   predicted it and a red cross if we did not. The feedback below explains
   why.
4. Press **Next term** (or Enter). After term 10, press **See the tally**.
5. Choose explanation **A**, **B**, or **C** (or press A, B or C). The
   feedback tells us whether it fits, and why not if it does not.
6. **Try Again** hides the Lausanne column and starts over.

## Lesson Plan

**Audience:** adult travelers preparing for the trip.  
**Duration:** 15 minutes.  
**Prerequisites:** the Treaty of Sèvres, the Treaty of Lausanne, the National
Pact, the Grand National Assembly, the Greco-Turkish War, the Dardanelles
Strait, and the capitulations.  
**Learning objective (Bloom: Analyze):** Differentiate the terms of the 1920
Treaty of Sèvres that the 1923 Treaty of Lausanne reversed from those it
kept, predicting at least 8 of 10 correctly and explaining the pattern by the
National Pact.

### Activities

1. **State the rule first (2 min):** Before term 1, write down in one sentence
   what the National Pact claimed. Use it to make every prediction.
2. **Predict (8 min):** Work through the ten terms. For each, say aloud
   whether the land or right lay inside the Pact's claim before choosing.
3. **Explain the pattern (3 min):** Look down the finished Lausanne column and
   choose an explanation. If you chose B or C, find the term that breaks it.
4. **Two memories (2 min):** Pick one reversed term, Smyrna or Armenia, and say
   how a Greek, an Armenian and a Turkish family might each remember what
   Lausanne decided.

### Assessment

Mastery is 8 of 10 correct predictions and explanation A. As a follow-up,
ask: *We sail through the Dardanelles on this trip. Did Lausanne give Turkey
full control of the Straits?* A strong answer says no: Lausanne kept an
international commission and demilitarized zones, and full Turkish control
came only with the Montreux Convention of 1936.
