## About This MicroSim

The most famous answers attributed to the Oracle of Delphi seem astonishing
because they "came true." This MicroSim asks a different question about each
answer: **could anything that happens prove it wrong?** The test is simple.
List two possible outcomes and ask whether the answer comes true under each.

| Category | Test |
|---|---|
| Could be proven wrong | At least one possible outcome would show the answer to be false. |
| Comes true either way | Every possible outcome can be read as fulfilling it. |
| Not a prediction | It tells someone what to do and says nothing about what will happen. |

The eight cards come in a fixed order:

| # | Answer | Source | Category |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | "If you make war on the Persians, you will destroy a great empire." | Herodotus 1.53, to Croesus, about 550 BCE | Comes true either way |
| 2 | "Divine Salamis, you will destroy the children of women." | Herodotus 7.141, to the Athenians, 481 or 480 BCE | Comes true either way |
| 3 | "I say that you, son of Aeacus, the Romans can defeat." | Cicero, *On Divination* 2.116, quoting a line said to be given to Pyrrhus | Comes true either way |
| 4 | "No one is wiser than Socrates." | Plato, *Apology* 21a, to Chaerephon | Could be proven wrong |
| 5 | "Found a city in Libya." | Herodotus 4.150, to the king of Thera, about 630 BCE | Not a prediction |
| 6 | The gods to whom Xenophon should sacrifice for a safe journey | Xenophon, *Anabasis* 3.1, about 401 BCE | Not a prediction |
| 7 | "Your son will kill you." | Sophocles, *Oedipus the King*, to Laius | Could be proven wrong |
| 8 | "It will rain at Delphi between noon and 3 PM tomorrow." | A modern weather forecast | Could be proven wrong |

The first three are the answers that "came true" most famously, and they are
exactly the three that could not lose. The Pyrrhus line is the purest example:
in Latin, *Aio te, Aeacida, Romanos vincere posse* can mean either "you can
defeat the Romans" or "the Romans can defeat you." Cicero himself mocked the
story, pointing out that Apollo never spoke Latin and that the line comes from
the poet Ennius. Cards 5 and 6 are a reminder that many real answers were not
predictions at all but commands and approvals, which is what the answers
recorded closest to their own time mostly look like.

**What is uncertain.** The quotations are the chapter's paraphrased
translations. The famous hedged answers survive in stories written down long
after the events, and some historians suspect that answers such as the second
"wooden wall" response were polished afterward. Card 7 is from a tragedy, not
a historical consultation. The categories describe each answer's *structure*,
not whether the oracle actually gave it.

## How to Use

1. Read the card and its source, and ask: could anything that happens prove
   this answer wrong?
2. Choose one of the three category buttons (or press keys 1 to 3). Only the
   first choice counts.
3. Press **Test both outcomes** (or T or Enter). Two outcome boxes show
   whether the answer comes true, is proven wrong, or does not apply, followed
   by the reason.
4. Press **Next card** (or Enter). After card 8, press **See the totals**.
5. On the totals screen, click any card on the strip to read it again.
   **Try Again** starts over with card 1.

## Lesson Plan

**Audience:** adult travelers preparing to visit Delphi.  
**Duration:** 10–15 minutes.  
**Prerequisites:** prediction, forecast, command, hedging, the Croesus
prophecy, the wooden walls prophecy, Chaerephon's question, the Oedipus
prophecy, and Xenophon's question (Chapter 17, Parts One, Three, Four and
Five).  
**Learning objective (Bloom: Analyze):** Distinguish answers that could be
proven wrong from hedged answers that come true whatever happens and from
answers that are not predictions at all.

### Activities

1. **Warm-up (2 min):** Before card 1, write down what Croesus probably took
   the answer to mean, without thinking about how the story ends.
2. **Classify and test (8 min):** For each card, commit to a category, then
   use the two-outcome test to check it.
3. **Compare (3 min):** On the totals screen, compare cards 1 to 3 with card
   8. Why is the weather forecast the bravest statement in the set?
4. **Transfer (2 min):** Think of a horoscope, a market forecast, or a
   political promise you have heard recently. Which category does it fall in?

### Assessment

Mastery is at least 6 of 8 correct on the first attempt, with cards 1
(Croesus) and 4 (Chaerephon) both correct. As a follow-up, ask: *Why does an
answer that cannot lose predict nothing?* A strong answer says that if every
outcome fulfills it, hearing the answer tells us nothing about which outcome
to expect, and it protects the reputation of whoever gave it.

## References

1. Herodotus, *Histories* 1.53, 4.150 and 7.140–143 ([Perseus Digital Library](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus:text:1999.01.0126)). The answer to Croesus, the command to found a city in Libya, and the two answers to Athens before Salamis.
2. Cicero, *On Divination* 2.115–117 ([LacusCurtius](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Cicero/de_Divinatione/2*.html)). The Pyrrhus line from Ennius, and Cicero's remarks that Apollo never spoke Latin and that Delphi had long stopped answering in verse.
3. Plato, *Apology* 20e–23b. Chaerephon's question and Socrates' long attempt to prove the answer wrong.
4. Xenophon, *Anabasis* 3.1.5–8. Xenophon asks Apollo to which gods he should sacrifice, and Socrates scolds him for the question.
5. Sophocles, *Oedipus the King* 711–725. Jocasta's account of the oracle given to Laius.
6. [Croesus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Croesus) — Wikipedia. The Lydian king, his tests of the oracles, and the fall of Sardis about 546 BCE.
7. [Pythia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pythia) — Wikipedia. Famous ambiguous answers attributed to the oracle and the modern debate over how they were recorded.
8. Joseph Fontenrose, *The Delphic Oracle: Its Responses and Operations* (University of California Press, 1978). The catalogue of about 535 responses showing that answers recorded close to their time are mostly plain commands and approvals.
