## About This MicroSim

The **classical orders** are the three related systems of design that Greek
architects used for a column and the beam it carries: the **Doric**, the
**Ionic**, and the **Corinthian**. Each order fixes the shape of the base, the
shaft, and the capital, and of the three layers of the entablature above: the
architrave, the frieze, and the cornice.

This MicroSim is the practice partner of the
[Three Classical Orders poster](../../posters/column-types/index.md), which
Chapter 10 embeds a few paragraphs earlier. The poster reads one whole column
at a time. This sim reads **one part at a time across all three orders**, and
then asks us to use what we have read.

- **Explore parts** reuses the poster's drawing. Choose Base, Shaft, Capital,
  Architrave, Frieze, or Cornice, and the part is outlined on all three
  columns, with a one-sentence definition for each order underneath. It opens
  on the capital, with the question *What does the top of a column tell you?*
- **Quiz** gives twelve cards in a fixed order. The first six are isolated
  features, such as a fallen drum or a capital; the last six are real
  buildings on or near our route. Each card has one first attempt.

Three patterns should stand out by the end. The capital decides most cards.
The flute count separates Doric (about 20 flutes meeting in sharp edges) from
the other two (about 24 flutes separated by flat strips), but it cannot
separate Ionic from Corinthian, so card 6 accepts either answer. And the
buildings of Ionia, the coast around Ephesus, are Ionic.

The sim also targets four common mistakes: that any capital with scrolls is
Corinthian (cards 2 and 11), that flutes alone name the order (card 6), that
all Greek temples are Doric (card 9), and that a smooth, unfluted shaft
belongs to one particular order (card 12). The features described are those
of the Greek orders; the Romans later gave some Doric columns a base.

## How to Use

1. In **Explore parts**, choose a part with the buttons above the drawing, or
   click a part on any column. Read the three definitions underneath and
   compare them.
2. Press **Start the quiz** (or the **Quiz** tab).
3. Read the card and choose **Doric**, **Ionic**, or **Corinthian**, with the
   buttons or the keys D, I, and C. Feedback appears at once.
4. Each card joins the summary below, in the column of its correct order,
   marked ✓ or ✗; a wrong card also shows what we chose.
5. Press **Next card** (or Enter). After card 12 the score appears:
   *You classified n of 12. Mastery is 10.* Below 10, **Try again** restarts
   from card 1 in the same order.
6. We can return to **Explore parts** at any time. Answers already given do
   not change.

## Lesson Plan

**Audience:** adult travelers preparing to walk through Ephesus and, later,
the Acropolis of Athens.  
**Duration:** 10–15 minutes.  
**Prerequisites:** classical orders, base, shaft, flutes, capital, volute,
acanthus, entablature, architrave, frieze, triglyph, metope, and the Doric,
Ionic, and Corinthian orders (Chapter 10, Part Three).  
**Learning objective (Bloom: Understand):** Classify twelve described column
features and buildings from our route as Doric, Ionic, or Corinthian, with at
least 10 of 12 correct on the first attempt.

### Activities

1. **Explore (3 min):** Step through the six parts. For each one, say aloud
   whether it separates all three orders, only Doric from the others, or none
   of them. (The capital separates all three; the base, the shaft, and the
   frieze separate Doric from the other two.)
2. **Classify (7 min):** Work through the twelve cards. Before each answer,
   name the part of the column that the card describes.
3. **Read the summary (3 min):** Which cards needed the capital? Which could
   be settled by the base or the flutes? Why does card 6 have two right
   answers?
4. **Transfer (2 min):** Picture the front of a courthouse, bank, or state
   capitol near home. Which order are its capitals?

### Assessment

Mastery is 10 of 12 correct on the first attempt. As a follow-up on site,
ask: *On Curetes Street a column has lost its capital, but its shaft has 24
flutes with flat strips between them, and it stands on a molded base. What
can we say, and what can we not say?* A strong answer says it is not Doric,
because of the base and the flute pattern, but it could be Ionic or
Corinthian until the capital is found.

## References

1. Vitruvius, *The Ten Books on Architecture*, Book III (Ionic) and Book IV
   (Doric and Corinthian), trans. Morris Hicky Morgan (Harvard University
   Press, 1914), available at
   [Project Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/20239). The only
   architectural treatise to survive from antiquity, including the Callimachus
   story of the Corinthian capital.
2. [Classical order](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classical_order) —
   Wikipedia. The parts of a column and entablature, and the five orders of
   the Renaissance.
3. [Doric order](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doric_order),
   [Ionic order](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ionic_order), and
   [Corinthian order](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corinthian_order) —
   Wikipedia. Bases, flute counts (about 20 for Doric, 24 for Ionic and
   Corinthian), capitals, and friezes.
4. [Parthenon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parthenon) and
   [Temple of Athena Nike](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Temple_of_Athena_Nike) —
   Wikipedia. The Doric Parthenon (447–432 BCE) and the small Ionic temple
   beside the Acropolis entrance.
5. [Temple of Artemis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Temple_of_Artemis) and
   [Temple of Olympian Zeus, Athens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Temple_of_Olympian_Zeus,_Athens) —
   Wikipedia. The giant Ionic temple of Ephesus, and the Corinthian temple
   completed under Hadrian.
6. Peter Scherrer, ed., *Ephesus: The New Guide*, rev. ed. (Ege Yayınları,
   2000). The State Agora and its Basilica Stoa, whose Ionic capitals carry
   bulls' heads.
7. John Summerson, *The Classical Language of Architecture* (MIT Press,
   1963). A short, readable account of how the orders work as a grammar, from
   Greece to modern buildings.
