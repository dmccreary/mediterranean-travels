## About This MicroSim

In Book 20 of Homer's *Iliad*, as the fighting before Troy reaches its peak,
Zeus lifts his ban and lets the gods go down to the battle while he watches
from Olympus. Homer then names who went to each side. This MicroSim turns that
passage into a sorting activity. Twelve god cards, shuffled, go into three
bins:

| Bin | Gods (revealed at the end) |
|---|---|
| Helped the Greeks | Hera, Athena, Poseidon, Hermes, Hephaestus |
| Helped the Trojans | Ares, Apollo, Artemis, Aphrodite |
| Above or outside the fight | Zeus (watching from above), Demeter and Dionysus (no part in the *Iliad*'s battles) |

Each card shows the god's name and domains; hovering over a card turns it
over to show the full domains and the symbol to look for on a vase or a
relief. The domain is a clue but not the answer. The real reasons are
stories: Hera and Athena lost the Judgment of Paris and Aphrodite won it;
Poseidon remembers the wages a Trojan king never paid him; Apollo's priest
was insulted by Agamemnon. Ares, the god of war himself, fights for the side
that loses.

Before sorting, the sim asks for a prediction of the three totals. Many
travelers expect all twelve gods to fight, or the war god to be on the
winning side. At the end the true totals (5, 4, 3) appear beside the
prediction, and the three cards whose sides follow the Judgment of Paris are
outlined in gold.

The *Iliad* lists two more gods on the Trojan side who are not Olympians:
Leto, the mother of Apollo and Artemis, and Xanthos, the river god. They are
mentioned in the feedback but are not cards.

## How to Use

1. Type your predicted number of gods for **Greeks**, **Trojans**, and
   **Outside** in the three boxes below the canvas (whole numbers that add up
   to 12) and press **Lock in prediction**. Sorting starts only after that.
2. Hover over a card to read its domains and symbol in the panel at the
   bottom.
3. Drag each card into a bin. You can also click a card and then click a bin,
   or press **1**, **2** or **3**. Cards can be moved between bins, or dragged
   back to the tray, until you check.
4. When all twelve are placed, press **Check**. Correct cards turn green and
   lock; misplaced cards turn red. After the first Check a red card shows only
   a hint; after the second it shows the story from the *Iliad*.
5. You have up to three Checks. Only the first one counts for the score
   shown in the top right. After the last Check, any card still misplaced
   moves to its correct bin, and the tray shows your prediction beside the
   true totals.
6. **Start over** reshuffles the cards and clears the prediction.

## Lesson Plan

**Audience:** adult travelers preparing for Day 6 at Troy.  
**Duration:** 10–15 minutes.  
**Prerequisites:** the Olympian gods, Mount Olympus, the *Iliad*, the
Judgment of Paris, and the Trojan War.  
**Learning objective (Bloom: Understand):** Classify each of the twelve
Olympian gods by the side they take in Homer's *Iliad* (helped the Greeks,
helped the Trojans, or stayed above or outside the fight), using each god's
domain and story, with at least 10 of 12 correct on the first Check.

### Activities

1. **Predict (2 min):** Before touching a card, commit to three totals. Ask:
   did every god fight? Which side would the god of war choose?
2. **Sort (5 min):** Place all twelve cards. For each one, name the story
   that decides it, not just the domain.
3. **Check and revise (3 min):** Use the hint, "Think about the Judgment of
   Paris and old debts," to move the red cards.
4. **Compare (3 min):** Read your prediction against the true totals. Which
   of your assumptions did the *Iliad* overturn?

### Assessment

Mastery is at least 10 of 12 correct on the first Check. A follow-up
question for discussion: *The gods in the Iliad are not impartial judges. What
does that tell us about how the Greeks imagined the causes of a war?* A good
answer notes that the gods choose sides for personal reasons (wounded pride,
unpaid debts, favorite heroes), so the war's outcome is not a verdict on who
was right.

Common misconceptions this activity targets:

- The gods acted as impartial judges of right and wrong.
- The god of war must have been on the winning side.
- Every Olympian fought at Troy.
- Zeus was simply a Trojan partisan. (He helps the Trojans for a time only to
  keep his promise to Thetis, and stays above the fight in Book 20.)
