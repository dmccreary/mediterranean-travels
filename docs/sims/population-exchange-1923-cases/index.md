## About This MicroSim

On January 30, 1923, Greece and Turkey signed the **Convention Concerning the
Exchange of Greek and Turkish Populations** at Lausanne. It made the exchange
compulsory: the Greek Orthodox Christians of Turkey were to move to Greece, and
the Muslims of Greece to Turkey, and none could return. About 1.2 million
Orthodox Christians, most of whom had already fled in 1922, and about 400,000
Muslims lost homes their families had lived in for generations. Both
communities still remember it as a loss, and many families on both sides of
the Aegean keep stories, recipes and keepsakes from the towns they left.

This MicroSim asks us to apply the convention the way its officials had to:
case by case, by its written rules. The five rules at the top are simplified
from the convention's first three articles and from Article 14 of the Treaty
of Lausanne:

1. Greek Orthodox Christians living in Turkey are exchanged to Greece.
2. Muslims living in Greece are exchanged to Turkey.
3. People of these two groups who had already left since 1912 count as
   exchanged, and may not return.
4. Exempt: the Greek Orthodox established in Istanbul before October 30, 1918;
   the Muslims of Western Thrace; the Greek Orthodox of Imbros and Tenedos.
5. The exchange applies only to Greece and Turkey.

The ten households are **illustrative composites**, not named families. They
are built so that the obvious guess, from language or nationality, is often
wrong. The convention's test was religion: a Turkish-speaking Orthodox family
from Cappadocia was sent to Greece, and a Greek-speaking Muslim family from
Crete was sent to Turkey. After each answer, the rule that decided the case is
highlighted, and the case joins a summary grouped by outcome.

The simplified rules leave out the convention's details on property,
nationality and the Mixed Commission that ran the exchange. In practice,
officials also had to decide who counted as "established" in Istanbul, and
those decisions were disputed for years.

## How to Use

1. Read the five rules at the top. They stay visible throughout.
2. Read the case, for example *A Greek Orthodox family whose grandparents
   settled in Istanbul in 1890.*
3. Press **Sent to Greece**, **Sent to Turkey**, or **Not exchanged** (or
   press 1, 2 or 3). Each case has one attempt.
4. Read the feedback. The deciding rule is highlighted in yellow, and the
   case appears as a chip in its correct column, with a green border if we got
   it right and a red border if we did not.
5. Press **Next case** (or Enter). After case 10, press **See result**.
6. At the end, hover over any chip to review that case and its rule. **Try
   Again** repeats all ten.

## Lesson Plan

**Audience:** adult travelers preparing for the trip.  
**Duration:** 10–15 minutes.  
**Prerequisites:** the Greco-Turkish War, the millet system (Chapter 6), the
Convention Concerning the Exchange of Greek and Turkish Populations and its
exemptions, and Rhodes as an Italian possession in 1923.  
**Learning objective (Bloom: Apply):** Apply the rules of the 1923 population
exchange convention to ten household cases, deciding whether each was sent to
Greece, sent to Turkey, or not exchanged, with at least 8 of 10 correct on the
first attempt, including cases 3 and 4.

### Activities

1. **Read the rules (2 min):** Before case 1, say in one sentence what the
   convention used to decide who was "Greek" and who was "Turkish". Notice
   that the rules never mention language.
2. **Apply (8 min):** Work through all ten cases. For each, name the rule you
   are using before choosing an outcome.
3. **Read the summary (3 min):** At the end, count the households in each
   column. Half were not exchanged. Find the three different reasons why
   (an exemption, a religion the convention did not cover, and a territory
   outside Greece and Turkey).
4. **Connect to the trip (2 min):** We see the Church of Agios Titos in
   Heraklion on Day 9, a mosque until 1923, and Rhodes on Day 8, which the
   exchange did not touch. Say which rule explains each.

### Assessment

Mastery is 8 of 10 correct on the first attempt, including cases 3 and 4,
which test the religion-not-language rule. As a follow-up, ask: *A
Turkish-speaking Orthodox family from Cappadocia and a Greek-speaking Muslim
family from Crete each crossed the Aegean in 1923, in opposite directions.
Why did each go where it went?* A strong answer says the convention sorted
people by religion, not by language, a legacy of the millet system.
