## About This MicroSim

Acts 17:22–31 gives Paul a speech to the Athenians on the Areopagus, with
Stoic and Epicurean philosophers among his listeners. It is a lesson in
persuasion: it starts from what the audience already believes and only later
asks them to accept something new. This MicroSim lets us find the turn
ourselves.

Ten lines, paraphrased from the speech, come one at a time in speech order.
For each, we predict how a Stoic listener would hear it:

- **Common ground:** a Stoic could already agree with the words.
- **Something new:** a Stoic would hear a claim his philosophy did not make.

After each prediction, the answer appears with a short note on the Stoic
background, and the line is placed on a strip from the beginning of the speech
to its end, in the upper lane for common ground or the lower lane for
something new.

| # | Line (paraphrase) | Acts | Answer | Stoic or Epicurean echo |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | "I see that in every way you are very religious." | 17:22 | Common ground | Stoic respect for piety |
| 2 | God does not live in shrines made by human hands. | 17:24 | Common ground | Zeno: no need for temples |
| 3 | God is not served by human hands, as though he needed anything. | 17:25 | Common ground | Stoics and Epicureans: the divine needs nothing |
| 4 | From one ancestor God made every nation. | 17:26 | Common ground | One human community, citizens of one world |
| 5 | God is not far from each one of us. | 17:27 | Common ground | Seneca: God is near you, with you, within you |
| 6 | "For we too are his offspring." | 17:28 | Common ground | Aratus, *Phaenomena* 5; Cleanthes' *Hymn to Zeus* |
| 7 | The divine is not like an image of gold, silver, or stone. | 17:29 | Common ground | Stoic criticism of images |
| 8 | God now commands all people everywhere to repent. | 17:30 | Something new | A command from a personal God |
| 9 | God has fixed a day to judge the world by a man he has appointed. | 17:31 | Something new | Stoics: endless cycles of fire, no single judgment |
| 10 | God has given proof by raising that man from the dead. | 17:31 | Something new | No school expected a body raised to life |

The tally is seven and three. After line 10, the sim shows what Acts says
happened next: at the mention of the resurrection some scoffed, others said
they would hear him again, and a few joined him, among them Dionysius the
Areopagite and a woman named Damaris. The scoffing comes exactly at the break.

**Reading the answers carefully.** "Common ground" means that a Stoic could
assent to the words, not that Paul and the Stoics meant the same thing by them.
For a Stoic, the divine reason pervades the world and is in a sense the world;
in the speech, God made the world and is distinct from it. Some scholars, such
as C. Kavin Rowe, argue that the speech uses Greek vocabulary to say something
quite different from the start, so the overlap is in the words more than in
the worldview. Nearly every idea in lines 2 to 7 also has roots in the Hebrew
scriptures, which the speech does not quote. The answers follow the usual
scholarly reading, and the sim says so; Stoics were not all alike.

**Two readerships.** For many Christian readers, this is Paul's own
proclamation and a model for speaking about faith to people of another
culture. Most historians read it as a speech Luke composed, as ancient
historians commonly did, to give the sense of what Paul said; its themes still
match what Paul's letters say he told Gentiles. The sim works the same way for
both readings: it asks only how the lines would sound to a Stoic in the
audience Luke describes.

**The quotations.** "For we too are his offspring" is from Aratus of Soli,
*Phaenomena* line 5, a 3rd-century BCE poem written under Stoic influence;
Cleanthes' *Hymn to Zeus* has a similar line. The line before it, "In him we
live and move and have our being," is attributed to the Cretan poet Epimenides
only by much later Christian writers, and that attribution is uncertain, so
the sim does not use it.

## How to Use

1. Read the line in the panel and its reference in Acts.
2. Predict with **1 Common ground** or **2 Something new** (or press 1 or 2).
   Only the first prediction counts.
3. Read the feedback and the Stoic background. The line joins the strip in its
   lane, with a green border if you were right and a red dashed border if not.
   The tally beside each lane appears after each reveal.
4. Press **Next line** (or Enter). After line 10, press **See the reaction**.
5. On the final screen, click any line on the strip to read it again.
   **Try Again** repeats all ten lines.

## Lesson Plan

**Audience:** adult travelers reading Chapter 16, "Day 11: Athens, the Agora,
Democracy, and Paul on the Areopagus", before or after climbing the
Areopagus.  
**Duration:** 10–15 minutes.  
**Prerequisites:** Stoicism, the Epicureans, Paul in Athens, the Areopagus,
Paul and the Stoics, the Areopagus speech and its five moves, and Aratus.  
**Learning objective (Bloom: Analyze):** Differentiate the lines of Paul's
Areopagus speech that build on what Stoic listeners already believed from the
lines that ask them to accept something new, judging at least 8 of 10 lines
correctly on the first attempt.

### Activities

1. **Predict (8 min):** Work through the ten lines. Before each answer, ask:
   *Could Zeno, Seneca, or Epictetus have said this?*
2. **Find the turn (2 min):** On the final strip, where does the speech
   change lanes? Which of the chapter's five moves does that match?
3. **Discuss (5 min):** Why might a speaker build seven lines of common ground
   before asking for anything? Is the common ground only in the words, or in
   the ideas too? Think of a time you had to persuade someone who did not share
   your starting point.

### Assessment

Mastery is 8 of 10 correct on the first attempt, including lines 9 and 10.
A follow-up question: *Line 6 quotes a Greek poet. Why would quoting Aratus
persuade this audience more than quoting scripture?* A strong answer says that
Aratus was the listeners' own poet, writing under Stoic influence, so the
quotation starts from what they already accepted.

## References

1. Acts 17:16–34, in any modern translation (for example the NRSV or the NIV). The source of the speech and of the audience's reaction; the sim paraphrases it.
2. [Areopagus sermon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Areopagus_sermon) — Wikipedia. The speech, its setting, its quotations from Greek poets, and its interpretation.
3. Aratus, *Phaenomena* 1–18, the opening hymn to Zeus, with line 5, "for we are also his offspring." See [Aratus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aratus) — Wikipedia.
4. Cleanthes, *Hymn to Zeus*, line 4, the Stoic hymn that also calls humans the offspring of Zeus. See [Cleanthes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cleanthes) — Wikipedia.
5. Plutarch, *On Stoic Self-Contradictions* 1034B — Zeno's teaching that temples should not be built, since no work of builders is worth much or holy.
6. Seneca, *Moral Letters to Lucilius* 41.1 — "God is near you, he is with you, he is within you."
7. Epicurus, *Letter to Menoeceus* and *Principal Doctrines* 1–2 — the gods untroubled and needing nothing, and death as nothing to us.
8. [Stoic physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stoic_physics) — Wikipedia. The divine *logos*, and the cosmic conflagration and renewal (*ekpyrosis*).
9. C. Kavin Rowe, *World Upside Down: Reading Acts in the Graeco-Roman Age* (Oxford University Press, 2009). Argues that the speech's overlap with Stoicism is largely in vocabulary, not worldview.
10. Martin Dibelius, "Paul on the Areopagus," in *Studies in the Acts of the Apostles* (London: SCM, 1956). The classic case that the speech is a Hellenistic composition by Luke.
