# Mind the Gap: How Old Is the Evidence?

## About This MicroSim

How much should we trust a story about the past? The chapter offers a simple
rule built on two questions: **how long after the event was the earliest
surviving evidence made**, and **did its author have a reason to shape it?**
This MicroSim applies the rule to eight stories from our two sites, Troy and
Gallipoli, on a timeline that runs from the Late Bronze Age to the 20th
century.

| Verdict | Rule |
|---|---|
| Firm history | Evidence within 10 years, no known reason to invent |
| Real core, shaped telling | Evidence within 10 years, but the author had a reason to shape it, or a detail is known to be invented |
| Late or unsupported | Earliest evidence more than 10 years after the event |

For each story the event appears on the timeline as a blue dot. Before
anything else is shown, we predict when the earliest surviving evidence was
made and mark it with a gold ring. Then the evidence appears as a red square,
joined to the event by a gap bar, together with the author and their stake.
Only then do we judge.

| Story | Event | Earliest evidence | Gap | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| A city on the Hisarlık mound burned | c. 1180 BCE | The Troy VIIa destruction layer itself | 0 years | Firm history |
| A Hittite treaty with Alaksandu of Wilusa | c. 1280 BCE | The treaty tablet from Hattusa | 0 years | Firm history |
| A ten-year Greek siege to win back Helen | c. 1180 BCE, if it happened | Homer's *Iliad*, c. 700 BCE | about 480 years | Late or unsupported |
| The wooden horse | c. 1180 BCE, if it happened | Brief mentions in the *Odyssey*, c. 700 BCE; fullest telling in Virgil, c. 19 BCE | about 480 years | Late or unsupported |
| Mehmed II at Troy, avenging the Trojans | 1462 | Kritoboulos's history of Mehmed, c. 1467 | about 5 years | Real core, shaped telling |
| Priam's Treasure and Sophia's shawl | 1873 | Schliemann's own account, 1874 | 1 year | Real core, shaped telling |
| Three Allied battleships lost on March 18, 1915 | 1915 | Naval records of both sides | 0 years | Firm history |
| The "Johnnies and Mehmets" words of 1934 | 1934 | Şükrü Kaya's newspaper interview, 1953 | 19 years | Late or unsupported |

When all eight are judged, the timeline shows every gap at once. The longest
bars belong to the most famous stories, the siege and the wooden horse.

Two stories ask for care. The losses of March 18, 1915, were men, not only
ships: the French battleship *Bouvet* sank within minutes with most of her
crew. And the "Johnnies and Mehmets" words are judged here only as a claim
about who wrote them in 1934. As cultural memory, read at Anzac Day services
and carved at Anzac Cove, they are real whoever wrote them, as the chapter
explains.

## How to Use

1. Pick a story card above the timeline. Its event appears as a blue dot in
   its own row. Hover over any marker for details.
2. **Predict:** click the timeline where you think the earliest surviving
   evidence was made, or type a year and choose BCE or CE. Your guess appears
   as a gold ring. Press **Reveal the evidence**.
3. The timeline zooms to the story and shows the evidence, the gap bar, and
   the author's stake. The panel says how close your guess was (within 50
   years counts as close for a BCE event, within 5 years for a CE event).
   The prediction is not scored.
4. **Judge:** choose one of the three verdicts and press **Check**. A wrong
   first answer gives a hint; you get two tries. The rule that decides the
   story is highlighted on the right.
5. Press **Next story** to continue. After all eight, press **See all eight**
   for the full timeline, then click any story card to review it.
6. **Full span** returns to the whole timeline; **Start over** reshuffles the
   stories. Drag the timeline sideways to pan.

## Lesson Plan

**Audience:** adult travelers preparing for Day 6 at Troy and Gallipoli.  
**Duration:** 15–20 minutes.  
**Prerequisites:** primary source and myth versus history (Chapter 4); Troy
VIIa, Wilusa, the *Iliad*, the *Odyssey*, the Trojan Horse and Priam's
Treasure (this chapter); the naval attack of March 18, 1915 (Chapter 7); the
Johnnies and Mehmets words (this chapter); and the three verdicts.  
**Learning objective (Bloom: Evaluate):** Judge each of eight stories about
Troy and Gallipoli as firm history, a real core with a shaped telling, or late
or unsupported, by comparing the date of the event with the date and author of
its earliest surviving evidence, with at least 7 of 8 correct on the first
try.

### Activities

1. **Predict (5 min):** For each story, commit to a date for the earliest
   evidence before revealing it. Notice which famous stories you expected to
   have early sources.
2. **Judge (8 min):** Apply both criteria, the gap and the author's stake,
   in that order.
3. **Look at the whole (3 min):** On the final timeline, compare the longest
   bars with the shortest. Which stories are most famous, and which are best
   supported?
4. **Discuss (4 min):** The Mehmed and Schliemann stories have tiny gaps but
   are still shaped. Why is an early source not automatically an honest one?

### Assessment

Mastery is at least 7 of 8 verdicts correct on the first try. A follow-up
question: *Hisarlık is a real Late Bronze Age city that burned around 1180
BCE. Does that make the ten-year siege of the Iliad firm history?* No: a real
place does not prove the story told about it; the earliest account of the
siege is about 480 years later.

Common misconceptions this activity targets:

- A famous story told everywhere must have an early source.
- A source written by an eyewitness, or soon after, is automatically
  reliable.
- A real place proves the story told about it.
- A story with a late source must be false. (It may be true; it cannot be
  shown to be.)
