## About This MicroSim

Almost everything we hear about Paul, from guides, sermons, paintings and
films, mixes four kinds of statement:

| Bin | What belongs there |
|---|---|
| **Paul's own letters** | Stated in one of the seven letters scholars agree Paul wrote, whether or not Acts also says it. |
| **Acts only** | Stated in the Book of Acts, written a generation later (most scholars say about 80–90 CE), and not in Paul's letters. |
| **Scholars' inference** | Not stated in any source; reasoned from the evidence. |
| **Later tradition** | First told by writers after the New Testament. |

This MicroSim gives us fourteen familiar claims, one at a time and in a fixed
order, and asks of each one: **who says so?** One rule decides the close
cases: when Paul's letters state a claim, it belongs in *Paul's own letters*
even if Acts says it too, because a man's own words come first.

Sorting a claim is not the same as judging it true or false. Acts is an
ancient source that is often accurate where it can be checked, and a claim
that comes only from Acts or from tradition may well be true; some readers
hold it as true on the authority of scripture or of the church. The sorter
asks a narrower historian's question: *where does this claim first appear?*
That question lets readers of faith and secular historians talk about the
same evidence.

The result usually surprises people. Most of the vivid details of the
familiar story (Tarsus, Roman citizenship, the name Saul, the light on the
road) come from Acts. Paul's own letters give the inner story: a Pharisee and
a persecutor, a retreat to Arabia after his call, and a man who worked with
his hands so as not to burden anyone.

## How to Use

1. Read the four bin definitions under the colored headings.
2. Read the statement in the white panel and ask: *who says so?*
3. Place it: press one of the four buttons below the bins, click a bin, or
   press keys 1 to 4. Each card has one attempt and cannot be moved.
4. Read the feedback, which cites the passage. The card stays in the bin we
   chose, marked ✓ or ✗; the correct bin is outlined in green and a wrong
   choice in red.
5. Press **Next card** (or Enter). After card 14, press **See result**.
6. The summary shows the score and regroups all fourteen cards by their
   correct bin. Hover over any card to read its reason again. **Sort again**
   clears the bins and restarts from card 1 in the same order.

## Lesson Plan

**Audience:** adult travelers preparing for the visit to Ephesus.  
**Duration:** 10–15 minutes.  
**Prerequisites:** primary sources and myth versus history (Chapter 4); the
Book of Acts, the Pauline letters as Paul's own words, Paul as persecutor, the
road to Damascus, Timothy and Titus, Roman citizenship, and Tarsus (Chapter 9).  
**Learning objective (Bloom: Analyze):** Differentiate claims about Paul by
their source, sorting fourteen statements into Paul's own letters, Acts only,
scholars' inference, or later tradition, with at least 11 of 14 correct.

### Activities

1. **Predict (2 min):** Before starting, write down three things you "know"
   about Paul. Guess which of them Paul says about himself.
2. **Sort (8 min):** Work through the fourteen cards. Before each placement,
   say aloud which text you would open to check it: a letter, Acts, or
   neither.
3. **Read the summary (3 min):** Compare the Acts-only bin with the letters
   bin. Notice which kind of detail each one holds.
4. **Test your own list (2 min):** Return to your three claims and sort them
   with the same four bins.

### Assessment

Mastery is 11 of 14 correct on the first attempt. As a follow-up, ask:
*In Ephesus tomorrow, a guide says, "Saul of Tarsus, a Roman citizen, was
struck blind by a light on the road and changed his name to Paul." Sort each
part of that sentence.* A strong answer says Tarsus, citizenship, the name
Saul and the light all come from Acts only, and the name change is in no
source at all: Acts simply begins calling him Paul (Acts 13:9).

   translation, for example the
   [New Revised Standard Version](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Galatians+1&version=NRSVUE).
   The passages cited in each card's feedback.
2. [Paul the Apostle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_the_Apostle) —
   Wikipedia. Paul's life, the sources for it, and the questions scholars
   debate, including his citizenship and the dating of his call.
3. [Aretas IV Philopatris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aretas_IV_Philopatris) —
   Wikipedia. The Nabataean king whose death around 40 CE anchors the date of
   Paul's escape from Damascus.
4. [Acts of Paul and Thecla](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acts_of_Paul_and_Thecla) —
   Wikipedia. The 2nd-century story that gives the only physical description
   of Paul.
5. [Tertullian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tertullian) — Wikipedia. One of
   the earliest writers, about 200 CE, to say that Paul died by the sword,
   "a death like John's," in Rome.
6. E. P. Sanders, *Paul: A Very Short Introduction* (Oxford University Press,
   2001). A clear account of how historians weigh the letters against Acts.
7. Wayne A. Meeks and John T. Fitzgerald, eds., *The Writings of St. Paul*,
   2nd ed. (Norton, 2007). The letters with ancient and modern readings of
   Paul, from many traditions.
