## About This MicroSim

In the Eastern Mediterranean, the cities that grew rich, and the cities that
were fought over, sat on the sea lanes. This map shows 16 places, from Venice
to Alexandria and from Chersonesos in Crimea to Byblos in Lebanon. Gold dots
are stops on our trip; dark blue dots are places we will not visit but that
explain the ones we will.

Four trade routes are hidden at the start, one from each of four eras:

| Route | Era | Path | Cargo |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | Bronze Age, c. 1300 BCE | Byblos → Enkomi → Uluburun wreck → Rhodes → Knossos → Mycenae | Copper, tin, glass, ivory, ebony |
| B | Classical, 5th century BCE | Chersonesos → Byzantium → Troy → Piraeus | Black Sea wheat for Athens |
| C | Hellenistic, 3rd century BCE | Byzantium → Troy → Rhodes → Alexandria | Grain, wine, and goods passing Rhodes's harbor |
| D | Byzantine and Venetian, 13th–15th centuries CE | Venice → Heraklion → Byzantium | Silk and spices from the Silk Roads; Cretan wine and grain |

Each route appears only after we answer the question about it, so we have to
reason from the map first: where are the narrow places, and who sits on them?
Once drawn, a route stays visible, and an arrow on each leg shows the
direction of travel.

These are **simplified routes**, not records of single voyages. Each line runs
through the listed places in order, with a few unmarked turning points added so
that it stays on the water rather than cutting across Cyprus, the Peloponnese,
or Anatolia. Real ships hugged the coasts and waited out the winter storms.

## How to Use

1. Read question 1 and look at the map before answering. Each question has
   one attempt.
2. Click an answer. The correct choice turns green, a wrong choice turns red,
   the explanation appears, and the related route is drawn.
3. Press **Next question**. Questions 2 and 3 both use route B, the grain
   route to Athens.
4. After question 5, press **See result** for your score. All four routes are
   then on the map.
5. Click any dot to read what the place was and which day of our trip it
   falls on. Click a route to read its era and cargo.
6. Zoom with the **+** and **−** buttons and drag to pan; the mouse wheel
   scrolls the page instead of the map.
7. **Restart** hides the routes, clears the score, and shuffles the order of
   the answer choices.

## Lesson Plan

**Audience:** adult travelers preparing for the trip.  
**Duration:** 10–15 minutes.  
**Prerequisites:** trade route, chokepoint, the Bosphorus and the Dardanelles,
the Uluburun shipwreck, and the Bronze Age, Classical, Hellenistic, and
Byzantine periods.  
**Learning objective (Bloom: Understand):** Infer why places on chokepoints and
sea lanes became rich or fought over, by predicting the outcome of five
route-based questions before each route is revealed, scoring at least 4 of 5.

### Activities

1. **Predict (5 min):** Answer the five questions in order, committing to an
   answer before each route appears. Say the reason aloud or jot it down
   before clicking.
2. **Look for repeats (3 min):** With all four routes drawn, find the places
   that appear on routes from more than one era. The Bosphorus at Byzantium
   and the Dardanelles at Troy lie on routes B, C, and D.
3. **Find the crossings (3 min):** Locate Rhodes and Crete. Explain why an
   island where routes cross could grow rich without ruling much land.
4. **Connect to the trip (3 min):** Click the gold dots. For each trip stop,
   name the route it sits on and the cargo that passed it.

### Assessment

Mastery is 4 of 5 correct on the first attempt. As a follow-up, ask: *The
Isthmus of Corinth is a chokepoint of a different kind, a narrow strip of land
between two seas. What could a city there profit from?* A strong answer names
harbor fees and tolls on cargo and ships carried across the isthmus, and
control of the only land road into the Peloponnese.
