## About This MicroSim

The Persian Wars are easy to remember as one battle at Marathon. This map walks
the whole war in order, from the burning of Sardis in 498 BCE to the Greek
victories at Plataea and Mycale in 479 BCE, and asks us to use the map as
evidence. At five places we choose why the fighting or the crossing happened
there before the explanation appears.

The ten places, in order:

| Step | Place | Date | Scored question? |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Sardis | 498 BCE; winter 481/480 BCE | No |
| 2 | Marathon | 490 BCE | Yes |
| 3 | Abydos, on the Hellespont | Spring 480 BCE | Yes |
| 4 | The Athos canal | Dug about 483–480 BCE | No (context stop) |
| 5 | Thermopylae | August or September 480 BCE | Yes |
| 6 | Artemisium | The same days as Thermopylae | No (context stop) |
| 7 | The Acropolis of Athens | September 480 BCE | Yes |
| 8 | The strait of Salamis | September 480 BCE | Yes |
| 9 | Plataea | August 479 BCE | No |
| 10 | Mycale | 479 BCE | No |

The map zooms to each place so that the ground itself is visible: the plain of
Marathon, the narrows of the Hellespont, the pass at Thermopylae, and the strait
between Salamis and the mainland. Colored lines are added as the war moves:
Darius's fleet in 490 BCE, then Xerxes' army and fleet side by side in 480 BCE,
then the Greek army and fleet in 479 BCE. The routes are simplified sketches
through the places Herodotus names, not traced roads or sea lanes.

**Two added stops.** The chapter's specification lists eight places. This sim
adds the **Athos canal** and **Artemisium** as unscored context stops, because
they show what the eight alone hide: Xerxes' army and fleet moved together and
depended on each other, and the Greeks defended a pass on land and a strait at
sea on the same days.

**Locations.** Coordinates were checked against each place's Wikipedia article
in November 2026. Artemisium, Salamis and Mycale mark the area of a sea or shore
battle, not an exact spot, and the sim says so. At Thermopylae, the modern
coastline lies several kilometers farther out than in 480 BCE, when the sea
came almost to the foot of the cliffs; the sim notes this, because the modern
plain would otherwise hide the very reason the pass mattered.

## How to Use

1. Read step 1, Sardis, and the opening question: *A Persian king wants revenge
   on Athens. How will he get there?* Press **Next place**.
2. At a numbered place with a question, look at the map first, then choose
   **(a)**, **(b)** or **(c)** (or press A, B or C). Only the first choice counts.
3. The feedback says **Correct.** or **Not quite.**, gives the reason, marks the
   correct option in green, and shows the place's date and note.
4. Press **Next place** (or Enter) to go on. **Back** returns to earlier places;
   answered questions show your choice but cannot be answered again.
5. After place 10, press **See the whole route** for the full map and your
   score out of 5. Click any numbered marker to read its note again.
6. Zoom with **+** and **−** and drag to pan; the mouse wheel scrolls the page.
   **Start Again** clears all answers.

## Lesson Plan

**Audience:** adult travelers preparing for Day 10 in Athens.  
**Duration:** 10–15 minutes.  
**Prerequisites:** the Persian Wars, the Ionian Revolt, Herodotus, the Battle of
Marathon, Themistocles, the trireme, the Battle of Salamis, and the Hellespont
(Chapters 7 and 15).  
**Learning objective (Bloom: Understand):** Infer, at five places in the
Persian Wars, why the fighting or the crossing happened there, choosing the
geographic reason before it is revealed and getting at least 4 of 5 correct.

### Activities

1. **Predict the road (2 min):** At Sardis, before pressing Next, trace with a
   finger how a Persian army could reach Athens: by land around the north of
   the Aegean, or by sea across it. Both were tried.
2. **Infer from the map (8 min):** At each question, name what you see on the
   map (a plain, a narrow strait, a pass between mountains and sea) before
   choosing a reason.
3. **Read the whole route (3 min):** On the final map, follow the red army
   line and the blue fleet line from the Hellespont to Athens. Where do they
   run side by side, and what happened to the army after the fleet lost?

### Assessment

Mastery is 4 of 5 correct on the first attempt. As a follow-up, ask: *Why could
the Greeks, with fewer ships and soldiers, choose Thermopylae and Salamis as
places to fight?* A strong answer says that a narrow pass and a narrow strait
let only a few men or ships fight at once, so the larger Persian numbers
counted for less, and that the army depended on the fleet for its food.
