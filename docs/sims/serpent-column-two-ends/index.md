## About This MicroSim

The Serpent Column has two ends. It was cast after the Greek victory over the
Persians at Plataea in 479 BCE and set up beside Apollo's altar at Delphi,
where three intertwined bronze snakes held up a golden tripod. By 330 CE
Constantine had moved it to the Hippodrome of his new capital, Constantinople,
and it still stands there, in Istanbul, headless, in a pit. Its coils carry the
names of the 31 Greek communities that fought, under the heading "These fought
the war."

No single place tells its whole story. This MicroSim gives ten claims about the
column and asks where each could be checked:

| Claim | Where to check it |
|---|---|
| Bronze coils carry the names of the Greek cities that fought. | In Istanbul |
| A stone base by the great altar marks where the monument stood. | At Delphi |
| A bronze copy shows how the column looked in its setting. | At Delphi |
| The upper jaw of one serpent head survives. | In Istanbul |
| The column stands in a pit because the ground has risen since Byzantine times. | In Istanbul |
| The temple terrace where the monument stood is held up by the polygonal wall. | At Delphi |
| The Spartan commander Pausanias had his own name inscribed first, and it was erased. | Only in a written source |
| A golden tripod once sat on top of the three heads. | Only in a written source |
| The monument was made from a tenth of the spoils of Plataea. | Only in a written source |
| The Phocian leaders took the tripod's gold during the Third Sacred War. | Only in a written source |

The cards appear in a shuffled order. The totals carry the lesson: Delphi has
the place (3), Istanbul has the object (3), and the writers have the story (4).
Four of the ten claims rest on Herodotus, Thucydides, and the travel writer
Pausanias, not on anything we can see at either end.

**Notes on the evidence.** Herodotus (9.81) says the Greeks set aside a tenth
of the spoils of Plataea for Delphi and dedicated from it the golden tripod
that stood on the three-headed bronze serpent next to the altar. Thucydides
(1.132) says the Spartan regent Pausanias first had a couplet inscribed on the
tripod naming himself as the victor, and that the Spartans erased it and
inscribed the names of the cities instead. The travel writer Pausanias
(10.13.9), in the 2nd century CE, says the Phocian leaders carried off the
gold during the Third Sacred War (356–346 BCE) while the bronze remained.
Ottoman pictures of the 16th century still show the three heads; accounts
differ on exactly when they broke off, at the end of the 17th century or soon
after. Card 6 simplifies slightly: the polygonal wall is the retaining wall
along the south side of the temple terrace, the terrace on which the monument
stood near the altar.

## How to Use

1. Read the question and the three bin names. All ten cards start in the tray
   at the top.
2. Click a card to read it in full in the white panel. Then drag it into a
   bin, or press one of the three colored bin buttons (or keys 1, 2, 3). The
   next card is selected automatically.
3. A placed card can be dragged to another bin, or back to the tray, until
   you press **Check**. Arrow keys move the selection.
4. When all ten are placed, press **Check** (or Enter). Each card is marked
   ✓ or ✗, and the panel shows why for the selected card. Only the first Check
   counts toward mastery.
5. Move the red cards and press **Check** again until all ten are correct.
   The bins then show their totals and the summary. **Reset** clears every
   placement and returns the cards to the tray in the same order.

## Lesson Plan

**Audience:** adult travelers reading Chapter 18, "Day 12: Delphi", who saw
the column's other end in Istanbul on Day 2 (Chapter 4).  
**Duration:** 10 minutes.  
**Prerequisites:** the Serpent Column, Plataea and the Persian Wars
(Chapter 15), the Hippodrome (Chapter 4), the Third Sacred War (Chapter 17),
Herodotus, Thucydides and Pausanias as sources, the great altar and the temple
terrace, and the polygonal wall (Chapter 18).  
**Learning objective (Bloom: Analyze):** Attribute each of 10 claims about the
Serpent Column to the place where its evidence can be checked, at Delphi, in
Istanbul, or only in an ancient written source, with at least 8 of 10 correct
on the first attempt.

### Activities

1. **Predict (1 min):** Before starting, guess how many of the ten claims you
   could check by standing in front of the bronze in Istanbul.
2. **Sort (6 min):** Place all ten cards and press Check once. Note the first
   score.
3. **Discuss (3 min):** Which claims felt as if they came from the object but
   really come from a writer? Why might a museum label mix the two without
   saying so?

### Assessment

Mastery is at least 8 of 10 cards in the correct bin on the first Check. A
follow-up question: *What would we lose if Herodotus and Thucydides had not
survived?* A strong answer says we would still have the names on the coils and
the base at Delphi, but not the tripod, the tithe of the spoils, or the story
of Pausanias' erased boast.
