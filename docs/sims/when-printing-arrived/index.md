## About This MicroSim

"The Ottomans had no printing until 1727" is one of the most repeated
sentences about the empire, and it is wrong in an instructive way. Istanbul
had a working press by about 1493, run by Sephardic Jewish refugees, and
Armenian and Greek presses followed. What waited until 1729 was printing in
the Arabic script for Muslim readers. This chart makes the difference
visible.

Each bar is the number of years from 1455, the approximate date of
Gutenberg's Bible, to the first printing named. Bars are ordered by year,
earliest at the top, and colored by region.

| # | Bar | Region | First printing | Years after 1455 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Latin, Mainz | Western Europe | c. 1455 | 0 |
| 2 | Latin, Venice | Western Europe | 1469 | 14 |
| 3 | Hebrew, Italy | Western Europe | 1475 | 20 |
| 4 | Greek, Milan | Western Europe | 1476 | 21 |
| 5 | Hebrew, Istanbul | Ottoman Empire | 1493 | 38 |
| 6 | Arabic script, Italy | Western Europe | 1514 | 59 |
| 7 | Armenian, Istanbul | Ottoman Empire | 1567 | 112 |
| 8 | Greek, Istanbul | Ottoman Empire | 1627 | 172 |
| 9 | Arabic script, Aleppo | Ottoman Empire | 1706 | 251 |
| 10 | Ottoman Turkish (Arabic script), Istanbul | Ottoman Empire | 1729 | 274 |

**Notes on the dates.** Several of these "firsts" carry scholarly caveats,
which the infoboxes mention:

- **Bar 1.** The first finished copies of the Gutenberg Bible were available
  in 1454 or 1455; about 180 copies were printed and about 49 survive.
- **Bar 3.** 1475 is the year of the first *dated* Hebrew books: Rashi's
  commentary on the Torah at Reggio di Calabria (February 1475) and the
  *Arba'ah Turim* at Piove di Sacco (July 1475). Undated Hebrew books printed
  in Rome may be a few years older.
- **Bar 5.** The *Arba'ah Turim* printed by David and Samuel ibn Nahmias
  carries a date equivalent to December 1493, but some scholars argue it was
  printed about ten years later, around 1503–1504.
- **Bar 6.** The *Kitab Salat al-Sawa'i*, a book of hours for Arabic-speaking
  Christians, names Fano on its colophon, but it may have been printed in
  Venice. It is the first known book printed in Arabic with movable type.
  (Arabic text had been printed from carved blocks in the Islamic world
  centuries earlier, so even "first Arabic printing" depends on the
  technology counted.)
- **Bar 8.** Nikodemos Metaxas's Greek press, backed by the Patriarch Cyril
  Lucaris, was closed by the authorities in 1628, within a year.
- **Bar 9.** The Aleppo press of the Patriarch Athanasius Dabbas, for
  Christian books, is usually counted the first press for Arabic script
  inside the Ottoman Empire. It ran until about 1711.
- **Bar 10.** Sultan Ahmed III's decree allowing İbrahim Müteferrika to print
  non-religious books came in 1727. The first book, the *Vankulu Lügati*, an
  Arabic–Turkish dictionary in two volumes, appeared in 1729. Its print run is
  usually given as 1,000 copies; Müteferrika's editions ran from 500 to 1,000
  copies each.

The chart does not show *why* the wait was so long, and it cannot confirm the
popular story that Sultan Bayezid II banned Arabic-script printing in 1485. No
copy of such a decree survives, and many historians now doubt it was issued.

## How to Use

1. Read the chart title and the axis, *Years after 1455*. Each bar ends with
   its year and its gap in years.
2. Hover over any bar, or tap it on a tablet, to open its infobox below the
   chart.
3. Answer the four questions, one attempt each. After each answer, the bars
   the question depends on are outlined in gold, the correct choice turns
   green, and the infobox opens on the key bar.
4. After question 4, press **See my result**. All the Ottoman bars are
   outlined so you can check who did the printing.
5. **Restart** clears the score and the outlines.

## Lesson Plan

**Audience:** adult travelers reading Chapter 6, "Day 4: Bazaars, Scripts,
and Presses".  
**Duration:** 10 minutes.  
**Prerequisites:** Gutenberg, the printing press, the Hebrew press of 1493,
İbrahim Müteferrika, delayed Arabic-script printing and the millet system,
from Chapter 6.  
**Learning objective (Bloom: Understand):** Interpret a chart of the years
between Gutenberg's Bible and the first printing in ten scripts and places,
answering at least 3 of 4 questions correctly.

### Activities

1. **Read the chart (2 min):** Which bar is the shortest Ottoman bar? Which
   is the longest? Open both infoboxes.
2. **Answer the questions (5 min):** Answer all four, then look at the gold
   outlines on the result screen.
3. **Explain the gap (3 min):** In two sentences, say what the 274-year gap
   measures and what it does not. (It measures the wait for Arabic-script
   printing for Muslim readers in Istanbul; it does not mean the Ottomans had
   no presses, or that Arabic could not be printed.)

### Assessment

Mastery is 3 of 4 correct on the first attempt. A follow-up question:
*Bar 6 shows Arabic printed in Italy in 1514. Why does that matter for
explaining the Ottoman delay?* Because the technology existed more than two
centuries before 1729, the reasons for waiting must also have been cultural,
economic and political, as the chapter's list of explanations suggests.
