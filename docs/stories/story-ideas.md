These are twenty-two ideas for short graphic novels, each 8 to 16 panels long.
They follow the trip from Istanbul to Athens, so a reader can meet each story
the day before or the evening after standing in the place where it happened.
Every story is built around something a traveler can actually see: a column
drum in a wall, a scratch on a marble railing, a jar taller than a person.

## Series Conventions

These recurring devices tie the stories together and send readers out to look
for themselves.

- **Then and Now.** The first panel shows the place in its own era. The last
  panel shows the same view today, ideally matched to one of our trip photos.
- **Find Glaux.** A little owl hides somewhere in one panel of every story:
  on a temple roof, on a ship's rail, on a coin. Little owls really do live on
  the Acropolis, so the hunt is not as far-fetched as it sounds.
- **Look Closer.** Glaux closes each story by pointing at one specific detail
  to find on site.
- **Myth or Evidence?** A small stamp marks any panel that shows legend rather
  than documented history. Several stories turn on exactly that question.
- **Crossovers.** Some characters and objects appear in more than one story
  (see the table at the end), so readers who follow the whole series get
  "wait, it's *him* again" moments.
- **Learning for All.** Where a story touches who got to read, write, or
  learn, the closing panel makes the connection, matching the thread that
  runs through the chapters.

## The Stories

### 1. Glaux and the Gods Who Moved In

**Chapter:** [1. The Long Timeline](../chapters/01-long-timeline/index.md) ·
**Setting:** Mount Olympus and the Acropolis, myth time · **Panels:** 14

**Hook.** Before we leave, Glaux introduces the gods we will keep running into
on the trip, and explains how a small owl ended up on the money of the richest
city in Greece.

**Panel beats:**

- Panels 1–3: Athena is born, fully armed, from the head of Zeus. Glaux,
  perched nearby, is unimpressed by the headache.
- Panels 4–8: Athena and Poseidon compete to be patron of Athens. Poseidon
  strikes the rock and a salt spring bubbles up. Athena plants an olive tree.
  The citizens pick the tree, because you cannot drink seawater.
- Panels 9–11: Athena's owl becomes the city's symbol and lands on the silver
  tetradrachm. People call the coins "owls."
- Panels 12–14: A "where the gods live on our route" map: Zeus's birth cave on
  Crete, Artemis at Ephesus, Helios at Rhodes, Apollo at Delphi, Athena in
  Athens.

**On the trip.** The olive tree beside the Erechtheion stands where the myth
says Athena's grew (it is a modern planting). Owl tetradrachms are on show in
Athens museums.

**Explore further.** Which gods took sides at Troy? Try
[The Olympian Gods at Troy](../sims/olympian-gods-at-troy/index.md).

### 2. Halfdan Was Here

**Chapter:** [4. Byzantine Constantinople and Hagia Sophia](../chapters/04-byzantine-constantinople/index.md) ·
**Setting:** Constantinople, 532–537 CE and about a thousand years ago ·
**Panels:** 12

**Hook.** The emperor Justinian builds the biggest dome in the world.
Centuries later, a bored Norse guard carves his name into its marble railing,
and it is still there.

**Panel beats:**

- Panels 1–3: The Nika riots of 532 burn the old church. Justinian decides to
  build something no one has seen before.
- Panels 4–7: Two mathematicians, Anthemius of Tralles and Isidore of Miletus,
  design a dome that seems to float on a ring of windows. In 537 Justinian
  walks in. A later legend gives him the line "Solomon, I have surpassed you."
- Panel 8: The first dome collapses in 558 and is rebuilt higher.
- Panels 9–11: A Norse soldier, probably of the emperor's Varangian Guard,
  standing through a very long service in the upper gallery, scratches runes
  into the parapet: a name, Halfdan.
- Panel 12: A tourist today leans over the same railing and finds the runes.

**On the trip.** The runic graffiti in the south gallery, the seraphim in the
dome's pendentives, and the building's change of use from church to mosque to
museum and back to mosque.

**Explore further.** Readers try to read the runes from a photo, then compare
their reading with the scholars'.

### 3. Ships Over the Hill

**Chapter:** [4. Byzantine Constantinople](../chapters/04-byzantine-constantinople/index.md)
and [5. Ottoman Istanbul](../chapters/05-ottoman-istanbul/index.md) ·
**Setting:** Constantinople, April–May 1453 · **Panels:** 16

**Hook.** A great iron chain blocks the Golden Horn. Twenty-one-year-old
Sultan Mehmed II solves the problem by sailing his ships over a hill.

**Panel beats:**

- Panels 1–3: The Theodosian Walls, a thousand years unbroken. The last
  emperor, Constantine XI, has too few men to defend them.
- Panels 4–6: The Ottoman cannons arrive, cast by a Hungarian founder named
  Orban who first offered his services to the Byzantines.
- Panels 7–10: The chain holds. On April 22, Ottoman crews haul dozens of
  ships on greased log rollers over the hills behind Galata and launch them
  into the Golden Horn. Dawn reveals them inside the harbor.
- Panels 11–14: The final assault on May 29. Constantine XI disappears in the
  fighting.
- Panels 15–16: Mehmed rides to Hagia Sophia. The city becomes Istanbul.

**On the trip.** Surviving links of the chain are displayed in Istanbul
museums. The land walls still run from the Sea of Marmara to the Golden Horn.

**Explore further.** [The Siege of 1453, Step by Step](../sims/siege-of-1453-step-through/index.md).

### 4. The Sultan's Sweet Tooth

**Chapter:** [6. Bazaars, Scripts, and Presses](../chapters/06-bazaars-scripts-presses/index.md) ·
**Setting:** Istanbul, 1660s to today · **Panels:** 12

**Hook.** Follow one box of Turkish delight and one pinch of saffron through
the Spice Bazaar and three centuries of Ottoman taste.

**Panel beats:**

- Panels 1–3: The Spice Bazaar opens in the 1660s as part of the New Mosque
  complex. Its Turkish name, *Mısır Çarşısı*, means "Egyptian Bazaar."
- Panels 4–6: Coffee arrives in Istanbul in the 1500s, and coffeehouses become
  places of gossip and politics. Sultan Murad IV shuts them down. The coffee
  wins in the end.
- Panels 7–9: In 1777 a confectioner remembered as Hacı Bekir opens a shop
  near the waterfront and makes *lokum* famous. His family's shop still sells
  it.
- Panels 10–11: Saffron's journey: the town of Safranbolu is named for it,
  and it is worth more by weight than almost anything else in the bazaar.
- Panel 12: Two travelers leave the bazaar with a box of lokum and a twist of
  saffron, which is exactly what we did.

**On the trip.** The L-shaped hall of the Spice Bazaar, the Hacı Bekir shop,
and the price board for saffron.

**Explore further.** [Reading Food in Layers](../sims/food-history-layers-sorter/index.md):
which dishes came from which era?

### 5. The Press That Waited

**Chapter:** [6. Bazaars, Scripts, and Presses](../chapters/06-bazaars-scripts-presses/index.md) ·
**Setting:** Istanbul, 1490s–1729 · **Panels:** 12

**Hook.** Istanbul had printing presses by the 1490s, so why did the first
Ottoman Turkish book in Arabic script not appear until 1729?

**Panel beats:**

- Panels 1–3: Jewish refugees from Spain set up a press in Istanbul in the
  1490s. Armenian and Greek presses follow.
- Panels 4–6: Ottoman scribes and calligraphers produce beautiful manuscripts.
  A popular legend says they blocked the press. Historians argue about whether
  that is true.
- Panels 7–10: İbrahim Müteferrika, a convert from Transylvania, petitions the
  Sultan, wins permission in 1727, and prints a dictionary in 1729.
- Panels 11–12: He prints a few dozen volumes before he dies. A Learning for
  All panel asks what a long delay costs a society, and who pays.

**On the trip.** Calligraphy in the mosques and at the Museum of Turkish and
Islamic Arts; old books in the shops near the Grand Bazaar.

**Explore further.** [Scribe or Press?](../sims/scribe-or-press/index.md) and
[When Printing Arrived](../sims/when-printing-arrived/index.md).

### 6. The Head Teacher in the Park

**Chapter:** [7. The End of Empire and the Birth of Modern Turkey](../chapters/07-end-of-empire-modern-turkey/index.md) ·
**Setting:** Istanbul, August 1928 · **Panels:** 10

**Hook.** A head of state stands at a chalkboard in a public park and teaches
a nation a new alphabet.

**Panel beats:**

- Panels 1–2: Ottoman Turkish is written in Arabic script, which fits Turkish
  vowels badly. Only about one person in ten can read.
- Panels 3–5: In Gülhane Park, Mustafa Kemal (Atatürk) chalks up the new
  Latin-based letters for a crowd. He earns the nickname *Başöğretmen*, "Head
  Teacher."
- Panels 6–8: Within months, the old script is replaced by law. Adults crowd
  into "Nation's Schools" to learn to read.
- Panels 9–10: A grandmother who learned to read in 1929 reads a street sign
  aloud to her grandchild. A Learning for All closing panel.

**On the trip.** Every Turkish sign we read. Dolmabahçe Palace, where the
clocks were stopped at 9:05, the minute Atatürk died in 1938.

**Explore further.** [Sort the Scripts](../sims/sort-the-scripts/index.md).

### 7. The Horse at the Gates

**Chapter:** [8. Troy and Gallipoli](../chapters/08-troy-and-gallipoli/index.md) ·
**Setting:** Troy, myth time and the 1870s · **Panels:** 16

**Hook.** Everyone knows the Trojan Horse. Almost nobody knows that the
*Iliad* ends before the horse ever appears, or that the man who "found" Troy
dug right through the city he was looking for.

**Panel beats:**

- Panels 1–6: The myth in quick cuts: ten years of siege, the Greeks pretend
  to sail away, the giant wooden horse, Laocoön's warning, soldiers climbing
  out at night, Troy burning.
- Panel 7: A Myth or Evidence? stamp. The horse story comes from the
  *Odyssey* and Virgil's *Aeneid*, not the *Iliad*.
- Panels 8–10: Frank Calvert, a British consul who owns part of the mound at
  Hisarlık, is sure Troy lies beneath it. Heinrich Schliemann arrives with
  money and a hurry.
- Panels 11–13: Schliemann cuts a huge trench, finds gold in 1873, and names
  it "Priam's Treasure." It turns out to be about a thousand years older than
  any Troy of Homer's war. He smuggles it out of the country.
- Panels 14–15: The treasure vanishes from Berlin in 1945 and turns up in a
  Moscow museum decades later.
- Panel 16: Children climb the wooden replica horse at the site, and on the
  Çanakkale waterfront stands the horse built for a Hollywood film.

**On the trip.** Schliemann's trench, which slices through layers of nine
cities stacked on top of each other, and both horses.

**Explore further.** [Mind the Gap: How Old Is the Evidence?](../sims/troy-gallipoli-evidence-gap/index.md)

### 8. The Soldier Who Carried His Enemy

**Chapter:** [8. Troy and Gallipoli](../chapters/08-troy-and-gallipoli/index.md) ·
**Setting:** Gallipoli peninsula, 1915 · **Panels:** 12

**Hook.** A famous statue shows a Turkish soldier carrying a wounded enemy
officer to safety. Did it really happen? The story is about the battle, and
about how nations decide what to remember.

**Panel beats:**

- Panels 1–4: Allied ships fail to force the Dardanelles. Troops land at
  Gallipoli in April 1915, among them Australians and New Zealanders.
- Panels 5–7: A young Ottoman officer, Mustafa Kemal, holds the high ground.
- Panels 8–9: During a truce to bury the dead, soldiers from both sides trade
  cigarettes and food in no-man's-land.
- Panels 10–11: The story behind the statue, told as a witness remembered it,
  with a Myth or Evidence? stamp: historians still debate its source.
- Panel 12: Australian, New Zealand and Turkish visitors stand together at the
  same memorial today.

**On the trip.** The *Mehmetçik* statue, trenches that sit only meters apart,
and the memorial words of reconciliation attributed to Atatürk.

**Explore further.** Readers sort which parts of the story are documented and
which are remembered, the same skill as
[Source, Myth, or Contested?](../sims/source-myth-or-contested/index.md)

### 9. Great Is Artemis!

**Chapter:** [9. Paul's Life and Letters](../chapters/09-paul-life-letters/index.md)
and [11. Paul's Ideas and Leadership](../chapters/11-paul-ideas-leadership/index.md) ·
**Setting:** Ephesus, about 52–55 CE · **Panels:** 16

**Hook.** A traveling tentmaker starts teaching in a lecture hall, and two
years later the silversmiths of Ephesus riot because he is hurting their
business.

**Panel beats:**

- Panels 1–3: Paul arrives in Ephesus, one of the largest cities in the Roman
  world, works at his trade, and teaches daily in the hall of Tyrannus.
- Panels 4–6: Letters go out and come in. He writes to the quarrelsome church
  in Corinth from here, and copies of his letters pass from hand to hand.
- Panels 7–12: Demetrius the silversmith, who sells little silver shrines of
  the goddess Artemis, warns his guild that Paul's message will ruin trade.
  The crowd floods the theater and chants "Great is Artemis of the Ephesians!"
  for two hours.
- Panels 13–14: The town clerk calms the crowd with a very Roman argument: we
  could be charged with rioting.
- Panels 15–16: Paul leaves. His letters outlive both the Temple of Artemis,
  one of the Seven Wonders, and the silversmiths' guild.

**On the trip.** The Great Theater, which held about 25,000 people. The single
re-erected column that is all that stands of the Temple of Artemis.

**Explore further.** [How Paul's Words Traveled](../sims/how-pauls-words-traveled/index.md)
and [Where in Paul's Life Was Each Letter Written?](../sims/paul-letters-on-his-timeline/index.md).

### 10. The Library With a Tomb in the Basement

**Chapter:** [10. Ephesus, a Greco-Roman City](../chapters/10-ephesus-greco-roman-city/index.md) ·
**Setting:** Ephesus, early 100s CE · **Panels:** 12

**Hook.** A son builds his father a library and buries him underneath it.
Then the sea walks away from the city.

**Panel beats:**

- Panels 1–3: Celsus, a Roman governor born in the East, dies. His son decides
  a library is the best monument a learned man could have.
- Panels 4–6: The façade rises, with four statues of the virtues: Wisdom,
  Excellence, Thought, and Knowledge. Inside, some 12,000 scrolls. Below, the
  father's marble coffin.
- Panels 7–9: A day in Ephesus: the marble public latrines where people sat
  side by side and talked business, the terrace houses with painted walls.
- Panels 10–11: River silt slowly fills the harbor. The coastline moves
  kilometers away and the port city dies.
- Panel 12: Archaeologists rebuild the library façade in the 1970s from its
  fallen pieces.

**On the trip.** The library façade, the latrines, the street that once led
to the harbor.

**Explore further.** [One Walk, Seven Centuries](../sims/ephesus-walk-timeline/index.md)
and [Documented, Debated, or Tradition?](../sims/ephesus-sites-evidence-judge/index.md).

### 11. The Giant Who Fell Down

**Chapter:** [12. Rhodes](../chapters/12-rhodes/index.md) ·
**Setting:** Rhodes, 305 BCE to the 650s CE · **Panels:** 12

**Hook.** Rhodes pays for a bronze giant about 33 meters tall by selling the
siege machines its enemy left behind. It stands for barely sixty years, then
lies on the ground for eight centuries as a tourist attraction.

**Panel beats:**

- Panels 1–3: The siege of 305–304 BCE. Demetrius "the Besieger" brings an
  enormous siege tower, fails anyway, and leaves his equipment behind.
- Panels 4–6: The sculptor Chares of Lindos spends twelve years raising a
  bronze statue of the sun god Helios.
- Panel 7: An earthquake topples it about 226 BCE.
- Panels 8–9: Visitors marvel that few people can wrap their arms around its
  fallen thumb.
- Panel 10: A Myth or Evidence? stamp over the famous image of the Colossus
  straddling the harbor entrance: physically impossible.
- Panels 11–12: In the 650s the bronze is sold for scrap. Today two bronze
  deer stand on columns at the harbor mouth.

**On the trip.** Mandraki harbor and its deer. Glaux asks where the statue
really stood. Nobody knows for sure.

**Explore further.** Some think the Antikythera Mechanism was built on Rhodes
(see story 21).

### 12. The Knights' Last Stand

**Chapter:** [12. Rhodes](../chapters/12-rhodes/index.md) ·
**Setting:** Rhodes, 1309–1523, and the 1930s · **Panels:** 14

**Hook.** For two centuries a military order of knights holds Rhodes. In 1522
Süleyman the Magnificent arrives with an army, and the siege ends with a
surprise: an honorable surrender.

**Panel beats:**

- Panels 1–3: The Knights Hospitaller take Rhodes and build. Each "tongue"
  (France, Italy, Spain, England and others) gets an inn on the Street of the
  Knights.
- Panels 4–9: The 1522 siege: tunnels and counter-tunnels under the walls,
  months of assaults, heavy losses on both sides.
- Panels 10–11: Süleyman offers terms. The knights sail away on January 1,
  1523, with their weapons, and end up on Malta.
- Panels 12–14: A twist ending: the "medieval" Palace of the Grand Master
  that visitors tour was largely rebuilt by the Italian government in the
  1930s.

**On the trip.** The Street of the Knights, the coats of arms over the inn
doors, the walk through the dry moat.

**Explore further.** [Who Built What We See in the Old Town?](../sims/rhodes-old-town-layers-sorter/index.md)
and [Rhodes Changes Hands, 1912–1948](../sims/rhodes-changes-hands-1912-1948/index.md).

### 13. Into the Labyrinth

**Chapter:** [13. Knossos, the Minoans, and Linear B](../chapters/13-knossos-minoans-linear-b/index.md) ·
**Setting:** Knossos, myth time and 1900 · **Panels:** 14

**Hook.** Theseus finds his way out of the Labyrinth with a ball of thread.
Three thousand years later an English archaeologist rebuilds the palace in
reinforced concrete, and visitors still struggle to find their way through
which parts are ancient.

**Panel beats:**

- Panels 1–6: King Minos, the Minotaur, Daedalus the inventor, Ariadne's
  thread, Theseus escaping.
- Panel 7: Glaux points out the *labrys*, the double axe carved all over the
  palace, and the much-debated idea that "labyrinth" means "house of the
  double axe."
- Panels 8–11: Arthur Evans buys the site, digs from 1900, and has artists
  "restore" frescoes from small fragments. He pours concrete columns and
  paints them red.
- Panels 12–14: Side by side, the original fragments and the restored fresco.
  What did the Minoans make, and what did Evans imagine?

**On the trip.** The red columns, the "throne room," the fresco copies on site
and the originals in the Heraklion Archaeological Museum.

**Explore further.** [Knossos: Minoan, Evans, or Copy?](../sims/knossos-minoan-or-evans/index.md)

### 14. The Architect Who Cracked the Code

**Chapter:** [13. Knossos, the Minoans, and Linear B](../chapters/13-knossos-minoans-linear-b/index.md) ·
**Setting:** Knossos, London and Brooklyn, 1900–1952 · **Panels:** 14

**Hook.** A palace fire bakes thousands of clay tablets hard. A schoolboy
hears a lecture about them, grows up to be an architect, and solves the puzzle
that defeated the experts. The thrilling secret turns out to be a count of
sheep.

**Panel beats:**

- Panels 1–2: The fire that destroyed the palace saved its records by firing
  the unbaked clay.
- Panels 3–4: Evans finds the tablets and calls the script Linear B. He never
  reads it.
- Panels 5–7: In 1936 fourteen-year-old Michael Ventris hears Evans lecture
  and decides to crack it.
- Panels 8–9: In Brooklyn, classicist Alice Kober builds the grids of related
  word endings that make the solution possible. She dies in 1950.
- Panels 10–12: Ventris expects the language to be Etruscan. In 1952 his grid
  spells out Cretan place names in Greek. He announces it on BBC radio.
- Panels 13–14: The tablets are inventories: sheep, wheat, chariot wheels.
  Glaux approves. Most of history is bookkeeping.

**On the trip.** Linear A and Linear B tablets in the Heraklion Archaeological
Museum.

**Explore further.** [Crack Linear B Like Ventris](../sims/crack-linear-b-like-ventris/index.md).

### 15. Twenty-One Years of Candia

**Chapter:** [14. Venetian Heraklion and Crete Today](../chapters/14-venetian-heraklion/index.md) ·
**Setting:** Candia (Heraklion), 1648–1669 · **Panels:** 12

**Hook.** One of the longest sieges in history: the Venetians hold the walled
city of Candia against the Ottomans for twenty-one years. The commander who
finally surrenders it turns up again eighteen years later, in Athens.

**Panel beats:**

- Panels 1–3: Venetian Candia: fortress walls, a harbor castle, the winged
  lion of St. Mark over the gates. A century earlier a local boy, Domenikos
  Theotokopoulos, grew up here and left to become El Greco.
- Panels 4–8: The siege drags on year after year as volunteers from across
  Europe come and go.
- Panels 9–10: Francesco Morosini surrenders the city in 1669 and is allowed
  to sail home.
- Panel 11: A teaser: Morosini in 1687, his guns aimed at the Acropolis (see
  story 18).
- Panel 12: Today, on the Martinengo Bastion, the grave of the novelist Nikos
  Kazantzakis with its epitaph, "I hope for nothing. I fear nothing. I am
  free."

**On the trip.** The walls, the harbor fortress, and the Lions Fountain,
which carries the name of an *earlier* Francesco Morosini, a Venetian
governor. Same name, different man.

**Explore further.** [Walking Venetian Candia](../sims/walking-venetian-candia-map/index.md).

### 16. The Jar Makers of Thrapsano

**Chapter:** [14. Venetian Heraklion and Crete Today](../chapters/14-venetian-heraklion/index.md) ·
**Setting:** Knossos storerooms, about 1600 BCE, and Thrapsano today ·
**Panels:** 10

**Hook.** In the Knossos storerooms stand clay jars taller than a person. In a
Cretan village today, potters still make them by hand.

**Panel beats:**

- Panels 1–3: A Minoan storeroom lined with giant *pithoi* holding olive oil,
  wine and grain.
- Panels 4–6: Thrapsano potters build a pithos in coils on a wheel turned by
  an apprentice. For generations they traveled across Crete each summer,
  setting up wherever they found clay and customers.
- Panels 7–8: The kiln firing.
- Panels 9–10: Travelers sip wine at a village winery and watch the potters,
  as we did on the Villages of Crete excursion. A Then and Now split: the same
  jar shape, thousands of years apart.

**On the trip.** The pithoi in the Knossos storerooms; the Thrapsano
workshops.

**Explore further.** [Read the Olive Oil Label](../sims/read-the-olive-oil-label/index.md).

### 17. Wooden Walls

**Chapter:** [15. Athens, the Acropolis, and the Persian Wars](../chapters/15-athens-acropolis/index.md) ·
**Setting:** Delphi, Athens and Salamis, 490–479 BCE · **Panels:** 16

**Hook.** The oracle at Delphi tells the terrified Athenians to trust their
"wooden wall." One clever politician decides that the wall is a fleet of
ships.

**Panel beats:**

- Panels 1–3: Marathon, 490 BCE. Athens beats the first Persian invasion.
- Panels 4–6: Ten years later King Xerxes returns with a far larger army.
  Athenian envoys at Delphi receive a grim prophecy and beg for a better one.
  The second answer mentions a "wooden wall."
- Panels 7–8: Themistocles argues that the wall means the new fleet.
- Panels 9–10: Athens is evacuated. The Persians burn the Acropolis.
- Panels 11–13: The sea battle at Salamis in 480 BCE: Xerxes watches from a
  throne on the shore as his fleet is wrecked in the narrows.
- Panels 14–15: After the final victory at Plataea, the Greeks send a bronze
  column of three twisted serpents to Delphi.
- Panel 16: Crossover: that Serpent Column now stands in Istanbul's old
  Hippodrome, where Constantine moved it.

**On the trip.** Column drums from the earlier, burned temple built into the
Acropolis north wall, visible from the streets of Plaka. The Serpent Column
in Sultanahmet.

**Explore further.** [Xerxes' Road to Salamis](../sims/persian-wars-campaign-map/index.md)
and [Two Ends of the Serpent Column](../sims/serpent-column-two-ends/index.md).

### 18. Pericles' Building Site

**Chapter:** [15. Athens, the Acropolis, and the Persian Wars](../chapters/15-athens-acropolis/index.md) ·
**Setting:** Athens, 447–432 BCE, and 1687 · **Panels:** 16

**Hook.** Pericles spends the allies' war chest on the most perfect building
in Greece, a temple with almost no straight lines, and is accused of misusing
the money.

**Panel beats:**

- Panels 1–3: The treasury of the Delian League moves from the island of Delos
  to Athens. Pericles proposes rebuilding the burned Acropolis.
- Panels 4–6: Ox carts haul marble from Mount Pentelikon. The architects
  Iktinos and Kallikrates check their plans; the sculptor Pheidias directs the
  sculpture.
- Panels 7–9: The secret of the curves: the floor rises in the middle, the
  columns swell and lean inward. Glaux hunts for a straight line and fails.
- Panels 10–11: Pericles' rivals accuse him of dressing Athens up in jewels
  bought with other cities' money.
- Panel 12: The towering gold-and-ivory Athena stands inside.
- Panels 13–15: A five-lives montage: temple, church, mosque, gunpowder store,
  and in 1687 a Venetian shell, fired under Morosini's command (see story 15),
  blows out its middle.
- Panel 16: Scaffolding and cranes today. The restoration continues.

**On the trip.** The rising floor line, the swelling columns, and the frieze
in the Acropolis Museum with gaps for the pieces held in London.

**Explore further.** [The Parthenon's Five Lives](../sims/parthenon-five-lives-timeline/index.md)
and [Agreed Fact or Position?](../sims/parthenon-marbles-fact-or-position/index.md)

### 19. The Man Who Asked Too Many Questions

**Chapter:** [16. The Agora, Democracy, and Paul on the Areopagus](../chapters/16-athens-agora-democracy/index.md) ·
**Setting:** The Athenian Agora, 480s–399 BCE · **Panels:** 14

**Hook.** The oracle says no one is wiser than Socrates. Socrates sets out to
prove it wrong by questioning everyone in the marketplace, and Athens
eventually votes to put him to death.

**Panel beats:**

- Panels 1–3: How the democracy worked: officials picked by lot with a stone
  allotment machine, the *kleroterion*, and speeches timed by a water clock.
- Panels 4–5: Ostracism: citizens scratch a name on a broken pot to exile a
  politician for ten years. Archaeologists found about 190 shards with
  Themistocles' name written by just a few hands. Someone was handing out
  ready-made ballots.
- Panels 6–8: Socrates' friend asks Delphi whether anyone is wiser. The answer
  is no. Socrates is baffled.
- Panels 9–11: He questions politicians, poets and craftsmen in the Agora and
  finds that they think they know more than they do.
- Panels 12–14: The trial of 399 BCE before 501 jurors, the verdict, and the
  cup of hemlock.

**On the trip.** The kleroterion, the water clock and the ostraka in the
Agora Museum, inside the rebuilt Stoa of Attalos.

### 20. The Pythia's Busy Day

**Chapter:** [17. The Oracle](../chapters/17-oracle/index.md) and
[18. Delphi](../chapters/18-delphi/index.md) · **Setting:** Delphi, about
550 BCE · **Panels:** 16

**Hook.** Ride along with a nervous farmer and a very rich king on
consultation day. One of them gets an answer that ruins him. A twist for
modern visitors: in November, Apollo was believed to be out of town.

**Panel beats:**

- Panels 1–2: Pilgrims climb the slopes of Mount Parnassus. Glaux notes that
  the oracle spoke only on set days, and traditionally not in winter, when
  Apollo was said to leave Delphi.
- Panels 3–5: The Pythia purifies herself at the Castalian Spring. Priests
  sprinkle cold water on a goat; if it does not shiver, there is no oracle
  today.
- Panels 6–8: The farmer pays his fee and asks whether to plant olives. The
  Pythia sits on her tripod in the inner chamber.
- Panels 9–12: King Croesus of Lydia asks whether to attack Persia. The
  answer: he will destroy a great empire. He attacks, and the empire he
  destroys is his own.
- Panels 13–14: The mystery of the vapors. Ancient writers describe a sweet
  smell; in 2001 geologists reported two faults crossing under the temple. A
  Myth or Evidence? stamp: the gas theory is still debated.
- Panels 15–16: Today: "Know thyself," the stone navel of the world, and a
  reader asking a chatbot a question.

**On the trip.** The Temple of Apollo, the Castalian Spring, and the omphalos
in the Delphi Museum.

**Explore further.** [A Day at the Oracle](../sims/delphi-consultation-steps/index.md)
and [Can This Answer Lose?](../sims/oracle-answer-hedging-test/index.md)

### 21. The Computer at the Bottom of the Sea

**Chapter:** [20. Athens, Museums, Tutors, and the City Today](../chapters/20-athens-museums-city-today/index.md) ·
**Setting:** Off Antikythera, the first century BCE and 1900–2005 ·
**Panels:** 14

**Hook.** Sponge divers sheltering from a storm find a sunken treasure ship.
Inside a lump of corroded bronze from the wreck hides the oldest known geared
calculator.

**Panel beats:**

- Panels 1–2: A heavily loaded cargo ship sinks off a tiny island.
- Panels 3–5: In 1900 Greek sponge divers find the wreck. Bronze and marble
  statues come up, and nobody pays much attention to a shoebox-sized lump.
- Panels 6–7: The lump splits. Someone sees gear teeth.
- Panels 8–10: Derek de Solla Price spends years with X-ray images and argues
  that it calculated the movements of the heavens.
- Panels 11–12: In 2005 CT scans reveal tiny inscriptions: it predicted
  eclipses and even tracked the cycle of the Olympic Games.
- Panels 13–14: Who built it? Rhodes? A follower of Archimedes? Glaux says,
  "Look closer."

**On the trip.** The fragments in the National Archaeological Museum in
Athens.

and the appendix [Who Created the Antikythera Mechanism](../appendices/antikythera-mechanism.md).

### 22. The Day the Imam Fainted

**Chapter:** [21. Going Home](../chapters/21-going-home/index.md) ·
**Setting:** Kitchens on both sides of the Aegean, 1600s to today ·
**Panels:** 12

**Hook.** A stuffed eggplant dish so good, or so extravagant with olive oil,
that an imam fainted. Greeks and Turks both cook it, both name it, and argue
over whose it is.

**Panel beats:**

- Panels 1–3: Two versions of the legend of *imam bayıldı*, "the imam
  fainted": he fainted at the taste, or he fainted when he learned how much of
  his new wife's olive oil it used up.
- Panels 4–6: More eggplant: *karnıyarık* ("split belly") and *hünkâr beğendi*
  ("the sultan liked it"), lamb served over smoky eggplant purée.
- Panels 7–9: In Greece the first dish appears as *imam baildi*. In the 1920s
  the chef Nikolaos Tselementes tops moussaka with a French béchamel.
- Panels 10–11: The 1923 population exchange carries cooks and recipes across
  the sea in both directions.
- Panel 12: One table, two languages, one eggplant. A closing on learning
  *with* people rather than *about* them.

**On the trip.** Every menu from Istanbul to Athens. Order the eggplant on
each side of the Aegean and compare.

**Explore further.** [Reading Food in Layers](../sims/food-history-layers-sorter/index.md)
and [Who Was Exchanged in 1923?](../sims/population-exchange-1923-cases/index.md)
