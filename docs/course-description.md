# Trip Description

## Title

Mediterranean Travels

## Summary

This is not a textbook. It is a personal travel guide for a thirteen-day journey
through Turkey and Greece, from **Wednesday, November 11 to Monday, November 23,
2026**. The trip has three parts: three days on our own in Istanbul, the Viking
Star's seven-night *Ancient Mediterranean Treasures* cruise from Istanbul to
Athens, and three days on our own in Athens, including a day trip to Delphi.

The guide is meant to be read **one chapter per day**, ideally at breakfast or the
night before. Each chapter prepares us for that day's place: what happened there,
who built it and why, what to look for when we are standing in front of it, what
to eat, and who lives there now. The goal is not to tick off sights. It is to
notice more, understand more, and meet the people of each place with real
empathy.

Two chapters break the one-place-per-day pattern on purpose. The first,
**Paul at Ephesus**, is a deep-dive chapter, read before our visit to Ephesus, about the
apostle Paul: who he was, what he taught, and how a single traveling teacher
with a small team built a movement that reshaped Western philosophy. It places
his two to three years in Ephesus inside the whole arc of his life and letters,
and it reads his work as a case study in **leadership and change**. Along the
way it draws parallels to a modern change problem: turning schools built
around legacy direct instruction into AI-native learning organizations, with
notes that link to our companion textbook,
[AI-Native Schools](https://dmccreary.github.io/ai-native-schools/).

The second, **The Oracle and the Algorithm**, is a reflective deep dive built
around our day at Delphi. For roughly eleven to twelve centuries, people
climbed the slopes of Mount Parnassus to ask Apollo's priestess what their
choices would bring. Kings, cities, and ordinary farmers all came, and the
answers helped decide wars, found colonies, and shape Greek philosophy. Today
we ask machine-learning models the same kind of question. The chapter looks at
the human need behind both: to see, before we act, what today's actions will
do to our future, and what we owe ourselves when we hand that seeing to someone
or something else.

A third thread runs through the whole guide: **Learning for All**. Almost every
stop on this route holds a story about who got to learn, who was shut out, and
which technologies or institutions widened the circle: an alphabet, a press, a
library, a tutor, an endowment. Each daily chapter ends with a short note that
connects that day's story to today's effort to use AI to bring a good education
to every child on Earth.

## Audience

The travelers on this trip: curious adults who love the history of
civilization, culture, and food, and who want to understand the people in each
place, not just see the monuments. We have no professional background in
archaeology, classics, theology, or Middle Eastern or Balkan history. We do
have a professional interest in how organizations change, especially schools
adapting to AI. One of us works as an evangelist for AI in the
democratization of education for all children on Earth. The two deep-dive
chapters and the Learning for All notes are written with that work in mind.

## Prerequisites

None. The guide assumes only general adult knowledge, such as having heard of
ancient Greece, the Roman Empire, Christianity, Islam, and the Ottoman Empire.
Every other term is explained when it first appears.

## Deep-Dive Chapter: Paul at Ephesus

### Why this chapter exists

Paul of Tarsus may be the most influential teacher in Western history who never
held an office, owned a building, or wrote a book. He wrote letters. Around
twenty years after Jesus died, Paul was turning a small Jewish renewal movement
into a network of mixed Jewish and Gentile (non-Jewish) communities spread
across the Roman East, and his letters became the oldest writings in the New
Testament. Ideas that later shaped Christian philosophy, from grace and faith
to conscience and love as the highest virtue, reach Augustine, Aquinas, Luther,
and the modern world largely through him.

Ephesus was the hub of that work. According to the Book of Acts, Paul taught
there for two to three years (about 52–55 CE), longer than anywhere else, and
"all the residents of Asia" heard the message from that base (Acts 19:10). He
wrote at least one letter from Ephesus (1 Corinthians), and possibly more. The
chapter uses Ephesus as the place where we can stand inside Paul's story, then
widens out to his whole career.

The second purpose is practical. Paul faced a classic change-leadership
problem: a respected legacy institution, deeply held rules about who belongs
and how learning happens, economic interests tied to the old way, internal
factions, and very slow communication. School leaders trying to move from
direct instruction (the teacher-at-the-front lecture model) to AI-native
learning face a smaller but recognizable version of the same problem. The
chapter treats Paul as a case study in leadership, and each lesson ends with a
short "For AI-native schools" note that links to the matching chapter of
[AI-Native Schools](https://dmccreary.github.io/ai-native-schools/).

### What the chapter should cover

1. **Paul's life in brief**: born in Tarsus, a Roman citizen and a Pharisee
   (a strict teacher of Jewish law) trained in Jerusalem; persecutor of the
   early Jesus movement; his turning point on the road to Damascus; and his
   travels across Anatolia, Macedonia, and Greece, ending in arrest and death
   in Rome.
2. **Paul in Ephesus**: three months in the synagogue, then two years teaching
   daily in the rented lecture hall of Tyrannus; his co-workers Priscilla and
   Aquila; the teacher Apollos; the riot of the silversmiths, led by Demetrius,
   who made shrines of the goddess Artemis and feared for their trade (Acts 19);
   his farewell to the Ephesian elders at Miletus (Acts 20).
3. **Paul's ideas**: grace and faith rather than law as the way in;
   "neither Jew nor Greek" (Galatians 3:28); the community as one body with many
   parts (1 Corinthians 12); love as the greatest virtue (1 Corinthians 13); and
   how he borrowed the language of Greek philosophy, especially the Stoics, to
   reach Greek listeners.
4. **Paul's lasting impact on Christian philosophy**: his influence on
   Augustine, the Reformation, and modern ideas of the individual conscience and
   human equality; and the harder parts of his legacy, including how his words
   on Jews, women, and slavery were later used.
5. **Which letters are his**: scholars broadly agree that seven letters are
   Paul's own (Romans, 1 and 2 Corinthians, Galatians, Philippians,
   1 Thessalonians, and Philemon). Others, including the Letter to the
   Ephesians, are debated. The Book of Acts was written a generation later and
   tells Paul's story from its own point of view. The chapter should say which
   source each claim comes from.
6. **Things to notice at Ephesus**: the Great Theater, where the silversmiths'
   crowd gathered; the street layout Paul would have walked; the single
   standing column of the Temple of Artemis near Selçuk; the Basilica of St.
   John; the Church of Mary, where a major church council met in 431; and the
   Artemis statues in the Ephesus Museum in Selçuk. It should be honest about
   which sites are traditional rather than historical, such as the tower called
   "St. Paul's Prison."
7. **A link to Athens**: Paul's speech on the Areopagus, the rock below the
   Acropolis, where he quoted Greek poets to a Greek audience (Acts 17). A
   bronze plaque with the speech sits at the foot of the rock, so this is
   something to look for on our Athens days.

### Where the parallel breaks down

The chapter should close by being honest about the limits of the comparison:

- Paul was founding a faith, not running a reform program. Treating his
  mission as a management technique flattens what it meant to him and to the
  people who believe it today, so the chapter should write with respect for
  Christian, Jewish, and Muslim readers alike.
- Much of Paul's success was visible only after his death, and the movement he
  started later built its own hierarchy and rules. Every new model risks
  becoming the next legacy system.
- Paul's sharp break with his own tradition also caused lasting harm,
  including centuries of Christian hostility toward Jews that drew on later
  readings of his words. Change leaders should ask who is hurt by the change,
  not only who benefits.

## Deep-Dive Chapter: The Oracle and the Algorithm

### Why this chapter exists

Every person who ever made a hard decision has wished to see how it would turn
out. Should we go to war? Should we marry, move, plant, sail, or invest? The
need to see the consequences of today's choices before we live with them is
one of the oldest parts of the human condition.

For a very long time, the most trusted answer to that need in the Greek world
was a woman sitting on a bronze tripod in the inner room of Apollo's temple at
Delphi. The Pythia, Apollo's priestess, gave answers from about the 8th century
BCE until Christian emperors shut the old sanctuaries in the late 4th century
CE, roughly 1,100 to 1,200 years depending on where we start the count. That is
longer than the Roman Empire in the West, and about five times as long as the
United States has existed. Greek cities asked Delphi before founding colonies
from Sicily to the Black Sea, before going to war, and before changing their
laws. The oracle's sayings shaped Socrates, and through him Western philosophy.

Today we ask a different kind of oracle. Machine-learning models predict the
weather we will pack for, the route our driver takes to Delphi, the loans and
jobs people are offered, and, in the large language models we talk to, simply
the next most likely word. This chapter is a slower, more reflective read than
the others. It asks what Delphi can teach us about living with machines that
predict, and about the questions we bring to them.

### What the chapter should cover

1. **The sanctuary and how a consultation worked**: the Sacred Way climbing past
   the treasuries that city-states built to show off their gifts; the Temple of
   Apollo; the Pythia and the priests who framed her words; the fees, the
   sacrifice, and the waiting line; and the *omphalos*, the stone that marked
   Delphi as the navel of the world.
2. **How long it lasted, and why**: Delphi stayed neutral ground shared by
   rival Greek states, guarded by a league of neighbors (the Amphictyony), and it
   adapted over the centuries, for example by answering in plain prose instead of
   verse. Plutarch, who served as a priest at Delphi around 100 CE, wrote essays
   on why the oracle had declined, an insider's view of an institution losing
   relevance.
3. **The famous answers**: King Croesus of Lydia told that if he attacked Persia
   he would destroy a great empire (it was his own); the Athenians told to trust
   their "wooden walls" before the Persian invasion of 480 BCE, which
   Themistocles argued meant their ships, leading to the victory at Salamis;
   and Socrates' friend Chaerephon told that no one was wiser than Socrates,
   which sent Socrates out questioning everyone to find out what it meant. Most
   of these stories come from Herodotus and Plato, written after the events, and
   the chapter should say so.
4. **The oracle's flaws**: Herodotus reports that the Pythia was bribed more
   than once for political ends, and that Delphi gave gloomy, hedging answers as
   Persia advanced. The chapter should explore the theory that gases rising
   from a geological fault under the temple affected the Pythia, and why that
   theory is still debated.
5. **The inscriptions**: "Know thyself" and "Nothing in excess," carved at the
   temple entrance, and what it means that the most famous fortune-teller in
   history pointed its visitors back at themselves.
6. **Things to notice on site**: the Castalian Spring, where visitors purified
   themselves; the Athenian Treasury; the polygonal wall covered with inscribed
   records of freed slaves; the theater and the stadium above the temple; the
   round Tholos at the sanctuary of Athena Pronaia; and in the museum, the
   bronze Charioteer and the omphalos stone.
7. **Delphi in winter**: in myth Apollo left Delphi during the winter months and
   the oracle fell silent while Dionysus ruled the sanctuary. We arrive in late
   November, in the oracle's own off-season, and the chapter should use that
   quiet as a theme.
8. **The people who live there now**: the modern village of Delphi, moved off
   the ancient site so French archaeologists could dig it in the 1890s; the
   olive groves of Amfissa below; the nearby mountain town of Arachova; and how
   these communities live with tourism in the off-season.

### The reflection

The chapter should end with a short, personal reflection rather than a
summary, built around a few questions to think about on the drive back to
Athens:

- What decision in our own lives would we most like to bring to an oracle, and
  what would we do if the answer were ambiguous?
- When have we trusted a prediction because we wanted it to be true?
- An institution built on foresight lasted about twelve centuries. What would
  it take for our own prediction machines to deserve that kind of trust, and
  what would make us stop trusting them?
- What actions we take today will matter most to people who live a hundred
  years from now, and how would we know?

### Where the parallel breaks down

- The oracle was a religious institution, and for the Greeks who consulted it
  Apollo was real. The chapter should treat that faith with respect, not as an
  early, failed version of data science.
- Machine-learning models find patterns in data and can be tested against
  what actually happens. The oracle could not be tested that way, and its
  ambiguity often protected it from ever being wrong.
- The oracle gave one answer to one visitor. Modern models make millions of
  predictions about people who never asked, which raises questions of consent
  and fairness that Delphi never faced.

## Learning for All: A Thread Through the Trip

### Why this thread exists

The word *evangelist* comes from the Greek *euangelion*, "good news." It took
its lasting meaning on this route, in the Greek letters of Paul and the
communities he wrote to. For a traveler whose own work is spreading the good
news that AI can bring a strong education to every child, this route is full of
earlier attempts to do the same thing, and of warnings about how those attempts
went wrong.

The pattern repeats from Knossos to Istanbul. Learning starts out locked inside
a palace, a temple, a guild, or a royal household. Then a new technology or
institution, such as a simpler alphabet, a printing press, a public library, or
a charitable endowment, opens it to many more people, usually against
resistance from those who benefited from the old way. Each daily chapter's
Learning for All note tells one of those stories, briefly, at the place where
it happened.

### How to write the notes

- Keep each note short: one story, one parallel, one link. The day belongs to
  the place, not to the argument.
- Tell the history accurately first, and make the parallel second. Where a
  number or claim is uncertain, such as literacy rates or where the Antikythera
  mechanism was made, say so.
- Include the costs alongside the gains: the scribes who lost their trade, the
  generation cut off from its old script, the people a reform left out.

### The caution the thread should end on

Evangelism has a shadow. Paul adapted his message to each audience and built
local leaders. The 1928 alphabet reform and the 1923 population exchange were
imposed from above, and both left real losses. The empathy this guide is built
around is the correction: democratizing education works best when it is done
*with* communities, in their languages and on their terms, not *to* them. The
closing chapter should make that point using the stories we actually saw.

## Topics

1. The long timeline of the Eastern Mediterranean, from the Minoans to modern
   Turkey and Greece
2. Minoan Crete: Knossos, palace culture, and Europe's first civilization
3. Troy, the Bronze Age, and how Homer's *Iliad* blends myth and history
4. Classical Athens: democracy, philosophy, theater, and the Acropolis
5. The Hellenistic and Roman worlds: Ephesus as a great Greco-Roman city
6. Early Christianity in Anatolia and Greece: Paul at Ephesus, the House of the
   Virgin Mary, and the Orthodox Church
7. Paul of Tarsus: his life, his journeys, his letters, and which letters
   scholars agree he wrote
8. Paul's ideas and their impact on Christian philosophy: grace, faith, love,
   community, and his debt to Greek philosophy
9. Paul as a leader of change: vision, team building, letters as a teaching
   technology, and handling resistance, with parallels to moving schools from
   direct instruction to AI-native learning
10. The Oracle of Delphi: Apollo's sanctuary, the Pythia, famous prophecies,
    and an institution of foresight that lasted more than a thousand years
11. Prediction then and now: the human need to foresee the results of our
    choices, from the oracle to machine learning and AI
12. Byzantium and Constantinople: Hagia Sophia, the cistern, and a Christian
    empire that lasted a thousand years
13. The Crusades and the Knights Hospitaller of Rhodes
14. Venice in the Eastern Mediterranean: the forts and harbors of Crete
15. The Ottoman Empire: Topkapi Palace, the mosques, the bazaars, and a
    multi-faith empire
16. The end of empires: Gallipoli, the birth of modern Turkey, Greek
    independence, and the 1923 population exchange between Greece and Turkey
17. Jewish communities of the region, including Rhodes' Jewish Quarter and the
    deportation of its community in 1944
18. Islam in daily life: prayer times, mosque etiquette, and the call to prayer
19. Turkish cuisine: breakfast, meze, kebab, simit, tea, coffee, and sweets
20. Greek and Cretan cuisine: olive oil, bread, cheese, fish, wine, and the
    Cretan diet
21. Daily life in Turkey and Greece today: families, work, the economy, faith,
    and attitudes toward visitors
22. Being a thoughtful traveler: empathy, etiquette, a few words of Turkish and
    Greek, and seeing past the tourist script
23. Reading a ruin: how to look at walls, columns, mosaics, and frescoes and
    understand what they tell us
24. Learning for all: alphabets, printing, libraries, tutors, and endowments
    that widened who could learn, and what they teach about bringing AI-powered
    education to every child

## Learning Outcomes

By the end of the trip, we will be able to:

- **Remember:** name the main civilizations of the region in order, and the key
  site that represents each one on our route.
- **Understand:** explain why each place mattered in its own time, and how
  empires, trade, and faith shaped its people.
- **Apply:** use the daily chapter to plan each day around opening hours, port
  times, prayer times, and weather, and to order and enjoy local food with
  confidence.
- **Analyze:** compare how Greek, Roman, Byzantine, Venetian, and Ottoman
  builders left their marks on the same places, and spot those layers on our
  own.
- **Evaluate:** weigh competing stories about contested history, such as Troy,
  Gallipoli, Hagia Sophia, and the population exchange, and understand why
  Turks and Greeks may remember the same events differently.
- **Create:** keep a short daily journal of what we noticed, ate, and learned
  from the people we met, and turn it into our own story of the journey.

From the Paul at Ephesus chapter in particular, we will be able to:

- **Remember:** name Paul's main journeys, the cities he wrote to, and the
  seven letters scholars agree he wrote.
- **Understand:** explain Paul's central ideas, such as grace, faith, love, and
  one community made of many kinds of people, and how they shaped later
  Christian philosophy.
- **Apply:** use the leadership lessons from Paul's time in Ephesus to plan a
  next step in moving a school from direct instruction toward AI-native
  learning.
- **Analyze:** match a moment in Paul's story to the change model it
  illustrates, such as building a guiding coalition, diffusion from a hub, or
  resistance rooted in economic fear.
- **Evaluate:** judge where the parallel between Paul's movement and school
  change holds and where it breaks down, and weigh the harm as well as the good
  in his legacy.
- **Create:** write a short leadership reflection that connects one Paul story
  to a change we want to lead, linked to the matching chapter of
  *AI-Native Schools*.

From The Oracle and the Algorithm chapter, we will be able to:

- **Remember:** describe how a consultation at Delphi worked, and name three
  famous answers the oracle gave.
- **Understand:** explain why the oracle stayed trusted for over a thousand
  years, and why that trust finally faded.
- **Apply:** use questions drawn from Delphi, such as who gathered the data,
  who pays, and what the horizon is, to judge an AI prediction we meet in
  daily life.
- **Analyze:** compare how the oracle and a machine-learning model each turn
  information into a prediction, and where human interpretation enters.
- **Evaluate:** judge when it is wise to rely on a prediction and when it is
  better to follow Socrates and treat it as the start of an inquiry.
- **Create:** write a short personal reflection on a decision we face today and
  what we would want to know about its effect on our future.

From the Learning for All thread, we will be able to:

- **Understand:** explain how one technology or institution at each of several
  stops, such as the Greek alphabet, the Library of Celsus, or the 1928 Turkish
  alphabet reform, widened who could learn.
- **Analyze:** identify, for each of those stories, who gained, who lost, and
  who resisted the change.
- **Evaluate:** judge which historical lessons best apply to bringing AI-powered
  education to every child, and where the comparison fails.
- **Create:** write a short "good news" message about AI and education that
  draws on one story from the trip and respects the community it is meant for.
